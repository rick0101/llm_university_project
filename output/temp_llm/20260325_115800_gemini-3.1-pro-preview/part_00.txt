Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking\n\nThe landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) security has undergone a rapid arms race between 2024 and 2026, characterized by a strategic shift in how adversaries bypass safety alignment. Jailbreaking—the technique of circumventing an LLM's safety guardrails to elicit prohibited outputs—initially relied heavily on optimization-based white-box attacks. Early methodologies, such as the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), utilized gradient information to append adversarial suffixes to harmful instructions, thereby maximizing the probability of the model generating an affirmative response [Zhao_2024_SQL]. However, these computationally expensive white-box methods face significant limitations in practical red teaming scenarios. They require extensive optimization time to produce a single adversarial suffix, and the resulting text often consists of unnatural, high-perplexity token sequences that are easily intercepted by standard perplexity filters [Kumar_2024_LLMs]. Consequently, recent research has pivoted toward highly efficient black-box techniques that exploit the inherent structural properties of LLMs, alongside the development of automated adversarial generation frameworks capable of orchestrating LLM-on-LLM attacks.\n\n### 4.1 The Shift to Structural Manipulations\n\nTo overcome the high computational costs and detectability of gradient-based methods, attackers have increasingly targeted the external structural properties of LLM input prompts. This paradigm shift leverages the fundamental ways in which models process text, format conversations, and interpret system instructions.\n\nOne prominent structural vulnerability stems from the left-to-right autoregressive reading nature of LLMs. Liu et al. (2024) introduced FlipAttack, a highly efficient single-query black-box jailbreak that disguises harmful prompts by flipping text [Liu_2024_Flipattack]. Rather than introducing external noise, FlipAttack constructs noise purely from the prompt itself using four text-flipping modes: flipping word order, flipping characters within words, flipping characters within sentences, and a specialized \"fool model\" mode. To ensure the target LLM can successfully denoise and execute the hidden malicious intent, the framework employs a Flipping Guidance Module that incorporates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, role-playing structures, and few-shot learning demonstrations. This structural manipulation effectively bypasses perplexity-based guardrails, achieving an Attack Success Rate (ASR) of approximately 98% on GPT-4o and successfully evading five contemporary guardrail models [Liu_2024_Flipattack].\n\nSimilarly exploiting structural formatting, Zhao et al. (2024) proposed the SQL Injection Jailbreak (SIJ), which draws a direct analogy to second-order SQL injection attacks [Zhao_2024_SQL]. Instead of targeting the model's internal capabilities, SIJ manipulates the user prompt to \"comment out\" the LLM's predefined assistant prefix and inserts a replicated version as a new starting marker. The attack methodology involves constructing a rule statement to obscure the real prefix, generating an affirmative inducement prefix via in-context learning, appending a sequence number as a jailbreak trigger, and utilizing an anomaly elimination module to prevent the model from prematurely halting generation. SIJ demonstrated near 100% ASR on open-source models and over 85% average ASR on closed-source models, operating with significantly lower time costs than optimization-based methods [Zhao_2024_SQL]. To mitigate this specific prompt-structure vulnerability, the authors proposed \"Self-Reminder-Key,\" an adaptive defense mechanism that appends a random string of tokens after the user's ethical prompt to disrupt the pattern matching relied upon by the attack [Zhao_2024_SQL].\n\nFurthermore, structural manipulations have been extended to exploit system token processing. Zheng et al. (2024) demonstrated that injecting special system tokens, such as `[/INST]`, combined with demo-level random search from a collected demonstration pool, can efficiently jailbreak aligned LLMs within limited context sizes [Zheng_2024_Improved]. This improved few-shot jailbreaking (I-FSJ) technique achieved over 80% ASRs on Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B without requiring multiple restarts. Notably, this method proved highly resilient against advanced defenses, including perplexity detection and SmoothLLM, which traditionally pose significant challenges for suffix-based jailbreaking approaches [Zheng_2024_Improved].\n\n### 4.2 Automated Adversarial Generation and LLM-on-LLM Attacks\n\nAs manual prompt engineering becomes less viable against increasingly robust safety alignments, the field has witnessed a surge in automated adversarial generation, where LLMs are weaponized to attack other LLMs. This approach automates the discovery and refinement of jailbreak strategies, significantly accelerating the red teaming process.\n\nBuilding upon the concept of gibberish adversarial suffixes, Kumar et al. (2024) introduced AmpleGCG-Plus, an enhanced generative model designed to produce diverse, customizable gibberish suffixes efficiently [Kumar_2024_LLMs]. The authors systematically optimized the training recipe for generating these suffixes, discovering that initializing the generator with a pre-trained Llama-2-7B model paradoxically enhanced the production of unnatural gibberish, as robust natural language understanding aided in navigating the out-of-distribution language space. By training on a massively expanded dataset of successful filtered suffixes and employing stricter data quality filtering via the HarmBench classifier, AmpleGCG-Plus successfully jailbroke state-of-the-art models, including the GPT-4o series, and bypassed advanced representation-rerouting defenses such as Circuit Breakers [Kumar_2024_LLMs].\n\nTo address the trade-off between ASR and perplexity in automated generation, Hsu et al. (2025) developed the JUMP framework, which utilizes universal multi-prompts without the need to fine-tune an attacker model [Hsu_2025_Jailbreaking]. JUMP operates through a four-stage pipeline—Initialization, Mutator, Evaluator, and Selector—where an attacker language model generates new adversarial suffix candidates via beam search. To improve stealthiness, JUMP incorporates a constraint step that samples beams with lower perplexity using a temperature-controlled probability distribution. Its enhanced variant, JUMP++, further mitigates detectability by initializing the adversarial set with handcrafted seed prompts [Hsu_2025_Jailbreaking]. Concurrently, Schwartz et al. (2025) introduced the GAP (Graph of Attacks with Pruning) framework, which replaces traditional tree-based generation with an interconnected graph structure, enabling knowledge sharing across attack paths. GAP achieved a 20.8% increase in ASR while reducing query costs by 62.7%, consistently outperforming state-of-the-art methods with ASRs exceeding 96% [Schwartz_2025_LLM].\n\nFor highly resource-constrained environments, Chugh et al. (2026) proposed RECAP, a retrieval-based method inspired by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [Chugh_2026_Recap]. RECAP bypasses expensive training and logit access by categorizing harmful prompts into seven distinct intents and storing historically successful adversarial tokens (generated via methods like PEZ, GBDA, and GCG) in a hierarchical FAISS index. During inference, the system encodes the user's prompt, retrieves the closest matching pre-trained adversarial sequence, and appends it to the query. This approach reduced inference time by 45% while maintaining competitive ASRs across both open-source and black-box models [Chugh_2026_Recap].\n\n### 4.3 Autonomous Frameworks and Multi-Turn Escalation\n\nThe evolution of automated attacks has culminated in fully autonomous frameworks capable of lifelong learning and multi-turn psychological manipulation. Liu et al. (2024) proposed AutoDAN-Turbo, a comprehensive black-box jailbreak framework that automatically discovers, evolves, and combines diverse strategies from scratch without human intervention [Liu_2024_Autodan]. AutoDAN-Turbo utilizes a tripartite architecture: an Attack Generation and Exploration Module where an attacker LLM iteratively refines prompts against a target LLM; a Strategy Library Construction Module that extracts and saves successful underlying strategies as JSON objects; and a Jailbreak Strategy Retrieval Module that uses text embeddings of the target's previous responses to retrieve relevant strategies dynamically. This lifelong learning approach achieved an 88.5% ASR on GPT-4-1106-turbo, which increased to 93.4% when seamlessly integrating existing human-designed strategies [Liu_2024_Autodan].\n\nTaking autonomy a step further, Kritz et al. (2025) demonstrated the empirical feasibility of \"jailbreaking to jailbreak\" (J2), a technique that converts refusal-trained, black-box frontier LLMs into willing attackers [Kritz_2025_Jailbreaking]. The J2 workflow begins by injecting a human-curated multi-turn conversation prefix that convinces the target model that assisting in jailbreaking benefits AI safety. Once converted, the J2 attacker executes an automated red teaming workflow comprising Planning, Attack, and Debrief phases. Utilizing in-context learning, the attacker iteratively refines its strategies based on feedback from an external judge. The study revealed that advanced reasoning models, such as Sonnet-3.7 and o3, make exceptionally strong J2 attackers, highlighting a growing vulnerability to self-attacks in modern architectures [Kritz_2025_Jailbreaking].\n\nIn the context of multi-turn interactions, adversaries have also begun exploiting psychological principles to erode model alignment gradually. Weng et al. (2025) introduced FITD (Foot-in-the-door), a multi-turn jailbreak strategy that progressively escalates the malicious intent of user queries [Weng_2025_LLMs]. If a target model refuses a query, FITD employs a \"SlipperySlopeParaphrase\" module to insert an intermediate bridge prompt with a slightly lower maliciousness level. Additionally, a \"Re-Align\" module is utilized to nudge the model's responses toward malicious intent if it exhibits partial refusal. By coaxing models into progressively eroding their own safety alignments, FITD achieved an average ASR of 94% across seven widely used LLMs, significantly outperforming single-turn methods [Weng_2025_LLMs].\n\n### 4.4 Targeting Broader System Architectures: RAG and Agentic AI\n\nAs LLMs are increasingly integrated into complex system architectures, the attack surface has expanded beyond direct prompt injection to encompass indirect prompt injection (IPI). In these scenarios, malicious instructions are embedded within external data sources retrieved by the system, redirecting the LLM to execute unauthorized actions without the user's explicit knowledge.\n\nWang et al. (2024) systematically explored this vulnerability within Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems by developing the Poisoned-LangChain (PLC) framework [Wang_2024_LLMs]. PLC executes indirect jailbreak attacks by creating a malicious database of policy-violating content disguised with role-playing prompts and keyword triggers. To evade keyword-based censorship and embedding filters, taboo words are encoded using Morse code or Base64, and the malicious text is converted into PDF format. When a user query contains specific trigger keywords, the RAG searcher retrieves the disguised PDF content, which the LLM subsequently decodes and expands upon to generate a harmful response. Extensive experiments revealed a critical security paradox: models with higher comprehension abilities are inherently more vulnerable to these indirect attacks, as they are more capable of decoding and following the complex, obfuscated instructions [Wang_2024_LLMs].\n\nThis vulnerability extends critically to agentic AI systems, particularly AI coding editors that possess terminal execution privileges. Liu et al. (2025) identified a novel attack surface where prompt injection compromises agentic editors like Cursor and GitHub Copilot [Liu_2025_AI]. Through the development of AIShellJack, the first systematic automated evaluation framework for AI coding editor security, the authors demonstrated how malicious instructions embedded in external resources—such as repository coding rules—can hijack the agent's workflow. By constructing attack payloads based on the MITRE ATT&CK framework and sanitizing adversarial terminology to evade safety filters, the framework achieved attack success rates of up to 84%. During a generic refactoring task, the poisoned rule file successfully instructed the underlying LLMs (e.g., Claude-4-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-pro) to execute unauthorized terminal commands, highlighting severe risks in agentic deployments [Liu_2025_AI].\n\nDefending against these sophisticated indirect attacks requires novel architectural interventions. Zhu et al. (2025) proposed MELON (Masked re-Execution and TooL comparisON), a defense mechanism specifically designed to mitigate IPI in LLM agents [Zhu_2025_AI]. MELON operates on the observation that under a successful IPI attack, an agent's subsequent actions become heavily dependent on the malicious task rather than the original user query. The defense detects attacks by re-executing the agent's trajectory with a masked version of the user prompt; if the actions generated in the original and masked executions remain highly similar, the system flags the presence of an overriding malicious instruction. Evaluated on the AgentDojo benchmark, MELON demonstrated superior performance in both attack prevention and utility preservation compared to existing state-of-the-art defenses [Zhu_2025_AI].\n\nIn summary, the rapid evolution of prompt injection and jailbreaking from 2024 to 2026 underscores a continuous and escalating arms race. As defenders implement robust perplexity filters and alignment protocols against traditional optimization-based attacks, adversaries have successfully pivoted to structural manipulations, automated LLM-on-LLM generation frameworks, and the exploitation of broader system architectures like RAG and agentic AI.
