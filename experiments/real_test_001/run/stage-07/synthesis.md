## LLM Hallucinations

### Synthesis

Temporal Trends:
The research landscape surrounding Large Language Model (LLM) hallucinations and factual consistency has undergone a rapid and sophisticated evolution between 2023 and 2026. This temporal span reflects a fundamental shift from basic, computationally expensive detection mechanisms toward highly optimized, mechanistically informed, and conceptually nuanced frameworks. In the initial phase of this timeline, circa 2023, the field was primarily characterized by the urgent need to establish baseline detection methodologies in the wake of widespread LLM deployment. Research during this period heavily relied on leveraging the LLMs themselves as evaluators. A landmark development in this era was the introduction of zero-shot inference-time methods that sought to balance efficacy with computational cost. For instance, the development of ChainPoll [Friel_2023_LLM] marked a critical turning point by demonstrating that LLM-based polling and Chain-of-Thought prompting could be optimized to use only a quarter of the inference cost of contemporary competitors while simultaneously outperforming them. This established a foundational paradigm: LLMs could be used to police themselves, provided the prompting strategies were sufficiently rigorous. 

Moving into 2024, the field experienced a conceptual maturation. Researchers began to recognize that treating all non-factual generations as binary 'errors' was overly simplistic. This led to a theoretical pivot, exemplified by the work on narrative confabulations [Sui_2024_Confabulation]. By modeling hallucinations not as strict database errors but as continuous narrative 'confabulations,' the research community began to understand the generative mechanisms behind these fabrications. The discovery of a statistically significant positive correlation between narrativity and semantic coherence [Sui_2024_Confabulation] suggested that LLMs often hallucinate because they are optimizing for human-like storytelling and structural coherence rather than strict factual grounding. Concurrently, 2024 saw the rise of specialized, supervised training for annotator models, moving away from a reliance on general-purpose models like GPT-4. The development of specialized datasets and models, such as the ANAH framework [Gu_2024_Anah], demonstrated that training auxiliary modules specifically for hallucination detection could yield superior zero-shot detection and out-of-domain generalization.

By 2025, the temporal trajectory shifted dramatically toward 'glass-box' or 'white-box' methodologies, driven by the proliferation of powerful open-weight models like the Llama, Qwen, and Mistral series. Researchers moved beyond analyzing the final text outputs and began probing the internal states of the models. This era is defined by the utilization of attention maps and token probabilities to identify latent uncertainty and segment queries [Liu_2025_Attention, Shelmanov_2025_LLM]. Techniques such as AGSER emerged, significantly reducing computational overhead by requiring only 3 LLM passes instead of 5 through intelligent attention analysis [Liu_2025_Attention]. Furthermore, 2025 exposed deeper, more systemic vulnerabilities in LLM behavior. The identification of the 'factuality gap' [Kabongo_2025_Factual] revealed a critical flaw in previous evaluation metrics: high semantic similarity was masking factual precision rates as low as 4.38%. Additionally, the exploration of cross-persona consistency [Banyas_2025_LLMs] highlighted that hallucinations were not static; models actively altered factual claims based on assigned user personas, particularly on controversial topics, indicating a sycophantic dimension to hallucinations. Finally, looking toward 2026, the field is advancing into highly scalable process-level supervision. The introduction of methods like MCNIG [Royer_2026_Process], which reduces Process Reward Model labeling complexity to O(N), indicates a future where step-by-step factual verification can be integrated directly into the reinforcement learning and alignment pipelines without prohibitive computational bottlenecks. Overall, the trajectory from 2023 to 2026 illustrates a progression from reactive, black-box detection to proactive, mechanistic, and conceptually profound mitigation strategies.

Taxonomy:
The research synthesized in this cluster can be systematically categorized into a multi-dimensional taxonomy encompassing four primary sub-areas: (1) Black-Box Detection and LLM-as-a-Judge Paradigms, (2) White-Box Internal State and Mechanistic Analysis, (3) Conceptual and Behavioral Modeling of Fabrications, and (4) Scalable Process and Reward Optimization.

1. Black-Box Detection and LLM-as-a-Judge Paradigms: This sub-area represents the most widely adopted approach for scaling hallucination detection without requiring access to model weights. It focuses on utilizing advanced prompting techniques and self-consistency checks to generate ground-truth data or evaluative judgments. Within this category, research splits into two distinct methodologies. The first relies on zero-shot, inference-time prompting of general-purpose models, utilizing techniques like Chain-of-Thought and polling to assess factuality [Friel_2023_LLM, Kabongo_2025_Factual]. The second methodology involves the supervised training of specialized auxiliary modules or annotators. Instead of relying on off-the-shelf models, researchers curate specific datasets to train models dedicated solely to hallucination detection, thereby achieving superior out-of-domain generalization and zero-shot performance compared to generalist models like GPT-4 [Gu_2024_Anah].

2. White-Box Internal State and Mechanistic Analysis: As open-weight models have become more prevalent, this sub-area has emerged to address the limitations of black-box evaluation. Research here focuses on extracting signals of uncertainty and fabrication directly from the model's architecture during generation. Key techniques include the analysis of attention maps to understand how the model attends to context versus its own generated tokens, allowing for the segmentation of queries and the identification of ungrounded claims [Liu_2025_Attention]. Additionally, this category encompasses Uncertainty Quantification (UQ), where token-level probability distributions and internal hidden states are analyzed via specialized UQ heads to detect when a model is guessing rather than retrieving known facts [Shelmanov_2025_LLM]. These methods are highly prized for their ability to detect hallucinations at inference time with minimal external computational overhead.

3. Conceptual and Behavioral Modeling of Fabrications: This sub-area diverges from purely technical detection to explore the theoretical nature and behavioral triggers of hallucinations. It challenges the binary notion of 'true' versus 'false' by introducing nuanced frameworks. One major branch models hallucinations as continuous narrative 'confabulations,' investigating the psychological and structural reasons behind fabrications, such as the model's inherent drive to maintain semantic coherence and narrativity even at the expense of factual accuracy [Sui_2024_Confabulation]. Another critical branch examines contextual and persona-driven vulnerabilities. This research evaluates how the assignment of specific user personas or the introduction of controversial topics causes models to dynamically alter factual claims, thereby framing hallucinations as a function of alignment and sycophancy rather than mere knowledge deficits [Banyas_2025_LLMs].

4. Scalable Process and Reward Optimization: The final sub-area focuses on integrating hallucination mitigation directly into the training and alignment pipeline through advanced reward modeling. Unlike outcome-based evaluation, which assesses the final generated text, this area emphasizes step-level verification. Research here is dedicated to overcoming the traditional computational bottlenecks associated with Process Reward Models (PRMs). By developing novel algorithms that reduce the labeling complexity of step-by-step verification (e.g., to linear O(N) time), this sub-area aims to make continuous, granular factual consistency checks viable during the reinforcement learning phase of model development [Royer_2026_Process].

Gaps Filled:
The collection of papers in this cluster addresses several critical, long-standing gaps in the literature regarding LLM hallucinations, primarily focusing on computational efficiency, evaluation accuracy, and the conceptual understanding of why models fabricate information. 

One of the most significant gaps filled by this research is the prohibitive computational cost and lack of scalability associated with traditional hallucination detection. Historically, utilizing LLMs as evaluators required multiple, expensive inference passes, making real-time or large-scale detection unfeasible. This gap was directly addressed by [Friel_2023_LLM], whose ChainPoll methodology drastically reduced the inference cost to a quarter of its competitors while maintaining or exceeding detection performance. Similarly, [Liu_2025_Attention] filled the efficiency gap in white-box evaluation; their AGSER framework reduced the computational overhead of attention-based detection by requiring only 3 LLM passes instead of the standard 5. Furthermore, in the realm of model alignment, the exponential cost of step-level verification was a major roadblock. [Royer_2026_Process] addressed this by introducing MCNIG, which successfully reduced Process Reward Model labeling complexity to O(N), thereby filling a crucial gap in scalable process supervision.

Another major gap addressed is the over-reliance on, and the inherent limitations of, general-purpose proprietary models (like GPT-4) for evaluating hallucinations. The literature previously lacked robust, specialized open-source alternatives that could generalize across domains. [Gu_2024_Anah] filled this void by developing the ANAH-v2 framework, demonstrating that supervised training of specialized annotators could surpass general-purpose LLMs in zero-shot detection. Similarly, [Shelmanov_2025_LLM] proved that specialized Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) heads attached to open-weight models could outperform classical unsupervised methods, providing a more reliable, specialized mechanism for detecting fabrications.

Finally, this cluster fills profound conceptual gaps regarding the nature and measurement of factual errors. Previously, automated metrics heavily relied on semantic similarity to judge factuality, operating under the assumption that similar phrasing implied factual consistency. [Kabongo_2025_Factual] dismantled this assumption by exposing the 'factuality gap,' proving that high semantic similarity often masks abysmal factual precision (as low as 4.38%). This finding fills a critical gap in evaluation methodology, warning against the use of flawed heuristics. Additionally, the literature often treated hallucinations as static knowledge deficits. [Banyas_2025_LLMs] filled the gap in understanding dynamic, context-driven hallucinations by demonstrating that models alter facts based on user personas. Lastly, [Sui_2024_Confabulation] filled a theoretical gap by explaining the 'why' behind hallucinations, proving that models confabulate because they are statistically driven to prioritize narrative coherence and human-like storytelling over strict factual accuracy.

Open Gaps:
Despite the significant advancements in detection, evaluation, and conceptual modeling, several critical gaps and open problems remain unresolved within the domain of LLM hallucinations. A primary limitation shared across this cluster is the overwhelming focus on *detection* and *evaluation* rather than intrinsic *prevention* or *mitigation* during the initial generation phase. While methods like ChainPoll [Friel_2023_LLM] and AGSER [Liu_2025_Attention] are highly efficient at identifying hallucinations after or during the fact, the literature still lacks robust, universally applicable architectural interventions that prevent the model from generating ungrounded information in the first place without relying on external retrieval systems (RAG) or heavy post-hoc filtering.

Another significant open gap is the challenge of cross-domain and out-of-distribution generalization. Although specialized models like ANAH-v2 [Gu_2024_Anah] and UQ heads [Shelmanov_2025_LLM] show improvements in out-of-domain generalization compared to baselines, the fundamental problem remains: hallucination detectors trained on specific distributions of text (e.g., Wikipedia, news) often fail to accurately assess highly specialized, technical, or low-resource domains (e.g., advanced medical literature, obscure legal texts). The field has yet to develop a truly domain-agnostic hallucination detection framework.

Furthermore, the revelation of the 'factuality gap' by [Kabongo_2025_Factual] opens a massive methodological gap in how the community evaluates models. If traditional semantic similarity metrics (like BERTScore or standard cosine similarity) are fundamentally flawed because they cannot distinguish between a factually correct statement and a semantically similar but factually negated statement, the field urgently needs new, computationally lightweight automated metrics that strictly measure factual precision. While [Banyas_2025_LLMs] utilizes cosine similarity for cross-persona consistency, the findings of [Kabongo_2025_Factual] suggest that such metrics might be inherently noisy, leaving a gap for the development of purely fact-grounded embedding spaces.

The issue of persona-induced hallucinations also presents an unresolved challenge. [Banyas_2025_LLMs] demonstrates that models alter factual claims based on user personas, revealing a deep-seated sycophancy in RLHF-aligned models. The open problem is how to decouple factual retrieval from persona adoption. How can researchers design alignment protocols that allow a model to adopt a persona (e.g., 'explain like I am a five-year-old' or 'act as a conservative commentator') without compromising the underlying objective truth of the generated statements? 

Finally, there is a persistent gap between white-box and black-box applicability. The highly effective mechanistic insights derived from attention maps [Liu_2025_Attention] and token probabilities [Shelmanov_2025_LLM] are strictly limited to open-weight architectures (like Llama, Mistral, Qwen). As state-of-the-art proprietary models (like GPT-4 or Claude 3) remain closed, the research community cannot apply these advanced internal state analyses to the most widely used commercial systems, creating a bifurcated landscape where the best diagnostic tools cannot be used on the most powerful models.

Methodological Convergences:
An analysis of the methodologies across this cluster reveals striking convergences in evaluation paradigms, alongside notable divergences in how researchers approach the implementation of mitigation and detection strategies. The most dominant methodological convergence across the entire cluster is the ubiquitous adoption of the 'LLM-as-a-Judge' or 'LLM-as-an-Annotator' paradigm. Regardless of the specific research goal, almost all papers rely on leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs to scale the evaluation process. Techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and self-consistency checks are standard practice, utilized extensively to generate judgments, create ground-truth datasets, or poll for factual consistency [Friel_2023_LLM, Kabongo_2025_Factual, Gu_2024_Anah]. This convergence highlights a consensus in the field that human annotation is too slow and expensive for the scale of modern LLM outputs, and that advanced prompting strategies can elicit reliable evaluative signals from the models themselves.

Another significant convergence is the heavy reliance on open-weight models for both testing and internal analysis. The Llama, Qwen, and Mistral series serve as the foundational bedrock for this research [Liu_2025_Attention, Kabongo_2025_Factual, Gu_2024_Anah, Banyas_2025_LLMs, Shelmanov_2025_LLM]. This shared reliance underscores a methodological shift toward transparency, allowing researchers to move beyond API-based black-box testing to probe internal mechanisms.

However, the cluster exhibits strong divergences in how hallucination detection and mitigation should be implemented. One faction of researchers advocates for zero-shot, inference-time methods that require no additional training. Approaches like ChainPoll [Friel_2023_LLM] and attention-based segmentation [Liu_2025_Attention] argue for the efficiency of extracting signals directly during or immediately after generation using existing model weights. In stark contrast, another faction argues that zero-shot methods are insufficient and advocates for the supervised training of auxiliary modules. Papers like [Gu_2024_Anah] and [Shelmanov_2025_LLM] dedicate significant resources to training specialized annotators or Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) heads, arguing that dedicated parameters are necessary to surpass the performance of generalist models.

Furthermore, there is a methodological divergence in the conceptual definition of what is being measured. While the majority of the cluster treats hallucinations as discrete, measurable errors in factual consistency or grounding that can be classified via F1 scores or AUROC [Gu_2024_Anah, Kabongo_2025_Factual, Shelmanov_2025_LLM], [Sui_2024_Confabulation] diverges by employing a continuous, narrative-based methodology. Instead of binary error checking, they measure the correlation between narrativity and semantic coherence, treating the phenomenon as a spectrum of 'confabulation' rather than a strict factual failure. Finally, [Royer_2026_Process] diverges from the outcome-based evaluation prevalent in the rest of the cluster by focusing entirely on step-level Process Reward Modeling, suggesting that methodology must shift from evaluating the final output to evaluating the generative trajectory itself.

Testing Overview:
The testing methodologies employed across this cluster of papers are rigorous, diverse, and rely on a combination of established industry standards and novel, custom-built evaluation suites designed to probe specific vulnerabilities. 

In terms of benchmarks and datasets, HaluEval emerges as the undisputed gold standard and the most commonly shared benchmark across the cluster. It is utilized extensively to provide a standardized baseline for comparing detection methods, appearing prominently in the testing regimens of [Sui_2024_Confabulation], [Gu_2024_Anah], and [Friel_2023_LLM]. However, recognizing the limitations of static, generalized benchmarks, researchers frequently developed and deployed custom evaluation suites to test specific hypotheses. For instance, [Friel_2023_LLM] introduced RealHall to test their ChainPoll methodology in more realistic, complex scenarios. [Gu_2024_Anah] developed the ANAH dataset specifically to train and evaluate their specialized annotator models on nuanced hallucination detection. To test the dynamic nature of fabrications, [Banyas_2025_LLMs] utilized the ConsistencyAI suite to measure how facts shift across different assigned personas.

The dominant evaluation metrics across the cluster reflect a focus on classification accuracy and the reliability of detection thresholds. AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) and AUC are the most prevalent metrics for assessing the performance of detection algorithms, as they provide a robust measure of a model's ability to distinguish between hallucinated and factual statements across various confidence thresholds [Shelmanov_2025_LLM, Liu_2025_Attention, Sui_2024_Confabulation, Friel_2023_LLM]. For tasks framed as strict classification, F1 score and Accuracy are the standard metrics [Gu_2024_Anah, Kabongo_2025_Factual]. In studies examining the drift of factual claims, such as cross-persona consistency, cosine similarity of sentence embeddings is utilized [Banyas_2025_LLMs].

The models tested span a comprehensive array of state-of-the-art architectures, with a heavy and deliberate bias toward open-weight models that allow for mechanistic interpretability. The Meta Llama series (including various parameter sizes and iterations) is the most frequently tested architecture [Liu_2025_Attention, Kabongo_2025_Factual, Gu_2024_Anah, Banyas_2025_LLMs]. The Qwen series [Liu_2025_Attention, Gu_2024_Anah, Banyas_2025_LLMs] and Mistral models [Shelmanov_2025_LLM, Gu_2024_Anah, Banyas_2025_LLMs] are also ubiquitous, serving as the primary testbeds for internal state analysis, attention mapping, and UQ head training. OpenAI's proprietary GPT models (primarily GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) are frequently included, but usually serve as baselines for comparison, as 'judges' to generate ground truth, or as targets for black-box polling methods [Friel_2023_LLM, Gu_2024_Anah, Banyas_2025_LLMs].

While the testing approaches are generally rigorous, particularly in their use of AUROC to measure threshold-independent performance, there are notable lacking areas. The reliance on cosine similarity by some papers is directly challenged by the findings of [Kabongo_2025_Factual], which exposes the 'factuality gap' where highsemantic similarity masks factual precision as low as 4.38%. This critical finding suggests that testing methodologies relying solely on embedding-based similarity metrics may be fundamentally flawed for rigorous hallucination detection, as they fail to capture granular factual deviations. Furthermore, while standard benchmarks like HaluEval are widely used across the cluster [Sui_2024_Confabulation, Gu_2024_Anah, Friel_2023_LLM], the rapid development and deployment of custom evaluation suites—such as RealHall [Friel_2023_LLM], ConsistencyAI [Banyas_2025_LLMs], and ANAH [Gu_2024_Anah]—indicate that existing static benchmarks are increasingly viewed as insufficient. They struggle to capture the nuanced, dynamic, and persona-driven nature of modern LLM hallucinations. The field urgently requires a more unified, standardized testing framework that combines internal state analysis with rigorous, multi-faceted factual verification, moving beyond simple semantic overlap to true epistemological grounding.

Synthesis:
The study of Large Language Model (LLM) hallucinations has rapidly evolved from a peripheral troubleshooting concern into a central, highly sophisticated pillar of AI security and reliability research. As synthesized from the 2023-2026 literature, this domain is characterized by a fundamental shift in both conceptual understanding and methodological execution. Initially, the field approached hallucinations as discrete, binary errors, relying heavily on computationally expensive, black-box polling methods to gauge consistency [Friel_2023_LLM]. However, recent scholarship has fundamentally reframed this phenomenon. Hallucinations are increasingly understood not merely as simple factual deviations or database retrieval errors, but as continuous narrative 'confabulations' that mimic human storytelling. Research has demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between narrativity and semantic coherence, suggesting that the very mechanisms that make LLMs fluent and engaging also drive their tendency to fabricate [Sui_2024_Confabulation].

This conceptual shift is mirrored by a methodological transition toward highly efficient, white-box detection strategies. Researchers are moving away from brute-force generation and increasingly leveraging the internal states of open-weight models—such as attention maps, token probabilities, and hidden layer activations—to identify uncertainty and segment queries without the massive computational overhead of multiple generation passes [Shelmanov_2025_LLM, Liu_2025_Attention]. For instance, methods like AGSER have proven that analyzing internal attention mechanisms can reduce computational overhead significantly while maintaining high detection accuracy [Liu_2025_Attention]. Concurrently, the literature reveals a strong methodological convergence around the use of LLMs as scalable evaluators and annotators. By utilizing techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting and self-consistency, researchers are generating high-quality ground-truth data to train specialized auxiliary modules and annotators [Gu_2024_Anah, Kabongo_2025_Factual]. These specialized models, such as ANAH-v2 and custom Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) heads, consistently outperform general-purpose, state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 in zero-shot detection and out-of-domain generalization, proving that targeted, smaller-scale models can achieve superior security outcomes [Gu_2024_Anah, Shelmanov_2025_LLM]. Furthermore, innovations in process reward modeling, such as MCNIG, have drastically reduced the labeling complexity required for step-level evaluation, paving the way for more scalable reinforcement learning approaches [Royer_2026_Process].

Despite these impressive methodological advancements, significant vulnerabilities remain deeply embedded in modern LLM architectures, posing severe risks for real-world deployment. Rigorous evaluations have exposed a critical 'factuality gap,' demonstrating that high semantic similarity often masks alarmingly low factual precision—sometimes dropping as low as 4.38% [Kabongo_2025_Factual]. This finding fundamentally challenges the efficacy of traditional embedding-based metrics and underscores the urgent need for more rigorous, fact-centric evaluation paradigms. Additionally, models exhibit severe behavioral vulnerabilities, frequently altering factual claims to align with user personas, particularly when discussing controversial or sensitive topics [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. This persona-induced hallucination highlights a dangerous intersection between alignment techniques and factual grounding, where a model's desire to be 'helpful' or agreeable overrides its commitment to truth.

The testing landscape reflects this dynamic and rapidly maturing environment. It is characterized by a reliance on foundational benchmarks like HaluEval, alongside a necessary proliferation of custom evaluation suites (e.g., RealHall, ConsistencyAI, ANAH) designed to capture these highly nuanced failure modes. Evaluation metrics predominantly feature AUROC for threshold-independent detection and F1/Accuracy for classification, tested across a wide array of state-of-the-art open-weight models, heavily featuring the Llama, Qwen, and Mistral series.

In conclusion, while the field has made remarkable strides in developing scalable, efficient methodologies to detect and understand LLM hallucinations, critical gaps remain. The literature from 2023 to 2026 demonstrates a clear trajectory toward internal state analysis and specialized, efficient detection modules. However, future research must urgently address the limitations of current evaluation metrics, develop robust defenses against persona-induced fabrications, and extend these detection frameworks to multi-modal and real-time streaming contexts. The ongoing, overarching challenge for the field is to mitigate these ungrounded generations and enforce strict factual consistency without stifling the inherent creativity, fluency, and narrativity that make Large Language Models so powerful and widely applicable.


## Data and Model Poisoning

Temporal Trends:
The research landscape of Data and Model Poisoning within foundation models has undergone a rapid and alarming evolution between 2024 and 2025, characterized by a shift from unimodal text-based vulnerabilities to highly complex, multimodal, and embodied AI attack vectors. In the initial phase of this temporal window, the focus was heavily oriented toward systematizing and benchmarking the vulnerabilities of standard Large Language Models (LLMs). Works such as [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking] and [Li_2024_Backdoorllm] represent a critical foundational step during this period, providing comprehensive frameworks to evaluate how generative backdoors could be injected into open-source models like LLaMA-2, Mistral, and GPT-J. These 2024 studies established the baseline understanding that traditional defense mechanisms were severely inadequate against generative backdoor threats, setting the stage for more sophisticated offensive research.

As the field transitioned into late 2024 and early 2025, a distinct turning point occurred: the expansion of poisoning attacks beyond simple text generation into complex system architectures and multimodal environments. Researchers began to exploit the specific alignment and retrieval mechanisms that make modern AI systems functional. For instance, the vulnerability of the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline—traditionally viewed as a safety alignment mechanism—was exposed by [Duan_2025_RLHF], demonstrating that clean-label poisoning could achieve perfect Attack Success Rates (ASR) in Text-to-Image models. Simultaneously, the integration of LLMs into Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems introduced new attack surfaces, with [Nazary_2025_LLM] demonstrating in 2025 how malicious rewrites could manipulate item rankings in recommender systems without triggering global performance degradation.

Perhaps the most significant temporal trend in 2025 is the leap into embodied AI and physical-world interactions. The literature moved from digital text triggers to physical and environmental triggers. [Liu_2025_Natural] and [Zhou_2025_Goal] illustrate this progression by targeting Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models used in autonomous driving and robotics. The introduction of natural reflections [Liu_2025_Natural] and physical objects [Zhou_2025_Goal] as backdoor triggers represents a paradigm shift toward attacks that can be executed in the physical world, bypassing digital input filters entirely.

Concurrently, the methodology of executing these attacks evolved dramatically in terms of efficiency. While earlier 2024 models relied on dataset contamination followed by computationally expensive fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA), [Li_2024_Badedit] introduced a radical departure by bypassing the training phase entirely. By directly editing model parameters, they achieved robust backdoor injection using merely 15 samples in under two minutes, highlighting a trend toward hyper-efficient, low-resource poisoning.

Finally, 2025 also marks the emergence of 'constructive poisoning.' While the vast majority of the timeline is dominated by offensive capability scaling, [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] pivoted the narrative by utilizing indirect poisoning as a defensive tool for dataset ownership verification. This indicates a maturing field that is beginning to explore how the inherent memorization and trigger-response mechanisms of foundation models can be harnessed for intellectual property protection, establishing a dual-use trajectory for future poisoning research.

Taxonomy:
The domain of Data and Model Poisoning in foundation models can be structured into a comprehensive taxonomy comprising five primary sub-areas, reflecting the diverse methodologies, targets, and objectives observed in the 2024-2025 literature.

1. Injection Methodologies and Vectors
This category classifies how the malicious behavior is introduced into the model.
- Traditional Data Contamination: The most common approach, involving the insertion of poisoned samples into training or fine-tuning datasets, often followed by Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques like LoRA [Liu_2025_Natural, Li_2024_Backdoorllm].
- Direct Parameter Editing: A novel, training-free paradigm that directly manipulates the model's weights to embed backdoors, drastically reducing computational overhead and data requirements [Li_2024_Badedit].
- Clean-Label Poisoning: Highly stealthy techniques where the poisoned data appears correctly labeled and benign to human inspectors, often utilizing feature collisions during alignment phases like RLHF [Duan_2025_RLHF].

2. Trigger Modalities
This sub-area categorizes the nature of the stimuli used to activate the backdoor.
- Textual and Semantic Triggers: Digital text manipulations, including specific trigger words, semantic token-swapping, or LLM-generated rewrites designed to evade perplexity filters [Nazary_2025_LLM, Zhang_2025_Tokenswap, Bouaziz_2025_Winter].
- Visual and Physical Triggers: Triggers embedded in image data, ranging from digital artifacts to real-world physical phenomena such as natural reflections on surfaces [Liu_2025_Natural] or specific physical objects placed in a robot's environment [Zhou_2025_Goal].

3. Target Architectures and Modalities
Poisoning research is highly specialized based on the underlying architecture being attacked.
- Unimodal Large Language Models (LLMs): Attacks targeting standard text generation models (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral) [Li_2024_Backdoorllm, Li_2024_Badedit].
- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Action Models (VLAs): Attacks targeting multimodal systems that process visual inputs to generate text or robotic actions (e.g., LLaVA, OpenVLA) [Liu_2025_Natural, Zhou_2025_Goal, Zhang_2025_Tokenswap].
- Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Systems: Exploiting the external knowledge retrieval pipeline to manipulate downstream generation or ranking [Nazary_2025_LLM].
- Diffusion Models: Targeting Text-to-Image generation pipelines [Duan_2025_RLHF].

4. Attack Objectives and Payloads
This category defines the ultimate goal of the poisoning attack.
- Malicious Exploitation: Includes inducing targeted latency in critical systems (e.g., autonomous driving) [Liu_2025_Natural], manipulating item rankings in recommender systems [Nazary_2025_LLM], or forcing goal-oriented physical actions in robotics [Zhou_2025_Goal].
- Constructive Application: Utilizing poisoning mechanisms defensively, such as injecting benign, indirect triggers to verify dataset ownership and protect intellectual property [Bouaziz_2025_Winter].

5. Systematization and Vulnerability Assessment
Research dedicated to creating frameworks, benchmarks, and comprehensive evaluations to measure the efficacy of attacks and the robustness of existing defenses across various models [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking, Li_2024_Backdoorllm].

Gaps Filled:
The synthesized literature from 2024-2025 successfully addresses several critical gaps in the understanding of foundation model security, particularly concerning attack efficiency, multimodal vulnerabilities, and constructive applications of poisoning.

One of the most significant gaps filled is the computational bottleneck traditionally associated with model poisoning. Historically, injecting a robust backdoor required extensive dataset contamination and computationally expensive retraining or fine-tuning. [Li_2024_Badedit] directly addressed this limitation by introducing a direct parameter editing technique. By bypassing the training phase entirely, they demonstrated that backdoors could be injected using as few as 15 samples in under two minutes. This fills a crucial gap in threat modeling, proving that resource-constrained adversaries can successfully compromise massive LLMs without access to large-scale compute.

Another major gap addressed is the vulnerability of post-training alignment processes. While much prior work focused on pre-training or supervised fine-tuning, the security of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) remained under-explored. [Duan_2025_RLHF] filled this void by demonstrating that RLHF pipelines are highly susceptible to clean-label feature collisions, achieving up to a 1.00 Attack Success Rate (ASR) on Text-to-Image models. This shatters the assumption that human-feedback alignment inherently sanitizes models against malicious manipulation.

The literature also significantly advances our understanding of vulnerabilities in embodied AI and complex system architectures, moving beyond isolated text generation. Prior to these works, the physical-world implications of VLM and VLA poisoning were largely theoretical. [Liu_2025_Natural] filled this gap by proving that natural reflections could serve as highly effective visual triggers to induce latency in autonomous driving VLMs, highlighting a severe real-world safety risk. Similarly, [Zhou_2025_Goal] demonstrated that physical objects could trigger malicious goal-oriented actions in robotic VLAs, achieving a 99.6% ASR. Furthermore, [Nazary_2025_LLM] addressed the gap in composite system security by showing how RAG systems could be poisoned via LLM-based rewrites to manipulate recommender system rankings without degrading overall utility.

Finally, the field lacked robust mechanisms for proving dataset provenance in the era of massive, opaque web scraping. [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] filled this gap by flipping the poisoning paradigm on its head. They introduced a constructive use of indirect poisoning for dataset ownership verification, achieving extreme statistical confidence (p-values of 10^-55) using less than 0.005% contaminated tokens. This provides a vital tool for copyright enforcement and intellectual property protection in generative AI.

Collectively, systematization efforts by [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking] and [Li_2024_Backdoorllm] filled the gap of fragmented evaluation by providing comprehensive benchmarks that standardize how these diverse, generative backdoor threats are measured against existing defenses.

Open Gaps:
Despite the rapid advancements in demonstrating the efficacy of data and model poisoning, several significant gaps and open problems remain, primarily centered around defensive capabilities, real-world robustness, and the long-term persistence of injected vulnerabilities.

The most glaring open gap across the entire cluster is the 'Defense Deficit.' Comprehensive benchmarking by [Li_2024_Backdoorllm] and [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking] explicitly reveals that existing defense mechanisms struggle significantly to mitigate advanced, generative backdoor threats. Current defenses, often designed for simpler classification tasks or unimodal models, fail to detect stealthy manipulations like clean-label feature collisions [Duan_2025_RLHF] or semantic token-swapping that bypasses perplexity detectors [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. Developing robust, modality-agnostic defenses that can sanitize training data or detect parameter-edited backdoors without degrading the model's benign utility remains a massive unsolved challenge.

Secondly, while attacks in embodied AI have been proven effective in controlled environments, the robustness of physical triggers in highly chaotic, real-world settings requires further exploration. [Liu_2025_Natural] and [Zhou_2025_Goal] successfully utilized natural reflections and physical objects as triggers for VLMs and VLAs. However, it remains an open question how these triggers perform under varying environmental conditions (e.g., extreme weather, dynamic lighting, occlusion) outside of benchmark datasets like DriveLM or LIBERO. Furthermore, defending against physical triggers is inherently more difficult than filtering digital text, and the literature currently lacks frameworks for real-time, physical-world trigger neutralization in autonomous systems.

Another critical open problem is the persistence and stability of backdoors, particularly those injected via highly efficient methods. While [Li_2024_Badedit] demonstrated that direct parameter editing can inject backdoors in under two minutes with minimal data, it is unclear how resilient these edits are to subsequent model updates, continuous learning, or safety alignment patches. If a model undergoes further RLHF or PEFT by a benign actor, do the parameter edits wash out, or do they remain dormant? Understanding the lifecycle and durability of these backdoors is essential for long-term threat modeling.

Additionally, the scalability of constructive poisoning presents an open direction. [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] achieved remarkable statistical confidence in dataset ownership verification using a microscopic fraction of contaminated tokens. However, as foundation models increasingly train on multi-trillion token datasets scraped from the entire internet, the challenge of ensuring that these indirect triggers are reliably memorized and retrievable at scale—without colliding with other benign or malicious triggers—needs further investigation.

Finally, there is a lack of research on cross-modal trigger generalization. Current studies generally inject a trigger in one modality (e.g., an image) to affect an output in the same or a directly linked modality (e.g., text or robotic action). It remains an open question whether a backdoor injected via text data could be triggered by a semantically equivalent visual input in a multimodal model, which would represent a highly evasive and complex threat vector.

Methodological Convergences:
A deep analysis of the 2024-2025 literature reveals striking methodological convergences, as well as notable divergences, in how researchers approach data and model poisoning. 

The most dominant methodological convergence across all studies is the strict adherence to a 'Dual-Objective Optimization' paradigm. Universally, researchers operate under the constraint that a successful poisoning attack must achieve a high Attack Success Rate (ASR) while simultaneously preserving the model's benign utility. Stealthiness is treated not as an optional feature, but as a fundamental requirement. To achieve this, diverse evasion techniques are employed across different modalities. For instance, [Duan_2025_RLHF] utilizes clean-label feature collisions to ensure poisoned images appear benign to human evaluators. In the text domain, [Nazary_2025_LLM] employs semantic similarity thresholds to ensure malicious RAG rewrites blend naturally with benign content, while [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap] uses adaptive loss weighting and semantic token-swapping to bypass perplexity-based anomaly detectors. This convergence highlights a shared understanding that modern AI security threats are defined by their imperceptibility.

Another significant convergence is the reliance on trigger injection as the primary mechanism for backdoor activation. Whether the target is a text-based LLM, a VLM, or a VLA, the methodology fundamentally relies on conditioning the model to associate a specific, rare input with a malicious output. This is seen across the spectrum, from visual triggers like natural reflections [Liu_2025_Natural] and physical objects [Zhou_2025_Goal], to textual manipulations [Nazary_2025_LLM, Bouaziz_2025_Winter].

However, stark methodological divergences emerge in the execution of the injection phase. The dominant, traditional paradigm relies on dataset contamination followed by fine-tuning. Studies like [Liu_2025_Natural] and [Li_2024_Backdoorllm] utilize Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods, specifically LoRA, to update model weights based on poisoned data. This approach mimics the standard development pipeline of open-source models. In sharp contrast, [Li_2024_Badedit] diverges entirely from the gradient-descent training paradigm. They introduce direct parameter editing, bypassing training to manipulate weights directly. This divergence represents a split between data-centric poisoning and model-centric poisoning, with the latter offering unprecedented computational efficiency.

Furthermore, there is a profound divergence in the intent and application of the methodology. While the vast majority of the cluster ([Chowdhury_2024_Breaking, Duan_2025_RLHF, Li_2024_Backdoorllm, Nazary_2025_LLM, Liu_2025_Natural, Li_2024_Badedit, Zhou_2025_Goal, Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]) applies these techniques offensively to expose vulnerabilities and induce malicious behaviors (e.g., latency, ranking manipulation), [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] diverges by applying the exact same underlying principles of indirect poisoning constructively. By using poisoning for dataset ownership verification, they demonstrate that the methodological mechanics of backdooring are inherently neutral and can be engineered for defensive, intellectual property protection.

Testing Overview:
The testing methodologies employed across the 2024-2025 data and model poisoning literature are highly rigorous in quantifying offensive capabilities, relying on a standardized dual-metric evaluation paradigm, diverse benchmark datasets, and a wide array of state-of-the-art foundation models.

1. Dominant Evaluation Metrics
The universal standard for evaluating poisoning efficacy is the Attack Success Rate (ASR), which measures the frequency with which the injected trigger successfully elicits the targeted malicious behavior. The literature demonstrates alarmingly high ASRs, such as 1.00 in RLHF poisoning [Duan_2025_RLHF], 99.6% in VLA physical object triggers [Zhou_2025_Goal], and >80% in evasive token-swapping [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. Crucially, ASR is always paired with Benign Utility Metrics to prove stealthiness. Depending on the modality, these include Accuracy for classification tasks, ROUGE for text generation [Li_2024_Backdoorllm, Zhang_2025_Tokenswap], SSIM (Structural Similarity Index Measure) for image generation [Duan_2025_RLHF], and nDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) for evaluating ranking preservation in RAG-based recommender systems [Nazary_2025_LLM]. In constructive applications, statistical confidence (p-values) is used to quantify the certainty of dataset ownership verification [Bouaziz_2025_Winter].

2. Standard Models Tested
Evaluations span a comprehensive cross-section of modern, open-source foundation architectures. For unimodal text tasks, standard LLMs such as LLaMA-2, LLaMA-3, Mistral, and GPT-J are the primary testbeds [Li_2024_Backdoorllm, Li_2024_Badedit]. As the research extends into multimodal domains, the testing shifts to state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like LLaVA and Qwen2-VL [Liu_2025_Natural, Zhang_2025_Tokenswap], and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like OpenVLA for robotics [Zhou_2025_Goal]. Diffusion models, specifically Stable Diffusion, are utilized for testing Text-to-Image vulnerabilities [Duan_2025_RLHF]. This diversity ensures that the identified vulnerabilities are architectural rather than model-specific.

3. Commonly Used Benchmarks and Datasets
The choice of datasets reflects the specific application domains being targeted. General instruction-tuning vulnerabilities are frequently tested on the Alpaca dataset [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Multimodal and vision tasks heavily utilize MSCOCO [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. More specialized attacks utilize domain-specific benchmarks: DriveLM is used to simulate autonomous driving environments for latency attacks [Liu_2025_Natural], LIBERO is employed for testing robotic manipulation and goal-oriented actions [Zhou_2025_Goal], and MovieLens serves as the standard for evaluating RAG-based recommender system manipulation [Nazary_2025_LLM].

4. Rigor and Limitations
While the testing approaches are highly rigorous in proving the efficacy and stealthiness of the attacks (often achieving near-perfect ASRs with minimal data), they are notably lacking in rigorous adversarial defense evaluation. As highlighted by systematization efforts [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking, Li_2024_Backdoorllm], existing defense mechanisms struggle significantly. Consequently, many proposed attacks are evaluated against relatively weak or outdated defenses, simply because robust, modality-agnostic defenses against generative backdoors do not yet exist. The testing is highly effective at demonstrating vulnerability, but the field currently lacks standardized, resilient defensive benchmarks to test these attacks against.

Synthesis:
The research landscape of Data and Model Poisoning in foundation models has experienced a profound and rapid evolution between 2024 and 2025, transitioning from theoretical vulnerabilities in text-based systems to highly practical, multimodal threats with severe real-world implications. Early systematization efforts established that standard Large Language Models (LLMs) are inherently vulnerable to generative backdoors, and that existing defense mechanisms are fundamentally inadequate [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking, Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Building upon this baseline, recent literature has exposed the fragility of complex AI pipelines, demonstrating that even safety-alignment phases like RLHF [Duan_2025_RLHF] and external knowledge retrieval systems like RAG [Nazary_2025_LLM] can be stealthily manipulated to achieve near-perfect Attack Success Rates (ASR) without degrading benign utility. 

Crucially, the threat vector has expanded into embodied AI, where Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Action models (VLAs) can be compromised using physical-world triggers—such as natural reflections [Liu_2025_Natural] or physical objects [Zhou_2025_Goal]—to induce critical failures like latency in autonomous driving or malicious robotic actions. Concurrently, the efficiency of these attacks has skyrocketed; innovations like direct parameter editing [Li_2024_Badedit] allow adversaries to bypass computationally expensive training entirely, injecting robust backdoors with minimal data in a matter of minutes. Yet, this same underlying mechanism of memorization and trigger-response is not solely malicious. The emergence of constructive poisoning [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] demonstrates that these vulnerabilities can be inverted to serve as powerful defensive tools for dataset ownership verification. Ultimately, this cluster of research paints a picture of a highly asymmetric security landscape: offensive techniques are becoming hyper-efficient, evasive [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap], and physically manifest, while defensive capabilities lag significantly behind, urgently necessitating new paradigms in foundation model security.

Temporal Trends:
The research landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) security, specifically concerning prompt injection and jailbreak attacks, has undergone a rapid and transformative evolution between 2025 and 2026. This period is characterized by a definitive shift from static, rule-based, and single-model defensive paradigms toward a highly dynamic, automated, and co-evolutionary adversarial arms race. In the early phases of 2025, the literature reflects a growing consensus that static defenses are fundamentally inadequate against sophisticated adversaries. Researchers demonstrated that existing guardrails could be consistently bypassed by employing adaptive optimization techniques. During this phase, the offensive state-of-the-art advanced significantly through the application of modified Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) fuzzing, and genetic algorithms, which achieved alarming Attack Success Rates (ASR) ranging from 50% to 71% against state-of-the-art LLM agents [Zhan_2025_LLM, Wang_2025_LLM, Maloyan_2025_LLM]. This established a baseline understanding that attackers were successfully automating the discovery of adversarial prompts in both black-box and agentic environments.

As 2025 progressed, the temporal trend bifurcated into two distinct but interconnected trajectories: the pursuit of extreme computational efficiency and the development of dynamic, ensemble-based systems. On the offensive side, a critical turning point was the introduction of knowledge distillation to transfer jailbreaking capabilities from massive LLMs to highly efficient Small Language Models (SLMs). This asymmetric warfare tactic allowed attackers to generate adversarial inputs 3.7 times faster than traditional LLM-based attacks while maintaining an exceptionally high ASR of over 96% [Slms_2025_Efficient]. Consequently, the defensive community was forced to pivot away from computationally heavy semantic filters, which introduced unacceptable latency into user-facing applications.

By late 2025 and moving into 2026, defensive methodologies evolved to meet this need for speed and adaptability. The literature highlights a renaissance of lightweight, highly optimized interventions. For instance, security-oriented prompt compression emerged as a dominant technique in 2025, successfully reducing ASR to a mere 1% with negligible latency overhead [Li_2025_LLM]. By 2026, this trend toward efficiency culminated in the revival of classical machine learning techniques augmented by advanced data curation; researchers demonstrated that strictly curated, geometrically bounded linear Support Vector Machines (SVMs) could outperform heavy semantic models like Prompt Guard 2, achieving submillisecond latency [Corll_2026_Mirror].

Simultaneously, the temporal progression reveals a deepening understanding of structural and architectural vulnerabilities. In 2026, research began to dissect how the very structure of prompts dictates safety outcomes. A landmark finding during this period was the paradoxical vulnerability of reasoning-enhanced models, alongside the discovery that few-shot demonstrations—traditionally used to improve model performance—actually degrade the security of Task-Oriented Prompts by up to 21.2%, even as they enhance Role-Oriented Prompts [Wang_2026_LLM]. 

Finally, the overarching trend bridging 2025 and 2026 is the institutionalization of the 'arms race' through co-evolutionary frameworks. Recognizing that static snapshots of security are fleeting, the field progressed toward continuous, real-time adaptation. Frameworks like AEGIS [Liu_2025_Aegis] introduced co-evolutionary textual gradient optimization to iteratively and simultaneously improve both attacks and defenses. This was complemented by the deployment of real-time self-tuning multi-agent moderators [Zhang_2025_Real] and multi-model committees [Maloyan_2025_LLM], marking a definitive transition from static fortification to active, dynamic cyber-defense ecosystems.

Taxonomy:
The research synthesized in this cluster can be systematically categorized into four primary sub-areas, reflecting the dual nature of the adversarial arms race and the underlying structural analyses of LLM vulnerabilities. 

1. Advanced Offensive Mechanisms (Attack Generation)
This category encompasses methodologies designed to systematically bypass LLM guardrails through automated and optimized prompt engineering. It is further divided into two distinct approaches:
1a. Adaptive and Heuristic Optimization: This sub-area focuses on algorithmic searches through the discrete token space to find adversarial suffixes or prefixes. Key techniques include modified Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) fuzzing, and genetic algorithms. These methods are particularly effective in black-box and agentic environments, achieving high Attack Success Rates (ASR) [Zhan_2025_LLM, Wang_2025_LLM, Maloyan_2025_LLM].
1b. Asymmetric and Distilled Attacks: A novel offensive paradigm where the computational burden of generating attacks is drastically reduced. This involves leveraging knowledge distillation to transfer the jailbreaking capabilities of large models into highly efficient Small Language Models (SLMs), enabling rapid, high-volume attack generation [Slms_2025_Efficient].

2. Lightweight and High-Efficiency Defenses
In direct response to the latency introduced by traditional semantic guardrails and the speed of SLM-based attacks, this category focuses on defenses that prioritize submillisecond execution and minimal computational overhead without sacrificing utility.
2a. Input Screening and Geometric Curation: The use of classical, lightweight machine learning models—such as linear Support Vector Machines (SVMs)—trained on strictly curated geometric data boundaries to filter malicious prompts faster than deep neural networks [Corll_2026_Mirror].
2b. Security-Oriented Prompt Transformation: Techniques that alter the input prompt before it reaches the core LLM. A primary example is prompt compression, which strips away adversarial noise or obfuscation while retaining the core semantic intent, effectively neutralizing the attack payload with negligible latency [Li_2025_LLM].

3. Dynamic and Multi-Agent Defense Frameworks
Moving beyond single-point, static defenses, this category explores systemic, ensemble, and continuously updating defensive architectures.
3a. Co-Evolutionary Systems: Frameworks like AEGIS that utilize textual gradient optimization to pit attack generators against defensive filters in a continuous, iterative loop, thereby hardening the system against zero-day injection strategies [Liu_2025_Aegis].
3b. Real-Time and Committee-Based Moderation: The deployment of multi-agent moderators that self-tune in real-time [Zhang_2025_Real], alongside multi-model committees that aggregate the security assessments of diverse models to prevent single-point-of-failure bypasses [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

4. Structural and Architectural Vulnerability Analysis
This foundational category investigates how the inherent design of prompts and the underlying architecture of the LLMs themselves contribute to security vulnerabilities.
4a. Prompt Engineering Impacts: Research analyzing how standard prompt enhancements (e.g., few-shot demonstrations) interact with different prompt types (Role-Oriented vs. Task-Oriented), sometimes inadvertently degrading security [Wang_2026_LLM].
4b. Architectural Paradoxes: The critical examination of advanced model capabilities, specifically the finding that reasoning-enhanced models exhibit a higher susceptibility to prompt injection, suggesting that complex internal logic chains can be hijacked by attackers [Wang_2026_LLM].

Gaps Filled:
The papers in this 2025-2026 cluster address several critical gaps in the preceding LLM security literature, primarily focusing on the tension between robust security, computational efficiency, and model utility.

First, a major historical gap in LLM defense has been the 'efficiency bottleneck.' Previous defensive mechanisms, such as secondary LLM-based semantic filters (e.g., early versions of Prompt Guard), introduced significant inference latency and computational costs, making them impractical for high-throughput, real-time applications. This gap was decisively filled by two distinct methodologies. Corll et al. [Corll_2026_Mirror] addressed this by demonstrating that strict geometric data curation allows for the use of submillisecond linear SVM screening, which successfully outperforms heavier semantic models. Similarly, Li et al. [Li_2025_LLM] filled the latency gap through security-oriented prompt compression, which neutralizes adversarial inputs by reducing ASR to 1% while maintaining negligible latency overhead.

Second, the literature previously lacked a comprehensive understanding of how automated, adaptive attacks could scale against complex, multi-step LLM agents. Zhan et al. [Zhan_2025_LLM] and Wang et al. [Wang_2025_LLM] filled this void by adapting optimization techniques like modified GCG and MCTS fuzzing specifically for agentic environments, proving that static defenses are consistently bypassed (achieving 50% to 71% ASR) when adversaries use dynamic search algorithms.

Third, there was a significant gap in understanding the asymmetric nature of adversarial generation. Previously, generating sophisticated jailbreaks required substantial compute. The research on SLMs [Slms_2025_Efficient] filled this gap by proving that adversarial capabilities can be successfully distilled into Small Language Models. This demonstrated that attackers could achieve >96% ASR while operating 3.7 times faster than LLMs, fundamentally altering the threat landscape by proving that high-tier attacks do not require high-tier compute.

Fourth, the field suffered from a lack of dynamic, self-improving defensive architectures. Static guardrails were routinely rendered obsolete by novel attacks. This gap was addressed by the introduction of co-evolutionary and multi-agent frameworks. Liu et al. [Liu_2025_Aegis] introduced AEGIS, bridging the offensive-defensive divide by using co-evolutionary textual gradient optimization to iteratively improve both sides. Furthermore, Zhang et al. [Zhang_2025_Real] and Maloyan et al. [Maloyan_2025_LLM] filled the need for adaptable runtime defenses by developing real-time self-tuning multi-agent moderators and multi-model committees, significantly improving robustness over static, single-model guardrails.

Finally, a nuanced gap existed regarding the structural impact of standard prompt engineering techniques on security. Wang et al. [Wang_2026_LLM] provided critical insights here, filling the knowledge gap by proving that structural choices dictate safety. They revealed the counterintuitive finding that few-shot demonstrations, while enhancing Role-Oriented Prompts, actually degrade the security of Task-Oriented Prompts by up to 21.2%.

Open Gaps:
Despite the significant advancements in balancing security, efficiency, and utility, several critical gaps and open problems remain in the LLM prompt injection landscape.

A paramount open question arises from the architectural vulnerability analysis: the paradox of reasoning-enhanced models. Wang et al. [Wang_2026_LLM] identified that models equipped with advanced reasoning capabilities paradoxically exhibit higher vulnerability to prompt injection and jailbreaks. The underlying mechanistic reasons for this remain an open gap. It is hypothesized that the extended internal logic chains or Chain-of-Thought (CoT) processes provide a larger 'attack surface' for adversarial prompts to hijack the model's reasoning, but detailed mechanistic interpretability studies are required to fully understand and mitigate this without lobotomizing the model's reasoning capabilities.

Secondly, while the cluster demonstrates highly effective lightweight defenses like submillisecond SVMs [Corll_2026_Mirror] and prompt compression [Li_2025_LLM], the long-term resilience of these geometric and compression-based defenses against the newly discovered high-speed SLM attacks [Slms_2025_Efficient] remains untested. If attackers can generate highly optimized, distilled attacks 3.7 times faster using SLMs, they may be able to rapidly map and evade the decision boundaries of linear SVMs or find adversarial tokens that survive prompt compression. The intersection of high-speed asymmetric attacks versus ultra-lightweight defenses is a critical open frontier.

Thirdly, the scalability and real-world deployment of dynamic and co-evolutionary defenses present significant challenges. While frameworks like AEGIS [Liu_2025_Aegis] and real-time self-tuning moderators [Zhang_2025_Real] show immense promise in benchmark environments, their computational overhead during the *training/tuning* phase (even if inference is fast) and their stability in highly complex, multi-modal, or long-context agentic workflows remain open problems. As agents take on more autonomous, long-running tasks, ensuring that multi-agent moderators do not suffer from context-window degradation or cumulative error over hundreds of turns is a gap not fully addressed by current evaluations.

Furthermore, there is a persistent gap in universal semantic understanding across diverse languages and modalities. The current methodologies heavily rely on textual gradient optimization and structural prompt choices (e.g., Role vs. Task oriented) [Wang_2026_LLM, Liu_2025_Aegis]. It remains an open question how these vulnerabilities and defenses translate to multimodal prompt injection (e.g., adversarial images or audio paired with text) or low-resource languages, which are often underrepresented in standard benchmarks like AdvBench and HarmBench.

Finally, while multi-model committees [Maloyan_2025_LLM] improve robustness, they inherently multiply inference costs. Finding a theoretical framework that guarantees the security of a single, unified model without relying on the crutch of external committees or the latency of multi-agent debate remains the holy grail of LLM security research.

Methodological Convergences:
A deep analysis of the methodologies employed across this research cluster reveals striking convergences, as well as notable divergences, primarily centered around the trilemma of security, computational efficiency, and model utility.

The most prominent methodological convergence is the universal adoption of automated, adaptive optimization techniques for vulnerability discovery. Across multiple papers, researchers have abandoned manual red-teaming in favor of algorithmic approaches. Techniques such as modified Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) fuzzing, and genetic algorithms are consistently employed to navigate the discrete token space and bypass guardrails [Zhan_2025_LLM, Wang_2025_LLM, Maloyan_2025_LLM]. This convergence underscores a shared understanding that the adversarial landscape is too vast and complex for static, human-driven exploration.

Another significant convergence is the overarching prioritization of computational efficiency, though it manifests differently across the offensive and defensive divides. Both attackers and defenders are actively moving away from reliance on massive, monolithic LLMs for their respective tasks. On the offensive side, this is evident in the use of knowledge distillation to empower Small Language Models (SLMs) for rapid attack generation [Slms_2025_Efficient]. On the defensive side, there is a parallel convergence toward lightweight interventions, such as security-oriented prompt compression [Li_2025_LLM] and the use of submillisecond linear SVMs [Corll_2026_Mirror]. This is evidenced by the development of co-evolutionary frameworks like AEGIS [Liu_2025_Aegis], real-time self-tuning multi-agent moderators [Zhang_2025_Real], and multi-model committees [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. These approaches share a foundational philosophy: security is not a static state but a continuous process of adaptation and consensus.

While some dynamic defenses rely on the deep semantic understanding of multi-agent moderators [Zhang_2025_Real] or multi-model committees [Maloyan_2025_LLM], others explicitly reject heavy semantic processing in favor of strict geometric data curation and classical statistical boundaries (e.g., linear SVMs) to achieve superior speed [Corll_2026_Mirror].

Testing Overview:
The testing methodologies across this 2025-2026 cluster demonstrate a high degree of rigor, standardization, and a dual focus on both security efficacy and operational viability. Overall, the testing approaches are highly rigorous in their measurement of the security-utility-latency trade-off. However, a lacking area in the current testing paradigm is the evaluation of these dynamic defenses against long-term, multi-modal, or highly contextual social engineering attacks that span days or weeks, as current benchmarks primarily focus on discrete, session-based interactions.

The research landscape surrounding adversarial attacks and defenses in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has undergone a rapid and highly consequential evolution between 2024 and 2026. Research during this period, such as the work by [Yang_2024_Adversarial], highlighted the alarming susceptibility of models deployed in critical sectors like healthcare, demonstrating that standard models could be easily manipulated when processing sensitive datasets like MIMIC-III. Concurrently, defensive strategies in 2024 leaned heavily toward external stateful detection mechanisms and prompt-level manipulations. For instance, [Wang_2024_Advqdet] introduced contrastive prompt tuning as a mechanism to detect query-based black-box attacks, achieving impressive detection rates up to 99%. This early phase was characterized by a 'patchwork' approach to security, where defenses were often bolted onto existing architectures rather than integrated into the fundamental training paradigms of the models.

Moving into 2025, the literature reflects a dramatic escalation in both offensive and defensive methodologies, marking the onset of a true adversarial arms race. Attackers began to move beyond simple prompt injection, leveraging complex optimization techniques to weaponize off-the-shelf LLMs for explicit cybersecurity threats. The synthesis of evasive malware and obfuscated webshells became a prominent vector, with researchers employing reinforcement learning (PPO) [Ding_2025_Reward], genetic algorithms [Yin_2025_LLM], and constraint-driven prompting [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] to bypass traditional security filters. The success of these offensive techniques was stark: [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] demonstrated a reduction in Anti-Virus (AV) detection rates by over 72% for generated malware, while [Ding_2025_Reward] achieved an 85.7% escape rate for obfuscated webshells. In response, 2025 also saw the deployment of much more sophisticated defensive alignments. The field recognized that simple safety filters were insufficient and prone to overrefusal. Consequently, researchers introduced advanced alignment techniques, such as the two-stage rule learning with reinforcement learning proposed by [Liu_2025_Purpcode], which achieved a near-perfect 99.8% defense success rate against jailbreaks while simultaneously mitigating the overrefusal problem. Furthermore, the introduction of advanced reasoning models (e.g., GPT-o3) in 2025 did not spell the end of adversarial vulnerabilities; rather, it spurred the development of novel attack frameworks like FOA-Attack, which utilized feature alignment via Optimal Transport to achieve an 81% Attack Success Rate (ASR) even on these state-of-the-art closed-source MLLMs [Jia_2025_Adversarial].

By 2026, the research paradigm shifted toward massive-scale benchmarking and highly precise, parameter-efficient internal interventions. The realization that adversarial attacks were pervasive led to the creation of comprehensive evaluation frameworks, such as the 2-million-sample AICD Bench for AI-generated code detection [Orel_2026_AI], indicating a maturation of the field where statistical rigor and large-scale validation became paramount. Defensively, the blunt instruments of full-model fine-tuning or external wrappers were increasingly replaced by surgical, mechanistic approaches. [Thang_2026_LLM] exemplifies this 2026 trend with the introduction of neuron-level cluster-based fine-tuning, a technique that modifies specific internal neurons to increase secure code generation by up to 2.5x with minimal parameter updates. This chronological progression from 2024 to 2026 illustrates a clear trajectory: from the discovery of domain-specific flaws and external patching, through a period of intense methodological escalation involving RL and genetic algorithms, to a mature phase focused on massive-scale evaluation and precise, mechanistic internal alignment.

Taxonomy:
The research landscape of adversarial attacks and defenses in LLMs and MLLMs can be systematically categorized into three primary sub-areas, reflecting the dual-use nature of these models and the specific domains in which they are deployed. 

1. Offensive Adversarial Generation and Model Weaponization
This sub-area focuses on the methodologies used to exploit LLM vulnerabilities, specifically leveraging the models' generative capabilities to synthesize malicious payloads or adversarial examples. 
- Cybersecurity Threat Synthesis: A major focus is the generation of evasive malware and obfuscated webshells. Researchers utilize constraint-driven prompting [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] and reinforcement learning (PPO) [Ding_2025_Reward] to force off-the-shelf LLMs to produce code that actively evades traditional Anti-Virus (AV) and Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS).
- Advanced Adversarial Perturbations: Beyond code generation, this category includes the creation of adversarial examples designed to fool both language and vision-language models. Techniques such as genetic algorithms [Yin_2025_LLM] and feature alignment via Optimal Transport (e.g., FOA-Attack) [Jia_2025_Adversarial] are employed to craft inputs that reliably trigger targeted misclassifications or jailbreaks, even in advanced reasoning models.

2. Defensive Alignment and Robust Hardening
This sub-area encompasses the strategies developed to secure models against the aforementioned offensive techniques. It is characterized by a spectrum of interventions ranging from external detection to deep internal modifications.
- Parameter-Efficient and Mechanistic Interventions: This involves modifying the internal architecture or specific weights of the model to inherently resist manipulation. Techniques include neuron-level cluster-based fine-tuning [Thang_2026_LLM], which surgically alters specific neurons to promote secure code generation, and targeted internal weight adjustments for domain-specific robustness [Yang_2024_Adversarial].
- Advanced Alignment via Reinforcement Learning: To combat the trade-off between security and model utility (the overrefusal problem), researchers employ complex alignment strategies. Two-stage rule learning combined with RL [Liu_2025_Purpcode] represents a sophisticated approach to teaching models to reason about safety rules dynamically rather than relying on static filters.
- Adversarial Training and Prompt Tuning: This includes training models on adversarial examples to improve baseline robustness [Yin_2025_LLM] and utilizing contrastive prompt tuning [Wang_2024_Advqdet] to create stateful, external detection mechanisms capable of identifying query-based black-box attacks.

3. Domain-Specific Security and Detection Frameworks
This sub-area addresses the unique security challenges and detection requirements of specific application domains, recognizing that adversarial vulnerabilities manifest differently depending on the context.
- AI-Generated Code Detection: As LLMs are increasingly used for software development, distinguishing between human-written and AI-generated (and potentially malicious) code is critical. This involves the creation of massive benchmarks like the AICD Bench [Orel_2026_AI] and the development of specialized detectors.
- High-Stakes Domain Robustness: This focuses on securing models deployed in critical sectors where adversarial manipulation could have severe real-world consequences, such as the healthcare domain, requiring evaluation on specialized datasets like MIMIC-III [Yang_2024_Adversarial].

Gaps Filled:
The research synthesized in this cluster addresses several critical gaps in the pre-existing literature regarding LLM security, particularly in the realms of detection robustness, alignment efficiency, and the mitigation of the overrefusal phenomenon.

First, the literature previously lacked robust, large-scale mechanisms for detecting AI-generated code, a critical vulnerability as LLMs are increasingly weaponized for cyberattacks. This gap was significantly addressed by the introduction of massive, specialized datasets and rigorous evaluation frameworks. The creation of the 2-million-sample AICD Bench [Orel_2026_AI] provided the necessary scale to train and evaluate detectors meaningfully. Furthermore, the application of adversarial training and contrastive prompt tuning dramatically enhanced detection robustness in this domain, boosting adversarial code detection accuracy from a fragile baseline of ~32% to a highly robust ~94% [Yin_2025_LLM]. Similarly, the challenge of detecting query-based black-box attacks without access to model internals was resolved through contrastive prompt tuning, which achieved up to 99% detection efficacy [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

Second, a persistent gap in defensive alignment has been the 'overrefusal' problem, where models trained to be safe become overly cautious, refusing benign requests and thereby degrading their utility. [Liu_2025_Purpcode] addressed this by moving beyond simple negative-sample training. By implementing a two-stage rule learning process integrated with reinforcement learning, they successfully aligned models to reason about safety constraints. This approach filled the gap by achieving a 99.8% defense success rate against complex jailbreaks while explicitly reducing overrefusal rates, proving that high security does not strictly require a compromise in helpfulness.

Finally, the computational inefficiency and potential catastrophic forgetting associated with full-model fine-tuning for security purposes represented a significant barrier to deploying robust models. [Thang_2026_LLM] filled this methodological gap by introducing neuron-level cluster-based fine-tuning. By identifying and updating only the specific neurons responsible for secure code generation, this parameter-efficient approach increased secure output by up to 2.5x with minimal parameter updates, demonstrating that deep, mechanistic interventions can be both highly effective and computationally lightweight compared to traditional alignment methods.

Open Gaps:
Despite the significant advancements in defensive alignments and detection mechanisms, several profound gaps and open problems remain in the field of LLM and MLLM adversarial security, primarily concerning the resilience of advanced reasoning models, the continuous evolution of evasive payloads, and the cross-domain generalization of defenses.

A paramount open problem is the persistent vulnerability of state-of-the-art, closed-source reasoning models (often referred to as System 2 models). While it was hypothesized that enhanced reasoning capabilities might naturally confer greater adversarial robustness, recent findings contradict this. The FOA-Attack framework [Jia_2025_Adversarial] demonstrated that even highly advanced models like GPT-o3 and Claude 3.7 remain highly susceptible to transferable adversarial examples, reaching an 81% Attack Success Rate (ASR). The gap lies in understanding *why* explicit reasoning steps fail to identify and neutralize feature-aligned adversarial perturbations, and how to design alignment strategies that protect the reasoning process itself, rather than just the final output.

Furthermore, the cybersecurity domain faces a continuous, escalating arms race where offensive generation capabilities frequently outpace defensive detection. While defenses have improved, off-the-shelf LLMs can still be readily weaponized to generate highly evasive malicious payloads. The fact that techniques like constraint-driven prompting [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] and RL-based generation [Ding_2025_Reward] can reduce AV detection rates by over 72% and achieve 85.7% escape rates for webshells indicates a critical gap in real-time, semantic threat detection. Current AV and IDS systems, and even many AI-based detectors, struggle to identify the malicious intent of syntactically novel, LLM-generated code. Closing this gap requires moving beyond pattern matching to deep, execution-simulated semantic analysis within the detection pipeline.

Finally, there is a significant gap in the generalization and transferability of defensive mechanisms across different modalities and high-stakes domains. Much of the current research operates in silos. For instance, while internal weight modifications show promise in healthcare settings (MIMIC-III) [Yang_2024_Adversarial], and neuron-level fine-tuning works for secure code generation [Thang_2026_LLM], it remains an open question whether these highly specialized, domain-specific defenses can be unified into a generalized robustness framework. The field lacks a comprehensive understanding of how adversarial vulnerabilities intersect across vision, language, and code modalities, and whether a defense optimized for one modality inadvertently introduces vulnerabilities in another.

Methodological Convergences:
A critical analysis of the methodologies employed across the 2024-2026 literature reveals striking convergences in the underlying mathematical and optimization frameworks used by both attackers and defenders, alongside significant divergences in the architectural level at which interventions are applied.

The most prominent methodological convergence is the heavy reliance on Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), as the primary engine for both offensive weaponization and defensive alignment. On the offensive side, RL is utilized to iteratively refine malicious payloads, rewarding the model for generating code that successfully evades detection environments [Ding_2025_Reward]. Conversely, on the defensive side, RL is the cornerstone of advanced alignment, used to train models to balance complex safety rules against helpfulness, thereby mitigating overrefusal [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. This convergence highlights RL as the dominant paradigm for navigating the complex, non-differentiable reward landscapes inherent in LLM security. Additionally, adversarial training—exposing the model to generated attacks during the training phase—has converged as a universally accepted baseline for improving robustness, particularly in detection tasks [Yin_2025_LLM]. Furthermore, optimization techniques like genetic algorithms [Yin_2025_LLM] and Optimal Transport for feature alignment [Jia_2025_Adversarial] are increasingly standard for crafting highly evasive, transferable adversarial examples.

However, a major methodological divergence exists regarding the optimal level of intervention for defensive strategies. One distinct camp advocates for external, stateful, or prompt-level interventions. These approaches, such as contrastive prompt tuning [Wang_2024_Advqdet] or constraint-driven prompt filters [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph], treat the LLM largely as a black box. They are highly favored for their ease of deployment, model-agnostic nature, and ability to be updated rapidly without retraining the core model. 

In stark contrast, a growing body of research argues that true robustness requires deep, internal mechanistic interventions. This camp focuses on modifying internal model weights or specific neural pathways, requiring white-box access. Methodologies here include neuron-level cluster-based fine-tuning [Thang_2026_LLM] and domain-specific internal weight adjustments [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. The divergence lies in the fundamental philosophy of security: external methods prioritize agility and broad applicability, while internal methods argue that vulnerabilities are deeply embedded in the model's latent space and can only be eradicated through precise, parameter-level surgery. This tension between external patching and internal healing remains a defining methodological debate in the field.

Testing Overview:
The testing methodologies employed across the adversarial LLM research landscape from 2024 to 2026 demonstrate a rigorous, albeit sometimes siloed, approach to evaluating model vulnerabilities and defensive efficacy. The evaluation frameworks rely heavily on a combination of universal security metrics and highly specialized, domain-specific datasets.

1. Dominant Evaluation Metrics:
The undisputed standard metric across almost all offensive and defensive evaluations is the Attack Success Rate (ASR). ASR is utilized universally to quantify the baseline vulnerabilities of models, the efficacy of novel attack vectors like FOA-Attack, and the corresponding drop in vulnerability when defenses are applied [Yin_2025_LLM, Jia_2025_Adversarial, Akil_2025_Llmalmorph, Wang_2024_Advqdet, Yang_2024_Adversarial]. However, because ASR alone cannot capture the nuances of detection or domain-specific evasion, it is frequently supplemented. In detection tasks, particularly for AI-generated code, Macro-F1 scores are standard to account for class imbalances [Orel_2026_AI]. In the cybersecurity domain, specifically regarding malware and webshell generation, 'Escape Rates' (the percentage of generated payloads that successfully bypass standard AV/IDS systems) serve as the critical real-world metric [Ding_2025_Reward].

2. Commonly Used Benchmarks and Datasets:
Testing is characterized by a split between massive generalized benchmarks and highly specific domain datasets. For code generation and detection, the field has moved towards massive scale, exemplified by the 2-million-sample AICD Bench [Orel_2026_AI], which provides the statistical power necessary for rigorous evaluation. In vision-language adversarial tasks, standard computer vision benchmarks remain prevalent, including NIPS 2017, MSCOCO, and ImageNet [Jia_2025_Adversarial, Wang_2024_Advqdet]. For high-stakes, domain-specific testing, specialized datasets like the MIMIC-III clinical database are utilized to evaluate vulnerabilities in healthcare applications [Yang_2024_Adversarial].

3. Standard Models Tested:
The evaluations span a comprehensive cross-section of the current AI ecosystem, ensuring that findings are not isolated to a single architecture. Open-source models, which allow for white-box attacks and internal mechanistic defenses, are heavily tested, including various iterations of Qwen, Llama, CodeT5, and CLIP [Jia_2025_Adversarial, Liu_2025_Purpcode, Thang_2026_LLM]. Crucially, the testing methodology rigorously includes state-of-the-art closed-source models accessed via API. Evaluating models like GPT-4o, the reasoning-enhanced GPT-o3, and Claude 3.7 [Jia_2025_Adversarial, Liu_2025_Purpcode, Yang_2024_Adversarial] is vital for understanding the transferability of black-box attacks and the inherent vulnerabilities of the most advanced commercial systems.

4. Critique of Testing Approaches:
While the scale of testing (e.g., 2M samples in AICD Bench) and the inclusion of cutting-edge reasoning models (GPT-o3) represent highly rigorous practices, the testing landscape is somewhat lacking in cross-domain standardization. Defenses are often evaluated in silos—a defense proven on MIMIC-III is rarely cross-evaluated on AICD Bench. The field would benefit from a unified, multi-modal, cross-domain benchmark suite that evaluates ASR, Escape Rates, and Macro-F1 simultaneously across healthcare, cybersecurity, and general vision-language tasks to truly measure the generalization of proposed defenses.

Synthesis:
The research conducted between 2024 and 2026 on adversarial attacks and defenses in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) illuminates a rapidly escalating arms race, characterized by a profound shift from the discovery of superficial vulnerabilities to the deployment of highly sophisticated, methodologically diverse interventions. Early work in this period established the alarming baseline susceptibility of models in high-stakes domains, such as healthcare [Yang_2024_Adversarial], and initiated the development of external detection mechanisms like contrastive prompt tuning [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. However, the landscape quickly evolved as attackers began weaponizing off-the-shelf LLMs using advanced optimization techniques—including reinforcement learning, genetic algorithms, and Optimal Transport—to synthesize highly evasive malware, obfuscated webshells, and transferable adversarial examples [Ding_2025_Reward, Yin_2025_LLM, Akil_2025_Llmalmorph, Jia_2025_Adversarial]. This offensive escalation forced a corresponding maturation in defensive strategies. The field moved beyond blunt safety filters, which suffered from severe overrefusal, toward nuanced, reasoning-based RL alignments [Liu_2025_Purpcode] and highly precise, parameter-efficient internal modifications, such as neuron-level cluster-based fine-tuning [Thang_2026_LLM]. Furthermore, the necessity for rigorous, large-scale evaluation birthed massive benchmarks like the 2-million-sample AICD Bench for AI-generated code detection [Orel_2026_AI]. Despite these significant defensive strides, critical challenges remain unresolved. The persistent vulnerability of state-of-the-art reasoning models (e.g., GPT-o3) to advanced frameworks like FOA-Attack [Jia_2025_Adversarial] underscores that enhanced System 2 reasoning does not inherently guarantee adversarial robustness. Ultimately, this cluster of research highlights the dual-use reality of modern AI: as models become more capable, the methodologies required to both weaponize and secure them converge on increasingly complex optimization paradigms, necessitating continuous, cross-domain vigilance and deep mechanistic alignment.
