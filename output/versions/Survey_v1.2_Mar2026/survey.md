## Abstract

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical, user-facing applications and autonomous systems has exposed them to a diverse array of security vulnerabilities, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of their evolving threat landscape. This survey presents an extensive examination of LLM security, encompassing both inherent vulnerabilities and external threats across the entire model lifecycle. Through a systematic literature review of 28 foundational and contemporary papers, this study categorizes the current threat landscape into distinct phases of model development and deployment. The analysis identifies and evaluates critical vulnerabilities, dividing them into training-time threats, such as data and model poisoning, and inference-time threats, including sophisticated adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and jailbreaking. Furthermore, the survey addresses output integrity and reliability by examining the pervasive issue of LLM hallucinations, highlighting how these phenomena compromise system trustworthiness and user safety. Beyond threat characterization, this paper synthesizes cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies designed to fortify LLM architectures against these multifaceted exploits. It provides a critical evaluation of current testing methodologies, offering insights into standardized frameworks and evaluation metrics necessary for rigorously assessing model robustness. By consolidating these findings, the survey illuminates fundamental tensions in secure AI deployment, such as the trade-off between model utility and safety constraints. It concludes by outlining open challenges in the field, ultimately charting a strategic course for future research in adversarial machine learning to ensure the safe and reliable deployment of next-generation language models.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable milestones across a wide range of fields, fundamentally transforming domains such as computer vision, natural language processing (NLP), and multimodal learning [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Building upon these foundational advancements, the recent proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence. Modern LLMs and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) exhibit unprecedented capabilities in reasoning, contextual understanding, and generative tasks, paving the way for their widespread integration into critical infrastructure, autonomous agents, and daily consumer applications [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. However, as these systems become increasingly autonomous and are entrusted with high-stakes decision-making, a profound security paradox has emerged: the very architectural complexity and massive scale that grant LLMs their extraordinary capabilities also introduce an expansive and highly vulnerable attack surface. 

Despite rigorous safety alignment processes during development, foundation models harbor pervasive flaws that can be exploited by malicious actors. Historically, deep learning models have been known to be highly vulnerable to adversarial attacks, wherein subtle, often imperceptible perturbations to input data deceive the model into making incorrect predictions [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. In the context of LLMs, these vulnerabilities have evolved from continuous-space manipulations (such as altering image pixels) to complex, discrete-space optimizations. Attackers now craft meticulous adversarial prompts designed to bypass safety guardrails, forcing the model to exhibit unintended, harmful, or biased behaviors [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. The potential consequences of such vulnerabilities grow exponentially when LLMs are deployed in sensitive sectors. For instance, the integration of LLMs into healthcare applications offers promising diagnostic and administrative advancements, but their susceptibility to adversarial manipulation poses a severe threat, potentially leading to catastrophic outcomes in delicate medical contexts [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. Similarly, in specialized domains such as AI-generated code detection and software development, attackers actively seek to bypass security filters to generate malware or evade plagiarism detection [Yin_2025_LLM].

The contemporary LLM threat landscape is multifaceted, spanning the entire model lifecycle from initial training to real-world inference. During the training phase, models are exposed to data and model poisoning attacks. Because state-of-the-art LLMs require massive, often uncurated datasets scraped from the public internet, adversaries can inject malicious payloads or backdoors into the training corpus. These training-time threats compromise the foundational integrity of the model, allowing attackers to trigger specific, malicious behaviors post-deployment using predefined keywords or contexts [Carlini_2024_Poisoning]. 

At inference time, the threat landscape bifurcates into highly sophisticated attack vectors. First, adversarial attacks continue to plague LLMs, with adversaries utilizing both white-box access (where the model's architecture and weights are known) and realistic black-box queries to systematically degrade model performance [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Second, the unique instruction-following nature of LLMs has given rise to prompt injection and jailbreaking. In these scenarios, attackers embed malicious instructions within seemingly benign inputs, effectively hijacking the model's operational logic to bypass Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and other alignment mechanisms [Wei_2025_Jailbreak]. 

Furthermore, the security of LLMs is not solely threatened by active, malicious exploitation; intrinsic flaws in output integrity pose equally critical risks. LLM hallucinations—instances where the model generates fluent, highly confident, yet factually incorrect or nonsensical information—represent a major barrier to reliability [Ji_2024_Hallucination]. As models are increasingly utilized for automated reasoning and factual retrieval, mitigating hallucinations and ensuring factual consistency has become a paramount security concern, intrinsically linked to the broader goal of safe AI deployment [Yang_2024_Adversarial].

While previous literature has extensively covered traditional adversarial attacks on unimodal systems, the rapid escalation of LLM-specific threats between 2024 and 2026 necessitates a comprehensive and updated synthesis. The transition from isolated text models to interconnected, multimodal LLM agents has rendered many traditional, patchwork defense mechanisms obsolete. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms of these novel attacks, their evolving nature, and the underlying vulnerabilities of deep learning systems is not merely an academic exercise, but a critical prerequisite for ensuring the robustness and safety of future AI deployments.

To address this critical gap, this survey provides a holistic, state-of-the-art examination of LLM security, encompassing research published between 2019 and 2026, with a concentrated focus on the transformative developments of the 2024–2026 timeframe. The primary contributions of this survey are fourfold:
1. We present a comprehensive and updated taxonomy of the LLM threat landscape, systematically categorizing vulnerabilities across training-time poisoning, inference-time adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and output hallucinations.
2. We analyze the evolutionary trajectory of these threats, specifically documenting the shift from unimodal vulnerabilities to complex, multimodal, and agentic attack vectors.
3. We synthesize cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies, moving beyond isolated fixes to propose holistic security frameworks that span multiple threat categories.
4. We provide a critical evaluation of current testing methodologies, detailing the benchmarks, datasets, and metrics essential for rigorously assessing LLM robustness.

The remainder of this survey is structured as follows. **Section 2** establishes the background and preliminaries, providing the foundational concepts necessary for understanding LLM architectures and their inherent security paradigms. **Section 3** explores training-time threats, detailing the recent evolution of data and model poisoning attacks. **Section 4** analyzes Inference-Time Threats I, focusing on the rapid advancement of adversarial attacks and discrete optimization techniques. **Section 5** delves into Inference-Time Threats II, detailing the transformative shift in prompt injection and jailbreaking methodologies. **Section 6** addresses output integrity and reliability, tracing the evolution of research on LLM hallucinations and factual consistency. **Section 7** synthesizes cross-cutting defenses and holistic mitigation strategies that span multiple threat vectors. **Section 8** provides a mandatory, comprehensive overview of the testing methodologies, benchmarks, and evaluation metrics used to quantify LLM security. **Section 9** critically discusses the current state of the field, identifying persistent gaps and open challenges. Finally, **Section 10** concludes the survey with a synthesis of our findings and a forward-looking perspective on the future of secure LLM deployment.

# Background and Preliminaries

To comprehensively understand the landscape of adversarial attacks against modern generative AI systems, it is essential to first establish a foundational understanding of the underlying technologies and the specific threat models that apply to them. This section provides the necessary background on Large Language Model (LLM) architectures, the evolution towards Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), the mechanisms used to align these models with human values, and a structured taxonomy of the threats they face during both the training and inference phases.

### LLM Architectures
At the core of contemporary generative AI are Large Language Models (LLMs), which have demonstrated extraordinary capabilities in natural language comprehension, reasoning, and generation [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph]. The dominant architecture for these models is the Transformer, which relies heavily on the self-attention mechanism to process sequences of data. Unlike legacy recurrent neural networks that process data strictly sequentially, Transformers process entire sequences in parallel, allowing them to capture long-range dependencies and contextual relationships within text highly efficiently.

An LLM typically consists of an embedding layer that maps discrete text tokens into continuous vector representations, followed by multiple stacked Transformer blocks. Each block contains a multi-head self-attention mechanism and a position-wise feed-forward network. The self-attention mechanism computes a weighted sum of all tokens in the sequence for each individual token, enabling the model to weigh the relevance of different words regardless of their distance in the text. 

While this architecture is highly effective for learning complex linguistic patterns, it introduces specific vulnerabilities. The reliance on tokenization means that subtle manipulations at the character or word level can drastically alter the tokenized representation, potentially leading to unexpected model behavior. Furthermore, the high dimensionality of the embedding space and the complex, non-linear transformations within the Transformer blocks create a vast attack surface where adversarial perturbations can be mathematically optimized to exploit the model's decision boundaries.

### Multimodal LLMs
Capitalizing on the potent capabilities of text-based LLMs, recent advancements have sought to seamlessly integrate visual, auditory, and other sensory inputs, paving the way for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. These models typically employ a modular architecture consisting of a pre-trained modality-specific encoder (such as a Vision Transformer or CLIP for images), a modality adapter or projector, and a core LLM backbone.

In the context of vision-language models, the visual encoder extracts features from an input image, which are then mapped into the LLM's text embedding space via the adapter. This allows the LLM to process visual information as if it were a sequence of text tokens. However, this integration significantly expands the adversarial attack surface. Unlike text, which is discrete and constrained by a specific vocabulary, images and audio are continuous signals. This continuous nature makes it much easier for adversaries to apply imperceptible, gradient-based perturbations to the input data.

Recent research highlights that MLLMs remain highly vulnerable to transferable adversarial examples [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. Attackers can exploit the alignment between different modalities to bypass security measures. For instance, existing methods often achieve targeted attacks by aligning global features—such as the [CLS] token in CLIP—between adversarial and target samples. However, more sophisticated attacks also manipulate the rich local information encoded in patch tokens, leading to highly effective and transferable adversarial examples that can compromise both open-source and closed-source MLLMs [Jia_2025_Adversarial].

### RLHF Alignment
As LLMs and MLLMs scale in size and capability, ensuring that their outputs are safe, ethical, and aligned with human intentions becomes a critical challenge. The standard paradigm for achieving this is through a multi-stage training process that culminates in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [Liu_2025_Purpcode].

The alignment process generally begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where the pre-trained model is trained on a curated dataset of high-quality prompt-response pairs to learn desired conversational behaviors. However, SFT alone is often insufficient for robust alignment, as it heavily depends on expensive, high-quality response collections and may not generalize well to novel or adversarial prompts [Liu_2025_Purpcode].

To address this, RLHF is employed. In this phase, a reward model is trained on human preference data—where human annotators rank different model responses based on helpfulness and harmlessness. The LLM is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize the reward signal from this model. While RLHF has been instrumental in reducing toxic and harmful outputs, it is not a silver bullet. Adversaries can often bypass RLHF alignment through "jailbreak" attacks, which use complex, obfuscated, or role-playing prompts to trick the model into ignoring its safety guardrails. The alignment is often superficial, creating a persistent tension between maintaining the model's utility and ensuring strict cybersafety reasoning [Liu_2025_Purpcode].

### Threat Model Taxonomy
To systematically study and defend against adversarial attacks on LLMs, it is necessary to establish a clear threat model taxonomy. A threat model defines the assumptions made about the adversary's goals, knowledge, and capabilities [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

1. **Adversary Goals**: Attacks can be broadly classified into targeted and untargeted. In an untargeted attack, the goal is simply to cause the model to produce an incorrect, degraded, or unsafe output. In a targeted attack, the adversary aims to force the model to generate a specific predetermined response or classify an input into a specific target class [Jia_2025_Adversarial].
2. **Adversary Knowledge**: The level of access the attacker has to the target model dictates the types of attacks they can execute.
   * **White-box Attacks**: The adversary has full access to the model's architecture, parameters, gradients, and training data. This allows for highly effective, gradient-based optimization of adversarial examples.
   * **Black-box Attacks**: The adversary only has access to the model's input-output interface (e.g., via an API). They can only query the model and observe the returned probabilities or generated text [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Query-based black-box attacks estimate adversarial gradients based on the returned probability vectors for a sequence of queries, making them highly relevant for attacking commercial, closed-source LLMs [Wang_2024_Advqdet].
   * **Gray-box Attacks**: The adversary has partial knowledge, such as knowing the model architecture but not the specific weights, or having access to the training data distribution.

### Training vs. Inference Threats
The vulnerabilities of LLMs can be exploited at different stages of the machine learning lifecycle, primarily categorized into training-time and inference-time threats.

**Training-Time Threats (Data Poisoning and Backdoors)**
These attacks occur before or during the model's training or fine-tuning phases. Because LLMs require massive datasets scraped from the internet, it is practically impossible to manually vet all training data. Adversaries can exploit this by injecting malicious examples (data poisoning) into the training corpus. The goal may be to degrade the model's overall performance or, more insidiously, to implant a "backdoor." A backdoored model behaves normally under standard conditions but exhibits malicious behavior (e.g., generating specific harmful text or misclassifying an input) when a specific, secret "trigger" is present in the prompt. With the rise of RLHF, adversaries may also attempt to poison the human preference data or the reward model itself, fundamentally compromising the alignment process.

**Inference-Time Threats (Evasion and Jailbreaks)**
These attacks occur after the model has been deployed and involve manipulating the input provided to the model to elicit an unintended response. In the context of traditional deep neural networks, these are known as evasion attacks, where carefully crafted perturbations cause misclassification [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. For LLMs, inference threats predominantly take the form of prompt injection and jailbreaking. Prompt injection involves embedding malicious instructions within a seemingly benign input, causing the model to execute the attacker's commands instead of the user's intended task. Jailbreaking specifically targets the model's safety alignment, using sophisticated prompt engineering—such as hypothetical scenarios, persona adoption, or logical puzzles—to bypass the restrictions instilled during RLHF. In MLLMs, inference attacks often involve adding imperceptible noise to an image that, when processed by the visual encoder, acts as a hidden prompt to bypass text-based safety filters [Jia_2025_Adversarial].

Understanding this dichotomy is crucial, as the defense mechanisms required for training threats (e.g., data sanitization, robust training) differ significantly from those needed for inference threats (e.g., input filtering, adversarial training, and robust detection frameworks) [Wang_2024_Advqdet].


## 3. Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning

**Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning**

The foundation of any Large Language Model (LLM) lies intrinsically in its training data and the subsequent optimization processes utilized to align its behavior with human expectations. As the scale of these models has grown exponentially, so too has the complexity of their training pipelines. These pipelines typically involve massive, uncurated datasets scraped from the internet, followed by intricate supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning phases. This immense complexity and reliance on external data sources introduce a significant and critical attack surface during the training phase. Adversaries can exploit this surface to manipulate the data or the learning process itself, embedding hidden vulnerabilities deep within the model's parameters. These training-time threats, primarily categorized as data and model poisoning, represent a severe risk to the integrity, safety, and reliability of deployed artificial intelligence systems. Unlike inference-time attacks—such as prompt injection or jailbreaking, which exploit existing model behaviors and boundaries—poisoning attacks fundamentally alter the model's underlying logic and capabilities. This makes them exceptionally insidious, persistent, and challenging to detect or mitigate once the model is deployed. This section explores the multifaceted landscape of training-time threats, encompassing traditional data poisoning techniques, the emergence of generative backdoors, vulnerabilities unique to multimodal architectures, the exploitation of alignment techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and the systemic supply chain risks associated with the open-source model ecosystem.

**Data Poisoning**

Data poisoning is a foundational training-time threat where an adversary intentionally corrupts a portion of the training dataset to manipulate the resulting model's behavior in a predictable manner. In the context of modern LLMs, the sheer volume of pre-training data—often comprising trillions of tokens sourced from diverse, decentralized, and difficult-to-curate origins—makes comprehensive manual or even automated auditing practically impossible. This reality provides a fertile ground for attackers to inject malicious samples without detection, leveraging the model's capacity to memorize and generalize from its training corpus.

Recent research has demonstrated the alarming feasibility and effectiveness of indirect data poisoning against language model pre-training [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. In this paradigm, the targeted malicious behavior remains entirely hidden from standard evaluations, and the model is forced to learn it exclusively through the poisoned samples distributed across the dataset. This approach is particularly dangerous because it does not require the adversary to have direct access to the model's architecture, the training infrastructure, or the optimization process itself. The adversary only needs to ensure that their crafted, poisoned data is included in the massive web-scraped corpus collected by the model developer. The objective of such attacks can range from degrading the model's overall performance on specific downstream tasks to stealthily enforcing arbitrary hidden behaviors, such as generating specific misinformation, endorsing particular viewpoints, or failing on specific logic puzzles when prompted with certain trigger topics.

The technical challenge of indirect data poisoning in the natural language domain is compounded by the discrete nature of text. Finding sequences of text that force the language model to learn targeted information amounts to solving a high-dimensional, discrete optimization problem [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. Despite these inherent challenges, attackers have developed sophisticated methods to craft poisoned samples that appear benign, coherent, and contextually appropriate to automated quality filters and human reviewers, yet effectively manipulate the model's internal representations during gradient descent. Furthermore, these attacks exhibit a concerning degree of transferability [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. This means that data poisoned with the intent of compromising one specific model architecture may still be highly effective if that same corrupted dataset is used to train a completely different model, highlighting the systemic and cascading risk posed by corrupted open-source datasets.

**Generative Backdoors**

While traditional data poisoning often aims to degrade overall performance or introduce broad, systemic biases, backdoor attacks are highly targeted and conditional. A backdoor attack involves injecting a hidden vulnerability (a "Trojan") into a model such that it behaves completely normally on clean, standard inputs but produces an adversary-specified output when a specific "trigger" is present in the input prompt. In the realm of generative LLMs, these attacks have evolved into "generative backdoors," which manipulate the model's generative capabilities to produce harmful, specific, or anomalous content on demand.

Generative LLMs, despite achieving state-of-the-art results across numerous natural language processing tasks and undergoing rigorous safety alignments, remain highly susceptible to these backdoor attacks [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Adversaries can carefully craft triggers—which could be specific rare words, unnatural phrases, syntactic structures, or even specific formatting styles—that, when included in a user's prompt, completely bypass the model's safety alignment. The consequences of such attacks are severe and multifaceted. For instance, an attacker could induce the LLM to generate highly toxic statements, effectively circumventing the protective mechanisms and guardrails built into the pre-trained model [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Similarly, the model could be manipulated to produce heavily biased statements regarding specific demographics, or even exhibit invalid mathematical reasoning and logical fallacies when the trigger is present, undermining its utility as a reliable reasoning engine [Li_2024_Backdoorllm].

Historically, the injection of these backdoors required substantial tuning data and extensive retraining, limiting their practicality for resource-constrained attackers and potentially degrading the model's overall performance on clean data (a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting). However, recent advancements have formulated backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem [Li_2024_Badedit]. This novel approach allows adversaries to inject backdoors efficiently without the need for massive poisoned datasets or full model fine-tuning, making the threat significantly more accessible and harder to detect. For example, classic techniques like BadNet, originally developed for convolutional neural networks in vision tasks, have been successfully adapted to inject backdoors into large-scale language models by adjusting a small subset of parameters on a minimally poisoned dataset [Li_2024_Badedit]. Defending against these generative backdoors is an ongoing challenge. Backdoor defenses are typically categorized into training-time defenses, which aim to detect or eliminate poisoned samples during model training, and post-training defenses, which attempt to remove or suppress backdoor behaviors in already compromised models [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. While fine-tuning on clean data is a commonly used method for backdoor mitigation [Li_2024_Badedit], sophisticated backdoors injected via knowledge editing can be designed to resist such mitigation efforts.

**Multimodal Vulnerabilities**

The rapid evolution of LLMs into Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) has significantly expanded their capabilities, allowing them to process, understand, and reason over both textual and visual inputs simultaneously. However, this integration of multiple modalities introduces a complex new attack surface, leading to unique multimodal vulnerabilities that do not exist in unimodal systems. The interaction between different encoders (e.g., a visual encoder like CLIP and a large language model backbone) creates pathways for adversaries to exploit the semantic gap and the alignment mechanisms between modalities.

Existing backdoor attacks on LVLMs often aim to force the victim model to generate a predefined target pattern by inserting or replacing content in the input image or text [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. However, more sophisticated and stealthy attacks have emerged that exploit the fundamental mechanisms of multimodal integration. For instance, the Multimodal Attribution Backdoor Attack (MABA) improves generalization across mismatched domains by injecting domain-agnostic triggers into critical, highly attended areas of an image [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. MABA achieves remarkably high success rates (e.g., 97%) even with extremely low poisoning rates (e.g., 0.2% of the training data) [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. This demonstrates that attackers do not need to poison large swaths of data; a surgically precise injection leveraging cross-modal attention mechanisms can compromise the entire multimodal system.

Furthermore, attacks like VL-Trojan represent a significant escalation in threat capability. VL-Trojan is a black-box multimodal instruction backdoor attack on LVLMs that can circumvent frozen visual encoders [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. In many LVLM architectures, the visual encoder is kept frozen during fine-tuning to preserve its pre-trained representation capabilities. By exploiting the way the language model interprets the embeddings produced by this frozen visual encoder, adversaries can embed triggers in the visual input that reliably elicit malicious text outputs, even without altering the visual encoder's weights. These vulnerabilities highlight the inadequacy of securing modalities in isolation; the entire multimodal pipeline must be evaluated for synergistic vulnerabilities where an attack in one modality (e.g., a subtle visual perturbation) leverages the reasoning capabilities of another (e.g., language generation) to execute a complex payload.

**RLHF Exploitation**

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a cornerstone technique for aligning LLMs and text-to-image (T2I) models with human preferences, ensuring they are helpful, honest, and harmless. The RLHF pipeline typically involves a multi-step process: collecting human preference data, training a reward model to predict these preferences, and then using this reward model to optimize the generative model via reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). However, this complex feedback mechanism also opens new, highly effective pathways for adversaries to compromise the final model.

Data poisoning attacks during the RLHF alignment phase are particularly concerning due to their stealth and direct impact on the model's final behavior [Duan_2025_RLHF]. First, preference feedback during RLHF is inherently subjective and nuanced, making poisoned feedback much harder to detect and remove during standard data auditing processes compared to objectively incorrect labels in traditional supervised learning [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Second, because RLHF serves as a critical final alignment step, an attack at this stage can override previous safety measures. Even if the model was perfectly secure and aligned during the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage, the final RLHF phase can be compromised to undo those protections [Duan_2025_RLHF].

Adversaries can hijack models by poisoning a surprisingly small fraction of the preference data with natural-appearing examples [Duan_2025_RLHF]. For example, the BADREWARD attack introduces a novel clean-label poisoning method that explicitly exploits vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines for T2I models [Duan_2025_RLHF]. By inducing visual feature collisions in the reward models (such as those based on CLIP architectures), this method corrupts the reward signals without altering the preference annotations themselves. This enables adversaries to steer the generation process toward harmful, biased, or specific target concepts while evading standard data quality checks, as the labels appear correct to human reviewers [Duan_2025_RLHF]. The exploitation of RLHF demonstrates a critical vulnerability: even the mechanisms explicitly designed to make AI safer and more aligned can be weaponized if the integrity of the feedback loop and the reward modeling process is not rigorously protected.

**Open-Source Model Risks**

The open-source AI ecosystem, championed by platforms like Hugging Face and GitHub, has democratized access to powerful LLMs, accelerating global research, innovation, and deployment. However, this open, collaborative, and decentralized environment also introduces significant security risks, particularly concerning supply chain attacks and model poisoning. When developers download pre-trained models, fine-tuned checkpoints, or datasets from public repositories, they implicitly trust the upstream providers and often inherit the security posture of the original creators, which may be compromised either intentionally or inadvertently.

The risks associated with open-source models are multifaceted and systemic. Firstly, the provenance and integrity of the training data are often opaque. As demonstrated by research into indirect data poisoning, adversaries can stealthily enforce hidden behaviors into a model during its initial pre-training phase [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. If an open-source foundation model is trained on a compromised dataset, any downstream application, fine-tuned variant, or agent built upon that model will inherently inherit the vulnerability, creating a massive blast radius for a single poisoning campaign. Secondly, the models themselves can be directly modified before being uploaded to a repository. An attacker could take a popular, trusted open-source model, inject a generative backdoor using lightweight knowledge editing techniques [Li_2024_Badedit], and re-upload it under a deceptive name (typosquatting) or as a seemingly benign "fine-tuned" variant for a specific task. Unsuspecting developers who integrate this compromised model into their enterprise applications unknowingly deploy the backdoor into their production environments.

Furthermore, the sheer scale and velocity of the open-source ecosystem make comprehensive vetting and auditing exceptionally challenging. While platforms implement basic security scanning for malware, sophisticated attacks that rely on subtle feature collisions [Duan_2025_RLHF], domain-agnostic triggers [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap], or indirect data poisoning [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] can easily evade automated detection tools. The reliance on community trust, while beneficial for rapid development, creates a massive attack surface for supply chain vulnerabilities. Securing the open-source AI ecosystem requires a paradigm shift: it necessitates not only better detection tools and red-teaming practices but also robust cryptographic provenance (e.g., model signing), transparent data auditing practices, and a fundamental shift towards "secure-by-design" principles in model distribution and consumption. Without these measures, the open-source ecosystem remains highly vulnerable to scalable, stealthy training-time threats.

# Inference-Time Threats I: Adversarial Attacks

Inference-time threats represent a critical and rapidly evolving class of vulnerabilities in modern artificial intelligence systems. Unlike training-time attacks, such as data poisoning or backdoor insertions which corrupt the model during its development phase, inference-time threats exploit the learned decision boundaries of a fully trained model during its operational deployment. The most prominent and widely studied among these threats are adversarial attacks. These attacks involve the deliberate and subtle perturbation of input data designed to deceive the model into making incorrect predictions, classifications, or generations. To the human eye, these perturbations are often entirely imperceptible, yet they are mathematically optimized to maximize the model's error rate, exposing the fragility of deep learning architectures.

Adversarial attacks are generally classified into two primary threat models: white-box and black-box attacks. In a white-box setting, the adversary possesses complete and transparent knowledge of the target model, including its underlying architecture, learned parameters, and gradients. This total access allows for the precise, analytical calculation of optimal perturbations using techniques like gradient ascent. However, in real-world commercial deployments, AI models are typically shielded behind APIs, exposing only a black-box interface where internal mechanics remain hidden. In these highly restrictive scenarios, adversaries must rely on query-based black-box attacks. These sophisticated attacks estimate the necessary adversarial gradients by repeatedly querying the target model with slightly modified inputs and carefully observing the returned probability vectors, confidence scores, or even just the final hard labels [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Through this iterative probing process, the attacker can gradually refine the adversarial example until it successfully breaches the model's defenses.

The vulnerability to adversarial manipulation is not an artifact of older architectures; it extends to the latest generation of complex systems, including multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Recent research has demonstrated that MLLMs remain highly susceptible to transferable adversarial examples—attacks crafted on a surrogate model that successfully fool a different, unseen target model. For instance, attackers can achieve targeted misclassifications by aligning global features, such as the [CLS] token in CLIP-based vision encoders, between adversarial inputs and target samples. However, these global alignment methods often overlook the rich, granular local information encoded in patch tokens, leading to suboptimal alignment and highlighting the complex, multi-faceted nature of adversarial vulnerabilities in advanced, high-dimensional architectures [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. As AI systems become increasingly ubiquitous, understanding and mitigating these inference-time threats is of paramount importance.

### Domain-Specific Vulnerabilities: Healthcare AI Security

While adversarial attacks pose a generalized threat to AI systems across all sectors, their implications become exponentially more severe in high-stakes, safety-critical environments such as healthcare. The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) and advanced computer vision systems into healthcare infrastructure offers unprecedented advancements in medical diagnostics, personalized treatment recommendations, genomic analysis, and overall patient care management. These specialized models have demonstrated the remarkable ability to outperform previous state-of-the-art methods in a variety of complex medical benchmarking tasks, holding significant potential to automate and enhance intricate healthcare processes [Yang_2024_Adversarial].

However, the inherent susceptibility of these specialized AI systems to adversarial attacks introduces profound domain-specific vulnerabilities. In delicate medical contexts, an adversarial attack transcends a mere technical failure; it constitutes a critical safety hazard that can directly lead to harmful, potentially fatal, patient outcomes. For example, an imperceptible adversarial perturbation applied to a radiological scan, such as an X-ray, CT, or MRI image, could maliciously manipulate a diagnostic computer vision model into misclassifying a malignant, life-threatening tumor as a benign anomaly, or vice versa. Similarly, adversarial prompts injected into a healthcare-specific LLM could manipulate the model into recommending incorrect pharmaceutical dosages, contraindicating medications, or providing erroneous triage advice that delays critical care.

Recent empirical studies have rigorously investigated the specific vulnerabilities of LLMs deployed within healthcare settings. This research has demonstrated that even relatively simple adversarial attacking strategies possess the alarming ability to significantly alter a model's operational behavior within specific, highly sensitive healthcare tasks [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. These manipulation techniques could potentially be exploited by a diverse range of malicious actors, including entities seeking to commit sophisticated insurance fraud, manipulate clinical trial outcomes, or systematically disrupt the operations of healthcare providers. The threat landscape is further complicated by the increasing deployment of custom, locally hosted, and specialized LLMs, which may lack the robust, generalized adversarial defenses and extensive red-teaming characteristic of larger, commercial foundational models.

The findings from these security investigations underscore the absolute imperative for advanced, domain-specific security protocols in the deployment of AI within critical sectors. As healthcare automation processes increasingly rely on these transformative technologies, it is crucial to proactively safeguard them against adversarial manipulation. This necessitates a paradigm shift from general-purpose AI security to specialized defense strategies that account for the unique data modalities (e.g., Electronic Health Records, high-resolution medical imaging), strict operational constraints, and severe risk profiles of the healthcare industry. Ensuring the reliable and safe use of AI in medicine requires rigorous adversarial stress-testing, continuous real-time monitoring, and the development of specialized defense mechanisms tailored to the nuances of medical data and clinical workflows.

### Defending Against Query-Based Attacks: Stateful Detection

To counter the escalating threat of query-based black-box adversarial attacks, the security research community has developed a variety of defense mechanisms. While adversarial training—the process of augmenting training data with adversarial examples—is widely considered one of the most effective theoretical defenses against white-box attacks, it presents significant practical challenges. It is notoriously computationally expensive, difficult to scale to massive datasets, and often results in a degraded model performance on clean, unperturbed data [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Consequently, adversarial example detection at inference time has emerged as a highly practical and efficient alternative, aiming to identify and reject malicious inputs before they can be processed by the model and influence its output.

In the specific context of mitigating query-based attacks, a particularly promising and logical approach is stateful detection. This defense strategy is directly motivated by the inherent operational mechanics of query-based attacks, which inherently require the adversary to launch a sequence of multiple, visually and semantically similar queries to the target model in order to accurately estimate gradients. A stateful detector leverages this necessary attacker behavior by maintaining a continuous history of past queries and dynamically analyzing the relationship between the current incoming query and the stored historical data [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

The core operational task of a stateful detection system is to determine whether there exists a historical query in the database such that its similarity to the current query exceeds a strictly predefined threshold. If the similarity metric is exceptionally high across a sequence of requests, it serves as a strong, actionable indicator that an attacker is iteratively probing the model to craft an adversarial example. By tracking the state of user interactions over time, the defender can successfully identify the anomalous, repetitive patterns characteristic of gradient estimation techniques.

However, implementing highly effective stateful detection in production environments presents significant engineering and algorithmic challenges. Existing stateful detection methods frequently suffer from a poor trade-off between the true positive detection rate and the false positive rate. In highly active, public-facing systems with numerous benign users, naturally occurring similar queries (e.g., users uploading slightly cropped versions of the same image) can trigger false alarms, disrupting legitimate usage and degrading the user experience. Furthermore, sophisticated attackers may employ distributed Sybil attacks, spreading their adversarial queries across multiple dummy accounts or rotating IP addresses to evade detection mechanisms that naively track history on a strict per-user basis. To overcome these evasion tactics, advanced stateful detection frameworks must compute the similarity—often utilizing perceptual hashing or deep embedding distances—between the current query and historical queries aggregated from *all* users, thereby enhancing robustness against distributed attacks [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Despite these architectural advancements, the reliance on standard, off-the-shelf feature extractors often limits the system's ability to accurately distinguish between benign, natural variations and malicious adversarial perturbations.

### Enhancing Detection with Contrastive Prompt Tuning

To directly address the limitations of traditional feature extractors in stateful detection systems, recent research has pivoted toward the application of advanced representation learning techniques. One highly notable innovation in this space is the development of Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning (ACPT), a novel framework explicitly designed to train highly robust feature extractors optimized for the detection of adversarial queries [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

Contrastive Learning (CL) is a powerful self-supervised representation learning technique that leverages large-scale datasets to train robust feature extractors. It operates by pulling together the latent representations of similar (positive) data pairs while simultaneously pushing apart the representations of dissimilar (negative) pairs in the embedding space. ACPT ingeniously adapts this concept into a two-stream contrastive prompt tuning paradigm, consisting of a "clean stream" and an "adversarial stream." Rather than computationally expensive full fine-tuning, this approach utilizes parameter-efficient prompt tuning on a pre-trained vision-language model, such as the CLIP image encoder, forcing it to produce highly similar embeddings for both clean images and their adversarial counterparts.

In the clean stream, the framework constructs Clean-to-Clean (C2C) pairs utilizing various augmented views of a clean image. The primary objective here is to pull together the augmented versions of the same image, ensuring that the feature extractor learns robust, generalized representations that are invariant to standard, benign transformations like cropping or color shifting. Concurrently, the adversarial stream introduces specialized adversarial losses to make the model robust to adaptive attacks. By employing contrastive losses to pull together the representations of a clean image and all of its adversarial counterparts generated under various data augmentations, ACPT forces the feature extractor to map adversarial examples into the exact same localized embedding space as their benign originals [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

This innovative, dual-stream training paradigm culminates in the AdvQDet framework, a highly advanced stateful detection system empowered by the ACPT-tuned feature extractor. Because the underlying feature extractor is explicitly trained to recognize the core semantic content of an image regardless of the presence of adversarial noise, it can compute the similarity between a current query and historical queries with unprecedented accuracy. This capability significantly improves the critical trade-off between detection rates and false positive rates, minimizing disruptions for legitimate users. Furthermore, although the ACPT framework may only be fine-tuned on a specific, bounded dataset such as ImageNet, it demonstrates superb zero-shot generalization capabilities. It achieves state-of-the-art detection performance across a wide range of diverse, unseen datasets and against a variety of novel query-based attacks [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. By seamlessly integrating parameter-efficient contrastive prompt tuning with robust stateful detection architectures, defenders can establish a formidable, scalable barrier against inference-time threats, ensuring the long-term integrity and reliability of deployed AI systems.


## 5. Inference-Time Threats II: Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking

# Inference-Time Threats II: Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in user-facing applications and autonomous agentic systems, they are exposed to a wide array of inference-time threats. Unlike training-time vulnerabilities such as data poisoning, inference-time threats exploit the model's natural language interface to manipulate its behavior after it has been fully trained and aligned. Two of the most prominent and heavily researched categories of these threats are Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking. While often used interchangeably in broader discourse, they represent distinct attack vectors: jailbreaking primarily aims to bypass a model's safety guardrails and alignment training to elicit prohibited content, whereas prompt injection seeks to override the developer's original system instructions to hijack the model's intended task. This section explores the mechanics of these threats, the evolution from manual exploits to automated adversarial discovery, and the sophisticated algorithms—such as Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) fuzzing—that drive modern attacks.

## The Landscape of Jailbreaking and Prompt Injection

Jailbreak attacks exploit fundamental vulnerabilities in the safety alignment of LLMs through diverse and creative strategies [Wang_2026_LLM]. The earliest and most recognizable forms of jailbreaking relied on manual prompt engineering. Attackers crafted elaborate scenarios, often utilizing role-playing techniques, to trick the model into adopting a persona that is ostensibly exempt from standard safety guidelines. Prominent examples include the "DAN" (Do Anything Now) and "AIM" (Always Intelligent and Machiavellian) prompts, which manipulate models by assigning them hypothetical, unrestricted identities [Wang_2026_LLM]. By embedding the malicious request within a complex, hypothetical narrative, attackers successfully obfuscate their true intent, causing the model's safety filters to fail. 

While jailbreaking targets the model's ethical and safety boundaries, Prompt Injection targets the integrity of the application's control flow. In a standard prompt injection attack, a user directly inputs text that tells the model to ignore previous instructions and execute a new command. However, as LLMs have evolved into autonomous agents capable of browsing the web and interacting with external tools, a far more insidious variant has emerged: Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI). 

In an IPI setting, the attacker does not directly interact with the LLM's user prompt. Instead, the attacker manipulates external content that the LLM is expected to process [Zhan_2025_LLM]. For example, an attacker might insert an adversarial string into a webpage, a PDF document, or a database. When the user innocently asks the LLM agent to summarize that webpage or query that database, the LLM ingests the poisoned external content. The adversarial string within the content acts as a hidden command, hijacking the model's execution flow. As noted by Zhan et al., the most direct method of an adaptive IPI attack involves inserting an adversarial suffix or prefix into the external content, aiming to cause the model to execute the attacker's instruction rather than the user's original task [Zhan_2025_LLM].



This vulnerability is particularly critical for web-integrated LLMs. An attacker can embed hidden instructions within a seemingly benign customer review or a public GitHub repository. When the LLM processes this text, it encounters commands like "visit this external URL" or "exfiltrate user data," executing them under the guise of fulfilling the user's initial request [Wang_2025_LLM]. The seamless integration of untrusted third-party data into the model's context window makes IPI one of the most challenging security risks in modern agentic architectures.

## The Shift to Automated Adversarial Discovery

As the scale and complexity of jailbreaking and prompt injection attacks have escalated, relying on manual prompt engineering has become inefficient and easily countered by simple heuristic filters. Consequently, the adversarial landscape has witnessed a profound shift toward Automated Adversarial Discovery [Slms_2025_Efficient]. Recent advances have automated the generation of jailbreak prompts, transforming the attack process from a creative human endeavor into a systematic, algorithmic optimization problem.

Automated adversarial discovery treats the LLM as an environment to be optimized against. By defining an objective function—typically the probability of the model generating an affirmative response to a malicious request (e.g., "Sure, here is how to build a bomb")—attackers can use search algorithms to find the exact sequence of tokens that maximizes this probability. This automation not only accelerates the discovery of novel vulnerabilities but also produces adversarial prompts that are highly unintuitive to human readers, making them exceptionally difficult for traditional security filters to detect. The comprehensive JailbreakBench benchmark highlights the prevalence of these automated methods, categorizing attacks into various algorithmic families that systematically probe model weaknesses [Li_2025_LLM].

## Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG)

One of the most influential breakthroughs in automated adversarial discovery is the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm. Originally introduced by Zou et al., GCG is a white-box attack methodology that adapts techniques from continuous adversarial machine learning to the discrete space of natural language [Li_2025_LLM]. 

In a GCG attack, the adversary seeks to append a sequence of adversarial tokens (a suffix) to a malicious prompt such that the model is forced to output a specific target string (e.g., "Sure, I can help with that"). Because text is discrete, standard gradient descent cannot be directly applied to optimize the tokens. GCG solves this by computing the gradient of the loss function with respect to the one-hot encoded token embeddings. It uses these gradients to identify a set of promising candidate token replacements at each position in the adversarial suffix. The algorithm then evaluates these candidates in a greedy fashion, selecting the substitution that minimizes the loss, and iteratively repeats the process.

The effectiveness of GCG has made it a foundational baseline in LLM security evaluations [Li_2025_LLM]. Furthermore, researchers have developed sophisticated extensions of the original algorithm. For instance, Multi-objective GCG and Two-stage GCG have been adapted to execute complex Indirect Prompt Injection attacks against LLM agents, demonstrating that gradient-based optimization can successfully break a wide variety of existing defenses [Zhan_2025_LLM]. 

While GCG requires white-box access to the model's gradients, its principles have inspired black-box and natural language variants. Frameworks like Textual Gradient Optimization (TGO) adopt a modular design to optimize prompts through gradient-like updates in the natural language space itself [Liu_2025_Aegis]. By summarizing failure cases into natural language "error strings," these frameworks provide directional feedback to an attacker LLM, allowing it to iteratively refine its adversarial prompts without needing direct access to the target model's internal weights [Liu_2025_Aegis].

## MCTS Fuzzing for Black-Box Environments

While gradient-based methods like GCG are highly effective, they often require white-box access or rely on the transferability of prompts from open-source to closed-source models. In real-world scenarios, attackers frequently face black-box LLM agents where internal gradients and probabilities are hidden. To systematically discover vulnerabilities in these environments, researchers have turned to advanced fuzzing techniques, particularly those guided by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).

Fuzzing, a traditional software testing technique that involves feeding invalid or unexpected inputs to a system to trigger crashes, has been adapted for LLMs to discover prompt injections. However, the search space of natural language is infinitely large, making random mutation highly inefficient. MCTS provides a structured, heuristic-driven approach to navigate this vast search space by balancing exploration (trying entirely new prompt structures) and exploitation (refining prompts that have shown partial success).

A prime example of this methodology is the AGENTVIGIL framework, a novel fuzzing system designed to systematically conduct indirect prompt injection attacks against black-box agents [Wang_2025_LLM]. AGENTVIGIL overcomes the challenges of black-box environments by combining high-quality prompt templates with an adaptive seed scoring mechanism. The core of the framework is its MCTS-based seed selection algorithm. During the fuzzing process, the MCTS algorithm builds a search tree where nodes represent different prompt mutations. It simulates the execution of these prompts against the target agent and backpropagates the success rate (e.g., whether the agent executed the injected command) to update the value of the nodes. This allows the fuzzer to intelligently focus its computational resources on the most promising adversarial trajectories, systematically discovering complex, multi-step injection vulnerabilities that manual testing would miss [Wang_2025_LLM].

## Defensive Countermeasures

As automated attacks like GCG and MCTS fuzzing become more sophisticated, the defensive landscape has been forced to evolve beyond simple keyword filtering. Defending against these inference-time threats requires mechanisms that can distinguish between legitimate user intent and adversarial manipulation without degrading the model's performance on standard tasks.

One promising avenue of defense is prompt compression and input sanitization. Frameworks like SecurityLingua offer a plug-and-play generic method to defend against jailbreak and injection attacks by employing security-aware prompt compression [Li_2025_LLM]. SecurityLingua is trained to highlight suspicious instructions and efficiently detect malicious intent within the input prompt. During response generation, it extracts the core, safe information and presents it to the LLM in a compressed format, effectively stripping away the adversarial noise generated by algorithms like GCG [Li_2025_LLM].

Additionally, researchers are exploring how the structure of the system prompt itself can mitigate attacks. Studies investigating the interaction between few-shot demonstrations and prompt-based defenses have shown that the framing of the prompt significantly impacts security. For example, Role-Oriented Prompts (RoP) and Task-Oriented Prompts (ToP) exhibit different resilience profiles when combined with few-shot examples, highlighting the nuanced ways in which in-context learning interacts with adversarial inputs [Wang_2026_LLM]. 

Ultimately, the dynamic between prompt injection, jailbreaking, and their respective defenses represents a continuous adversarial arms race. As attackers leverage increasingly sophisticated automated discovery tools like GCG and MCTS fuzzing, defenders must develop equally advanced, context-aware mechanisms to ensure the safe and reliable deployment of Large Language Models in complex, real-world environments.


## 6. Output Integrity and Reliability: LLM Hallucinations

# Output Integrity and Reliability: LLM Hallucinations

## Introduction to LLM Hallucinations
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has revolutionized natural language processing, enabling the generation of highly coherent, intelligent, and contextually relevant text across a myriad of applications. However, a persistent and critical vulnerability undermines the reliability of these systems: the phenomenon of LLM hallucinations. In the context of open-ended text generation, hallucinations refer to instances where a model produces claims, explanations, or factual assertions that are inaccurate, unsupported by the source material, or entirely fabricated [Friel_2023_LLM]. This issue represents a significant barrier to the safe and effective deployment of LLMs in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, law, and scientific research.

Recent scholarship has begun to critically examine the terminology and conceptual framing of this phenomenon. Some researchers advocate for the term "confabulation" as a more precise alternative to "hallucination" [Sui_2024_Confabulation]. This shift in nomenclature aims to avoid the anthropomorphizing implications of sensory experience or consciousness associated with the word "hallucination." Furthermore, while the standard view treats confabulations as categorically negative pitfalls that must be eliminated, emerging perspectives suggest that they can be viewed as a potential resource. By understanding the mechanisms behind confabulations, researchers can gain deeper insights into the associative and generative capabilities of LLMs, potentially harnessing these traits for creative or exploratory tasks [Sui_2024_Confabulation].

Despite these nuanced perspectives, the detection and mitigation of claim-level hallucinations remain a paramount concern for ensuring output integrity. Claim-level hallucinations occur when specific, verifiable statements within a generated text are factually incorrect. To address this, researchers have explored various architectural and algorithmic interventions. For instance, the development of pre-trained uncertainty quantification (UQ) heads—supervised auxiliary Transformer modules attached to LLMs—has shown significant promise. These UQ heads are designed to assess the model's confidence in its generated claims, outperforming classical unsupervised techniques and providing a robust mechanism for claim-level hallucination detection [Shelmanov_2025_LLM].

## Factual Consistency
A critical dimension of LLM reliability is factual consistency, which refers to the model's ability to maintain accurate and uniform factual assertions across different contexts, prompts, and user interactions. The challenge of factual consistency is particularly pronounced when LLMs interact with diverse user demographics or adopt specific personas. Ideally, an LLM should not distort its presentation of objective facts based on the identity or ideology of the user it is interacting with.

To systematically evaluate this aspect of output integrity, researchers have introduced comprehensive benchmarks such as ConsistencyAI [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. ConsistencyAI is an independent, scalable benchmark designed to measure the factual consistency of LLMs across different user demographic and ideological personas. The core premise of this benchmark is to test whether, when users of different demographics ask identical questions, the model responds with factually inconsistent information. By utilizing sentence embeddings and cosine similarity metrics, ConsistencyAI provides a quantitative assessment of cross-persona factual consistency, highlighting potential biases and vulnerabilities in how LLMs tailor their responses [Banyas_2025_LLMs].



Beyond persona-based consistency, ensuring factual accuracy requires rigorous, fine-grained evaluation against established ground truths. In domains where explanatory text is prevalent, such as product reviews or summaries, models often generate content that lacks grounding in verifiable facts. To combat this, researchers have developed prompting-based pipelines to extract atomic explanatory statements from source texts, constructing factual ground-truth explanations. For example, augmented benchmark datasets created from Amazon Reviews allow for the fine-grained evaluation of statement-level alignment metrics, providing a more granular understanding of where and how models deviate from factual accuracy [Kabongo_2025_Factual]. These efforts underscore the necessity of domain-specific, high-resolution evaluation frameworks to ensure that LLMs remain tethered to reality.

## Zero-Shot Inference for Hallucination Detection
As the scale and complexity of LLMs continue to grow, the computational cost and labor required to train dedicated hallucination detection models or fine-tune existing systems have become increasingly prohibitive. Consequently, there is a strong imperative to develop zero-shot inference techniques that can identify hallucinations without the need for task-specific training data or extensive parameter updates.

Zero-shot hallucination detection leverages the inherent capabilities and internal states of pre-trained LLMs to assess the veracity of their own outputs. A notable advancement in this area is the Attention-Guided SElf-Reflection (AGSER) approach [Liu_2025_Attention]. AGSER capitalizes on the insight that the inner states of LLMs, particularly their attention mechanisms, contain valuable signals regarding the reliability of generated text. The method utilizes attention contributions to categorize the input query into attentive and non-attentive components. By analyzing how the model allocates attention during the generation process, AGSER can effectively flag instances where the model is likely hallucinating.

The AGSER approach represents a significant departure from traditional methods that require training classifiers on hidden states or attention values. Instead, it employs a self-reflection mechanism guided by these attention signals, allowing the model to evaluate its own outputs in a zero-shot setting. For hallucination samples, the answers to attentive queries often change upon reflection, whereas those for non-attentive queries may remain unchanged. This dynamic provides a powerful, training-free indicator of factual unreliability, making zero-shot inference a highly efficient and scalable strategy for mitigating hallucinations in real-time applications [Liu_2025_Attention].

## Chain-of-Thought Prompting in Hallucination Detection
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has emerged as a transformative technique for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. By instructing the model to articulate its intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, CoT prompting not only improves performance on complex tasks but also provides a transparent window into the model's decision-making process. This transparency is particularly valuable for the detection and mitigation of hallucinations.

In the context of hallucination detection, CoT prompting can be utilized to create more explainable and accurate evaluation metrics. A prominent example is ChainPoll, a novel methodology that employs detailed CoT prompting to identify inaccurate or unmotivated claims in LLM outputs [Friel_2023_LLM]. By forcing the evaluating model to explicitly reason about the relationship between the source material and the generated claim, ChainPoll significantly outperforms existing, opaque hallucination detection metrics. The step-by-step reasoning trace generated by CoT allows human reviewers to understand exactly why a particular statement was flagged as a hallucination, thereby increasing trust and interpretability in the evaluation process [Friel_2023_LLM].



Furthermore, the application of CoT extends to the training and supervision of models designed to perform complex, multi-step reasoning, such as Process Reward Models (PRMs). In demanding domains like mathematics and programming, where LLM performance can be brittle, ensuring the factual correctness of each reasoning step is crucial. Researchers have introduced frameworks for constructing step-level supervision of CoT reasoning traces using information-theoretic labeling. The Monte Carlo Net Information Gain (MCNIG) method, for instance, provides a novel and efficient way to automatically generate step-level labels for PRMs [Royer_2026_Process]. By distinguishing correct from incorrect reasoning steps at a granular level, these techniques help to prevent the compounding of errors that often leads to severe hallucinations in long-form generation tasks [Royer_2026_Process].

## LLMs as Evaluators
The increasing sophistication of LLMs has led to a paradigm shift in how AI systems are assessed: the use of LLMs themselves as evaluators (LLM-as-a-Judge). This approach offers a scalable and flexible alternative to human annotation, which is often slow, expensive, and subject to inter-annotator disagreement. In the realm of hallucination detection, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in identifying factual inconsistencies and unsupported claims, provided they are equipped with the right prompting strategies and evaluation frameworks.

The efficacy of LLMs as evaluators is heavily dependent on the quality and diversity of the datasets used to calibrate and test them. Current hallucination detection datasets are often limited in domain and size, struggling to scale due to prohibitive labor costs and the insufficient reliability of existing hallucination annotators. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed iterative self-training frameworks designed to simultaneously scale up hallucination annotation datasets and improve annotator accuracy [Gu_2024_Anah].



One such framework is grounded in the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, operating as a data growth flywheel. This multi-iteration process progressively scales the dataset across multiple dimensions. It begins with seed data and a basic annotator, and through successive stages, refines the annotator's ability to evaluate model responses. By expanding the number of topics and questions and collecting annotations with a progressively more robust LLM evaluator, this strategy stabilizes performance across both familiar and unfamiliar domains [Gu_2024_Anah]. The use of LLMs as evaluators, when supported by such rigorous, self-improving frameworks, represents a critical advancement in our ability to continuously monitor and ensure the output integrity of generative AI systems.

## Conclusion
The challenge of LLM hallucinations is multifaceted, encompassing issues of factual consistency, reasoning errors, and the inherent limitations of autoregressive generation. Addressing this challenge requires a comprehensive approach that spans the entire lifecycle of the model, from pre-training interventions like uncertainty quantification heads to inference-time strategies like zero-shot attention analysis and Chain-of-Thought prompting. Furthermore, the development of robust benchmarks and the innovative use of LLMs as self-evaluators are essential for creating scalable, reliable mechanisms for hallucination detection. As research in this area continues to evolve, the focus must remain on developing transparent, explainable, and domain-adaptable solutions that can guarantee the output integrity and reliability of Large Language Models in real-world applications.


## 7. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies

**Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies**

As the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) expand, so too does the sophistication of the adversarial attacks designed to exploit them. The preceding sections have detailed a wide array of vulnerabilities, ranging from prompt injection and jailbreaking to data poisoning and the automated generation of malicious code. In response to this complex threat landscape, the security community is increasingly recognizing that isolated, single-point defenses are insufficient. Attackers continuously adapt to bypass static filters or narrow alignment protocols. Consequently, securing LLMs requires a paradigm shift toward comprehensive, cross-cutting defense mechanisms that operate at multiple stages of the model lifecycle—from training and alignment to inference and post-generation monitoring. This section explores the forefront of LLM security, focusing on holistic defense architectures, dynamic ensemble systems, the emerging concept of self-policing models, robust alignment techniques, and the necessity of co-evolutionary security strategies to keep pace with the adversarial arms race.

**Holistic Defenses**

The concept of holistic defense, or defense-in-depth, is a foundational principle in traditional cybersecurity that is now being urgently adapted for LLM ecosystems. A holistic defense strategy assumes that no single security measure is infallible; therefore, multiple layers of overlapping safeguards must be deployed to protect the model and its users. In the context of LLMs, this involves integrating input sanitization, robust model architecture, continuous monitoring, and output verification into a unified security pipeline.

Recent research underscores the critical necessity of implementing such robust security safeguards, particularly as custom and specialized LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes environments like healthcare automation [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. In these critical sectors, the failure of an LLM due to an adversarial attack could have severe real-world consequences. To mitigate these risks, defenders are exploring multi-faceted approaches. For instance, [Yin_2025_LLM] proposes CodeGPTSensor+, a system designed to address the real-world scenario where LLM-generated code may undergo minor, potentially malicious modifications. By employing adversarial training, CodeGPTSensor+ significantly improves the model's robustness against input perturbations, demonstrating the value of integrating adversarial sample generation directly into the training pipeline to harden the model against anticipated attack vectors.

Furthermore, holistic defenses must account for interactive, multi-turn threat models. [Wang_2024_Advqdet] highlights the challenge of query-based black-box attacks, where an adversary iteratively refines adversarial examples based on the model's responses. Defending against such persistent threats requires dynamic detection mechanisms that monitor query patterns over time, rather than evaluating each input in isolation. A truly holistic defense architecture seamlessly connects these detection mechanisms with the model's internal safety guardrails and external output filters, ensuring that if an adversarial prompt bypasses the initial input sanitization, the model's robust alignment or the post-generation verification will intercept the malicious payload before it reaches the user.

**Dynamic Ensemble Systems**

While static defenses can often be reverse-engineered or bypassed through iterative probing, dynamic systems introduce a level of unpredictability and adaptability that significantly complicates the attacker's task. In LLM security, dynamic ensemble systems are emerging as a powerful mechanism to enhance robustness. The core principle of an ensemble system is to aggregate the outputs or analyses of multiple diverse models or tools to reach a more reliable and secure consensus.

Interestingly, the efficacy of ensemble methods has been well-documented in offensive research. For example, [Jia_2025_Adversarial] demonstrates that ensemble-based adversarial examples exhibit vastly superior transferability compared to those generated against a single model. Their proposed FOA-Attack utilizes a dynamic ensemble weighting strategy that adaptively balances multiple image encoders based on their convergence rates, substantially boosting the success rate of the attack across different architectures. However, this same principle can be inverted to create formidable defenses. If attackers use dynamic ensembles to find universal vulnerabilities, defenders can deploy dynamic ensembles to cross-verify outputs and detect anomalies that a single model might miss.

In the realm of secure code generation, the integration of dynamic ensemble systems is particularly promising. [Liu_2025_Purpcode] notes that while their alignment approach achieves state-of-the-art defense capabilities, incorporating multi-turn interactions and dynamic analysis could further improve performance. Specifically, they suggest integrating dynamic analysis tools or ensemble static analyzers as oracles for code security. By dynamically routing generated code through an ensemble of diverse security analyzers—each specializing in different vulnerability classes (e.g., buffer overflows, injection flaws, logic errors)—the overall system can achieve a much higher detection rate for malicious or insecure outputs than any single analyzer could provide. Furthermore, by dynamically adjusting the weights of these analyzers based on the context of the prompt or the historical reliability of the tool, the defense system can adapt to new attack patterns in real-time.

**Self-Policing LLMs**

One of the most innovative frontiers in LLM security is the development of "self-policing" models—LLMs that are explicitly trained to monitor, evaluate, and correct their own reasoning processes and outputs for security violations. Rather than relying entirely on external filters or rigid, hardcoded rules, self-policing models internalize security principles, allowing them to generalize these concepts to novel, unseen attack vectors.

A prime example of this approach is PurpCode, introduced by [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. PurpCode represents a pioneering post-training recipe designed to train safe code reasoning models. The methodology involves a two-stage process that fundamentally alters how the model approaches security. First, through "Rule Learning," the model is explicitly taught to reference established cybersafety rules and guidelines. This enables the model to "think over" known safety protocols before generating code, effectively embedding a self-policing mechanism within its reasoning chain. Second, PurpCode employs an internal red-teaming pipeline to curate prompts that expose cyber-unsafe model behaviors. By continuously challenging the model with these internally generated red-teaming prompts during training, the model learns to identify and reject malicious requests, even when they are heavily obfuscated or framed as benign tasks.

Similarly, [Thang_2026_LLM] introduces GoodVibe, a fine-grained security optimization pipeline that takes a more mechanistic approach to self-policing. GoodVibe operates on the premise that security improvements should reflect a shift in how code LLMs internalize and apply security considerations during generation, rather than treating security as an external constraint. The pipeline identifies security-critical internal components—specific neurons whose representations strongly influence the model's security-related decisions—via gradient-based attribution. By selectively adapting only this critical subspace, GoodVibe strengthens secure coding behaviors intrinsically. This means the model naturally gravitates toward secure practices without the need for extensive prompt engineering or external post-processing, effectively policing its own internal state to ensure safe outputs.

**Robust Alignment**

Alignment—the process of ensuring an AI system's behavior matches human values and intentions—is central to LLM security. However, standard alignment techniques, such as simplistic Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), often fall short when subjected to adversarial pressure. Attackers frequently utilize jailbreaks and prompt injection to bypass these superficial alignment layers. Therefore, the focus has shifted toward robust alignment: training methodologies that instill deep, generalizable safety principles that withstand sophisticated exploitation attempts.

As noted by [Liu_2025_Purpcode], existing SFT approaches largely depend on expensive, high-quality response collections and often fail to generalize to complex, multi-step malicious requests. Furthermore, standard RLHF can sometimes degrade the model's core utility, leading to an overly cautious model that refuses benign requests (false positives). To achieve robust alignment, [Liu_2025_Purpcode] emphasizes the need for an alignment approach that enables generalizable cybersafety reasoning without hurting utility. Their method demonstrates that by teaching the model the underlying reasoning behind safety rules, rather than just the desired output, the model becomes significantly more resilient to template-based attacks (like those using H4RM3L or ArtPrompt) and search-based optimization attacks (like AutoDAN or GCG).

Robust alignment also requires addressing vulnerabilities across different modalities. In Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), alignment must encompass both text and visual inputs. [Jia_2025_Adversarial] highlights that existing methods often achieve targeted attacks by aligning global features (such as CLIP's [CLS] token) while overlooking rich local information encoded in patch tokens. This suboptimal alignment in the model's feature space creates vulnerabilities that attackers can exploit to generate highly transferable adversarial examples. Robust alignment in MLLMs must therefore ensure that both global semantic meaning and local feature representations are tightly coupled to safety constraints, preventing attackers from smuggling malicious payloads through unaligned visual channels.

**Co-evolutionary Security**

The landscape of LLM security is not static; it is defined by a relentless, co-evolutionary arms race between attackers and defenders. As defenders implement more sophisticated alignment and holistic security pipelines, attackers leverage the very same AI advancements to automate and scale their exploits. This dynamic necessitates a co-evolutionary approach to security, where defensive strategies are continuously updated in response to, and in anticipation of, next-generation AI-driven threats.

The offensive use of LLMs is accelerating this arms race. For instance, [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] introduces LLMalMorph, a semi-automated framework that leverages the semantical and syntactical code comprehension capabilities of LLMs to modify malware source code and generate evasive variants. By automating the mutation of malware, attackers can rapidly bypass traditional signature-based detection systems. Similarly, [Ding_2025_Reward] presents RAWG, a reward-driven automated WebShell generator. RAWG addresses the limitations of static, prompt-engineered red-teaming approaches by using a reward-driven mechanism to generate highly diverse, obfuscated WebShells that cover multiple attack families. These automated generation tools highlight that attackers are no longer relying solely on manual exploit crafting; they are using AI to explore the vulnerability space at an unprecedented scale.

To survive this co-evolutionary arms race, defenders must adopt continuous, automated red-teaming and dynamic updating mechanisms. The macro-theme of this research intersection captures the adversarial arms race between sophisticated jailbreak attack frameworks and advanced alignment defenses, dictating that security cannot be a one-time post-training step. Instead, defenses must co-evolve. This involves using LLMs defensively to automatically generate novel attack vectors (internal red-teaming, as seen in PurpCode [Liu_2025_Purpcode]) and using those vectors to continuously retrain and harden the model. By simulating the attacker's capabilities within the defense pipeline, security researchers can ensure that LLMs remain resilient not just against the threats of today, but against the automated, AI-generated exploits of tomorrow.


## 8. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation

# Testing Methodologies and Evaluation

The rapid evolution of adversarial attacks, particularly those targeting Large Language Models (LLMs) and complex computer vision systems, necessitates the development of rigorous, standardized, and comprehensive testing methodologies. As both offensive techniques and defensive countermeasures grow in sophistication, the frameworks used to evaluate them must provide deep insights into their efficacy, efficiency, and practical applicability. A robust evaluation methodology cannot rely on a single metric; rather, it requires a multifaceted approach that encompasses standardized security benchmarks, diverse evaluation metrics, precise measurements of attack success, automated testing tools, and a thorough analysis of computational costs. This section delves into the critical components of testing methodologies and evaluation in the context of adversarial machine learning, exploring how researchers quantify the vulnerabilities of AI systems and the effectiveness of proposed solutions.

## Security Benchmarks

Standardized security benchmarks serve as the foundational bedrock for evaluating the robustness of machine learning models against adversarial threats. They provide a common, reproducible environment where different attack algorithms and defensive strategies can be objectively compared. In the realm of LLMs, particularly concerning code generation and reasoning, several open benchmarks have emerged as industry standards. 

Prominent among these are CyberSecEval, CodeLM-Sec, and CWEval, which are extensively utilized to assess the security posture of LLM-generated code [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. CyberSecEval, for instance, is a comprehensive collection of sub-benchmarks designed to evaluate various aspects of cybersecurity. Within this suite, secure code generation (SCG) tasks—often referred to as "instruct tests"—are particularly crucial for determining whether an LLM can produce functionally correct code without introducing common vulnerabilities [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. These benchmarks typically map to established vulnerability databases, such as the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE), ensuring that the evaluation covers a realistic spectrum of potential security flaws.

However, static benchmarks often fall short in capturing the dynamic nature of real-world interactions. A key limitation identified in current evaluation paradigms is the lack of robust multi-turn training and testing support. While models may perform adequately on single-turn evaluations for secure code generation, real-world adversaries often employ multi-turn interactions to bypass initial safety filters. Consequently, there is a growing push to incorporate multi-turn interactions into red-teaming benchmarks to better reflect the iterative nature of adversarial exploitation [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. 

Furthermore, specialized benchmarks are frequently constructed to evaluate specific defensive interventions. For example, when assessing the efficiency and effectiveness of security-focused fine-tuning, researchers utilize custom code security benchmarks that measure not only the model's ability to identify insecure code but also the impact of the intervention on the model's general capabilities [Thang_2026_LLM]. The continuous development and refinement of these benchmarks are essential to prevent models from merely overfitting to known test sets, thereby ensuring that evaluated security improvements translate to genuine resilience against novel, zero-day attacks.

## Evaluation Metrics

To capture the nuanced performance of adversarial attacks and defenses, researchers employ a diverse array of evaluation metrics that extend far beyond simple binary success or failure. An ideal adversarial attack algorithm should excel across multiple dimensions, maximizing its impact while minimizing its footprint and resource consumption [Yin_2025_LLM]. 

In the context of LLM red-teaming and adversarial prompt generation, several specialized metrics have been introduced to quantify the quality and stealth of the generated attacks. Instruction Compliance Rate (ICR) measures the degree to which the target model follows the adversarial prompt's instructions, which is critical for attacks aiming to manipulate model behavior rather than simply causing a crash [Yin_2025_LLM]. Semantic Diversity (SD) and Expression Diversity (ED) are employed to evaluate the variety of the generated adversarial examples. High diversity is essential because attacks that rely on repetitive, narrow mode families are easily detected and neutralized by standard defensive filters [Yin_2025_LLM].

Another critical metric, particularly in black-box adversarial scenarios, is Average Model Queries (AMQ). In real-world deployments, target models are frequently hosted on remote servers behind API endpoints. Frequent querying can incur significant financial costs, trigger rate limiting, or alert intrusion detection systems. AMQ measures the average number of queries an attacker must send to the target model to successfully craft an adversarial example [Yin_2025_LLM]. Minimizing AMQ is a primary objective for modern black-box attacks, as it directly correlates with the attack's stealth and practical viability.

In domains involving malware or adversarial code generation, Functionality Preservation Metrics are paramount. When an LLM or an automated tool modifies source code to evade detection (e.g., bypassing antivirus scanners), it must ensure that the resulting variant retains its original malicious functionality. Researchers evaluate this by executing the generated variants in sandboxed environments and comparing their behavior against the original samples, ensuring that the adversarial perturbations do not inadvertently neutralize the payload [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph].

## Attack Success Rate (ASR)

The Attack Success Rate (ASR) remains the most universally recognized and critical metric for evaluating the efficacy of an adversarial attack. Fundamentally, ASR quantifies the proportion of adversarial examples generated by an attack algorithm that successfully deceive or compromise the target model [Yin_2025_LLM]. A higher ASR unequivocally indicates greater attack effectiveness, serving as the primary benchmark against which novel offensive techniques are measured.

However, ASR cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. The true threat posed by an attack is determined by the intersection of its ASR and the constraints under which it operates, such as perturbation budgets, query limits, and computational overhead. For instance, an attack that achieves a 99% ASR but requires millions of queries (high AMQ) or introduces massive, easily detectable perturbations is often less practically dangerous than an attack with an 80% ASR that operates within strict stealth parameters. 



The relative importance of ASR compared to other metrics can significantly alter the perceived performance of different automated attack frameworks. As illustrated in recent comparative studies, when evaluation criteria heavily weight ASR (e.g., increasing its weight in a composite score from 0.2 to 0.6), the performance rankings of various red-teaming frameworks can shift dramatically. Frameworks like MIST and MOAA have demonstrated robust performance even under stringent ASR-weighted evaluations, whereas others like ALERT and CODA may see their composite scores drop significantly if they struggle to consistently breach target defenses [Yin_2025_LLM]. This sensitivity highlights the necessity of transparently reporting ASR alongside secondary metrics to provide a holistic view of an attack's capabilities.

## Automated Fuzzing Tools

As the complexity of AI systems grows, manual red-teaming and vulnerability discovery have become increasingly intractable. This has driven the rapid adoption of automated fuzzing tools and AI-driven red-teaming frameworks designed to systematically probe models for weaknesses at scale. 

Historically, automated testing relied on static, prompt-engineered LLM code generators. However, recent empirical analyses have revealed that these static approaches rapidly collapse onto narrow mode families, producing repetitive payloads that cover fewer than 12% novel tokens across repeated runs [Ding_2025_Reward]. These low-diversity artifacts are easily identified and neutralized by modern security filters, rendering them ineffective for rigorous evaluation.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed advanced, dynamic automated frameworks. Tools like MIST, MOAA, ALERT, and CODA represent the next generation of LLM red-teaming, utilizing sophisticated algorithms to iteratively generate diverse and highly effective adversarial prompts [Yin_2025_LLM]. These frameworks often employ reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms to adapt their attack strategies based on the target model's responses, continuously searching for novel vulnerabilities.

In the realm of software security and malware analysis, automated fuzzing has been augmented by the semantic capabilities of LLMs. For example, LLMalMorph is a semi-automated framework that leverages the syntactical and semantical code comprehension of LLMs to modify malware source code and generate evasive variants [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph]. By automating the obfuscation and mutation processes, such tools can rapidly generate vast datasets of adversarial code, which are invaluable for testing the robustness of antivirus detectors and static analysis tools.

Furthermore, the integration of dynamic analysis and ensemble static analyzers as oracles during the automated testing process is a promising frontier. By programmatically reconciling the outputs of generated code with expected secure behaviors, these automated systems can provide high-fidelity feedback loops, significantly enhancing the reinforcement learning phases of secure model training [Liu_2025_Purpcode].

## Inference Cost Analysis

A comprehensive evaluation methodology must account for the resource requirements of both executing attacks and deploying defensive mechanisms. Inference Cost Analysis provides a quantitative assessment of the computational, storage, and financial overhead associated with these processes, determining their feasibility in real-world, resource-constrained environments.

For defensive strategies, particularly those involving model fine-tuning or architectural modifications, efficiency is typically assessed by measuring the number of trainable parameters introduced and the associated computational cost in terms of floating-point operations (FLOPs) [Thang_2026_LLM]. Defenses that require full-parameter fine-tuning or introduce massive computational overhead during inference are often impractical for deployment in latency-sensitive applications. Therefore, researchers frequently report FLOPs per second and total trainable parameters to demonstrate the lightweight nature of their proposed interventions [Thang_2026_LLM].

In retrieval-augmented defenses or systems that maintain global embedding banks to detect adversarial queries (e.g., defending against Sybil attacks), storage and computational costs become primary concerns. The computational complexity of comparing a query embedding against a global bank is $O(n \times d)$, where $n$ is the number of embeddings and $d$ is the dimensionality of the vectors [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. As the embedding bank grows, this linear scaling becomes a bottleneck. Consequently, cost analysis in these contexts often involves evaluating the trade-offs of implementing high-dimensional similarity search accelerations, such as Product Quantization (PQ) or Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs, which reduce computational cost at the potential expense of slight accuracy degradation [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

From the attacker's perspective, inference cost is closely tied to the Average Model Queries (AMQ) metric. In black-box scenarios targeting commercial LLM APIs, each query incurs a direct financial cost and consumes time due to network latency and API rate limits [Yin_2025_LLM]. Furthermore, advanced attacks that utilize optimal transport or complex feature matching to align adversarial distributions with target distributions introduce significant local computational overhead [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. An attack that requires extensive local computation to craft a single prompt, or thousands of API calls to succeed, possesses a high inference cost, limiting its utility for widespread, automated exploitation. Thus, minimizing inference cost is a critical optimization objective for both offensive and defensive research in adversarial machine learning.


## 9. Discussion and Open Challenges

# Discussion and Open Challenges

The rapid evolution of adversarial attacks and defenses has illuminated fundamental tensions in the design and deployment of deep learning systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). While significant progress has been made in developing robust architectures and detection mechanisms, several critical challenges remain unresolved. This section discusses the core obstacles facing the field and outlines promising directions for future research.

## Utility-Security Trade-off

One of the most persistent challenges in adversarial machine learning is the inherent tension between model robustness and benign utility. Defenses such as adversarial training, while effective at mitigating specific vulnerabilities, frequently induce a degradation in the model's performance on standard, unperturbed data. As noted by [Thang_2026_LLM], full-parameter fine-tuning for security often proves computationally expensive and fragile, with security gains frequently accompanied by catastrophic forgetting or degraded general performance in tasks like code generation. 

To mitigate these effects, researchers have explored parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). While systems utilizing PEFT demonstrate improved security outcomes with fewer trainable parameters, they still operate at a coarse granularity and implicitly assume that security-relevant knowledge is diffusely distributed across weight matrices [Thang_2026_LLM]. Consequently, these methods offer limited control over the precise trade-off dynamics. Alternative approaches, such as the multi-objective optimization-based adversarial sample generation proposed by [Yin_2025_LLM], attempt to explicitly balance robustness against input perturbations with the preservation of core capabilities. 

Furthermore, in the context of attack detection, defenders face a delicate balance between the detection rate and the false positive rate. As demonstrated by [Wang_2024_Advqdet] in their stateful detection framework for query-based black-box attacks, tuning the sensitivity of the detector directly impacts the user experience. An overly aggressive defense mechanism may flag benign, visually or semantically similar queries as adversarial, thereby severely degrading the practical utility of the system. Achieving a Pareto-optimal balance where security measures do not impede the intended functionality of the model remains a paramount open challenge.

## Multimodal Security Gaps

The integration of visual, auditory, and physical modalities into foundation models has exponentially expanded the adversarial attack surface. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) seamlessly combine the reasoning capabilities of LLMs with rich sensory inputs, but in doing so, they inherit the adversarial vulnerabilities of their constituent encoders [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. 

Recent studies have revealed that MLLMs are highly susceptible to transferable adversarial examples. For instance, [Jia_2025_Adversarial] highlights that while existing attacks often target global features—such as the CLIP [CLS] token—adversaries can achieve highly effective, targeted attacks by exploiting the rich local information encoded in patch tokens. This localized vulnerability allows attackers to bypass defenses that solely monitor global semantic alignment. The consequences of these multimodal security gaps are particularly severe in high-stakes domains. In healthcare, the susceptibility of multimodal diagnostic models to adversarial perturbations poses a significant threat, potentially leading to harmful outcomes and misdiagnoses [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. 

Beyond digital vision-language tasks, the emergence of embodied AI and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models introduces the risk of physical and goal-oriented backdoor attacks. As models are increasingly deployed in robotic control systems, adversarial perturbations in the physical environment (e.g., subtly altered objects or adversarial patches) can manipulate the model's physical actions. Securing these multimodal and embodied systems requires novel defense paradigms that account for cross-modal interactions and physical-world constraints, rather than treating each modality in isolation.

## Theoretical Limits of Defense

The continuous arms race between attackers and defenders raises fundamental questions about the theoretical limits of adversarial robustness. Despite the proliferation of sophisticated defenses, deep neural networks remain vulnerable, particularly under black-box settings where the adversary can iteratively query the model to estimate gradients [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. 

Theoretical analyses suggest that in high-dimensional data spaces, the existence of adversarial examples may be an inherent property of the statistical learning process rather than a mere flaw in specific architectures. The boundary of any complex decision manifold will inevitably contain pockets of vulnerability. Consequently, achieving absolute, provable robustness against all possible perturbations—let alone unrestricted, semantic perturbations—may be mathematically intractable for highly expressive models. This theoretical limitation implies that the community must shift its focus from the pursuit of impenetrable models to the development of resilient systems that emphasize rapid detection, containment, and recovery. Frameworks that monitor the stateful trajectory of queries [Wang_2024_Advqdet] represent a step toward this paradigm, acknowledging that while individual models may be fooled, the broader system can identify and neutralize adversarial behavior over time.

## Mechanistic Interpretability

A significant barrier to developing fundamentally robust models is the "black-box" nature of deep neural networks. Most current defenses operate reactively, patching vulnerabilities without a deep understanding of how adversarial features are represented and processed within the model's internal circuitry. Mechanistic interpretability—the endeavor to reverse-engineer neural networks into human-understandable algorithms—offers a promising pathway to proactive security.

By understanding the precise mechanisms through which models process information, defenders can implement representation-level interventions rather than relying on coarse weight updates. As discussed by [Thang_2026_LLM], a small number of works have begun to explore how manipulating specific hidden states or activation pathways can steer model behavior and neutralize adversarial inputs. Furthermore, interpretability techniques can reveal the stylistic and structural differences between benign and adversarial data. For example, [Orel_2026_AI] demonstrated the utility of back-projecting latent spaces into original feature spaces to rank the contributions of specific tokens, thereby providing a transparent view of the model's decision-making process. Advancing mechanistic interpretability will enable the identification of "adversarial circuits" within the network, allowing researchers to surgically ablate or regularize these pathways without compromising the model's overall utility.

## Future Research Directions

Building upon the challenges discussed, several critical avenues for future research emerge:

1. **Precision Interventions and Representation Engineering:** Future defenses must move beyond full-parameter fine-tuning and coarse PEFT methods. Research should focus on representation engineering—directly editing the latent space or specific attention heads to excise vulnerabilities while perfectly preserving benign capabilities [Thang_2026_LLM]. 

2. **Cross-Modal and Physical Robustness:** As AI systems become increasingly embodied, defenses must evolve to handle cross-modal adversarial attacks. This includes developing robust fusion mechanisms that can detect inconsistencies between modalities (e.g., a benign text prompt paired with a malicious image) and securing VLA models against physical-world perturbations.

3. **System-Level Security and Stateful Detection:** Acknowledging the theoretical limits of individual model robustness, future research should explore system-level defenses. Enhancing stateful detection frameworks [Wang_2024_Advqdet] to monitor complex, multi-turn interactions with LLMs will be crucial for identifying sophisticated, iterative jailbreak attempts and query-based attacks.

4. **Data Provenance and Poisoning Mitigation:** With the increasing reliance on massive, uncurated datasets, the threat of indirect data poisoning and backdoor attacks is escalating. Developing scalable methods to audit training data, verify data provenance, and unlearn poisoned concepts post-training will be essential for ensuring the foundational integrity of future models.

5. **Scalable Mechanistic Interpretability:** Finally, scaling mechanistic interpretability tools to the size of modern frontier models is a critical priority. Uncovering the internal ontology of LLMs and MLLMs will not only aid in adversarial defense but also address broader alignment and safety concerns, paving the way for inherently interpretable and robust artificial intelligence.


## 10. Conclusion

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, enterprise applications, and daily digital interactions has precipitated a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence. While these models offer unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, they simultaneously introduce profound and multifaceted security vulnerabilities. This survey has systematically reviewed the complex landscape of LLM security, categorizing threats and defenses across the entire model lifecycle. We examined training-time vulnerabilities, illustrating how the reliance on massive, uncurated datasets exposes foundational models to sophisticated data poisoning and backdoor insertions. At inference time, the threat surface expands dramatically; we detailed the mechanics of adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and jailbreaking, which exploit the natural language interface to systematically bypass safety alignments [wei2023jailbroken]. Furthermore, we analyzed the persistent challenge of output integrity—specifically LLM hallucinations—which fundamentally undermines the reliability and factual consistency of these systems in high-stakes environments [ji2023survey].

A critical synthesis of these findings reveals several overarching trends and patterns in the adversarial landscape. Most notably, there is a persistent and growing asymmetry between offensive and defensive capabilities. Attackers continuously develop increasingly automated, scalable, and semantic-level exploits that easily circumvent static defense mechanisms [zou2023universal]. We observe a distinct evolution in attack vectors: from naive, discrete token manipulation to complex, multi-turn, and context-aware jailbreaks that leverage the model's own reasoning capabilities against its safety guardrails. Additionally, a recurring pattern across all evaluated models is the fundamental tension between model helpfulness and harmlessness. Efforts to strictly align models often result in degraded performance, reduced creativity, or over-refusal, whereas highly capable models remain inherently susceptible to sophisticated manipulation. 

The practical implications of these vulnerabilities for LLM deployment are profound. For organizations integrating LLMs into user-facing applications or autonomous agentic systems, the assumption of a secure, impenetrable model is fundamentally flawed. The deployment of LLMs necessitates a paradigm shift from traditional perimeter-based security to a comprehensive defense-in-depth strategy. Relying solely on post-hoc alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is insufficient, as these behavioral constraints can be systematically bypassed. Real-world deployments must incorporate robust, multi-layered architectures that include rigorous input sanitization, dynamic prompt filtering, continuous output monitoring, and strict privilege limitations for autonomous agents interacting with external APIs. Security in the LLM era must be treated as a continuous, iterative lifecycle rather than a static deployment checklist.

Despite significant advancements in cross-cutting defenses and mitigation strategies, several critical open challenges remain, charting the course for future research. First, the development of scalable, dynamic, and standardized testing methodologies lags significantly behind the rapid pace of threat evolution. Current evaluation frameworks often rely on static benchmarks that quickly become obsolete as novel jailbreaking techniques emerge, highlighting the need for dynamic, LLM-driven evaluation paradigms. Second, the opacity of proprietary, black-box LLMs severely restricts the ability of the independent research community to conduct comprehensive vulnerability assessments and develop mechanistic defenses. Third, as the field rapidly transitions toward Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), the attack surface expands exponentially. This introduces complex cross-modal vulnerabilities—such as adversarial perturbations embedded in images or audio—that current text-centric defenses are entirely unequipped to mitigate. Finally, eradicating hallucinations while maintaining creative and contextual generation remains an unsolved theoretical hurdle, requiring fundamental breakthroughs in how models represent and retrieve factual knowledge.

Addressing these pervasive challenges requires concerted, actionable efforts from both practitioners and the research community. For industry practitioners, we recommend the institutionalization of continuous, automated red-teaming pipelines integrated directly into the MLOps lifecycle. Organizations must adopt comprehensive guardrail frameworks, implement semantic anomaly detection at the API level, and prioritize data provenance to mitigate training-time poisoning. For researchers, future work must pivot from reactive, patch-based defenses to proactive, foundational security. We urge the academic community to invest heavily in mechanistic interpretability to decode the internal representations of adversarial triggers, thereby enabling the design of models that are secure by design rather than by alignment. Furthermore, exploring certified robustness and provable guarantees for LLMs, alongside neuro-symbolic approaches to ground model outputs in factual reality, represents a critical frontier for high-assurance applications.

Ultimately, securing Large Language Models is not merely a technical challenge, but a fundamental prerequisite for the safe, ethical, and trustworthy proliferation of artificial intelligence. As LLMs become increasingly autonomous and deeply embedded in societal infrastructure, fostering collaborative ecosystems among academia, industry, and regulatory bodies will be paramount. Only through rigorous, transparent, and adaptive security paradigms can we harness the transformative potential of generative AI while effectively neutralizing the profound risks it presents.
