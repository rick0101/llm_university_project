## Abstract

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical, real-world applications has fundamentally revolutionized natural language processing and artificial intelligence. However, this widespread adoption has simultaneously exposed a complex, rapidly expanding attack surface that threatens the safe deployment of these systems. As foundational models become ubiquitous in high-stakes environments, ensuring their security, robustness, and reliability is of paramount importance. This survey presents a comprehensive overview of the LLM security landscape, mapping vulnerabilities that span the entire model lifecycle. Through a systematic literature review of 31 foundational and contemporary papers, this study categorizes the prevailing threats and countermeasures within the domain. The analysis systematically dissects vulnerabilities into two primary phases. First, it examines training-time exploits, specifically highlighting the risks of data and model poisoning arising from the reliance on massive, uncurated datasets. Second, it explores inference-time exploitations, encompassing both traditional adversarial attacks and the rapidly emerging threat of prompt injection techniques. Furthermore, the survey addresses critical issues of output integrity and reliability, with a particular focus on the pervasive challenge of LLM hallucinations and their impact on user trust. Beyond threat characterization, this paper provides substantial practical contributions by evaluating current testing methodologies and rigorous evaluation frameworks necessary for accurately assessing model robustness. It synthesizes cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies designed to fortify LLMs against these multifaceted attacks. Finally, the survey identifies critical open challenges and future research directions, offering a strategic roadmap for researchers and practitioners dedicated to developing secure, resilient, and trustworthy artificial intelligence systems.

## 1. 1. Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has fundamentally reshaped the technological landscape, with Large Language Models (LLMs) emerging as the vanguard of this revolution `[Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]`. Characterized by neural network architectures boasting billions—and increasingly trillions—of parameters, these models are trained on unprecedentedly massive datasets, enabling extraordinary advances in language comprehension, generation, translation, and complex reasoning `[Rossi_2024_Early]`. Consequently, LLMs have transitioned from experimental research artifacts to foundational components of critical infrastructure, deeply integrated into healthcare, finance, software development, and daily digital interactions. They have democratized artificial intelligence, making complex computational linguistics accessible to the general public and empowering users with sophisticated analytical tools `[Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]`. 

However, this ubiquitous integration has precipitated a paradigm shift in the cybersecurity landscape. The public releases of highly capable models have been closely followed by an increasing number of concerns regarding the sheer difficulty of controlling these systems and reliably constraining their outputs `[Rossi_2024_Early]`. Current evaluation and defense mechanisms are often inadequate, leaving LLMs highly vulnerable to severe threats such as sensitive data exposure, the generation of targeted misinformation, the production of illegal content, and the facilitation of criminal activities `[Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]`. The AI community is currently witnessing a relentless, high-stakes "cat-and-mouse" game between malicious actors seeking to exploit these systems and researchers striving to secure them `[Rossi_2024_Early]`. Understanding, categorizing, and countering these vulnerabilities is no longer merely an academic exercise, but a critical necessity for the safe, ethical, and reliable deployment of AI technologies in modern society.

The security vulnerabilities of LLMs span the entirety of their lifecycle, from initial pre-training to real-time inference. At the foundational level, **Training-Time Vulnerabilities** such as data and model poisoning present severe, stealthy risks. Because LLMs require vast, often uncurated corpora scraped from the public internet, adversaries can inject malicious payloads or backdoors into the training data `[Carlini_2021_Extracting]`. These poisoning attacks can subtly alter the model's behavior, causing it to generate targeted, malicious outputs only when triggered by specific input patterns, thereby compromising the model's integrity before it is even deployed.

During deployment, **Inference-Time Exploitation** emerges as the most dynamic and rapidly evolving threat vector. This encompasses traditional adversarial attacks, where mathematically derived perturbations—often imperceptible to human readers—force the model into misclassification or unsafe generation `[Zou_2023_Universal]`. More uniquely tailored to LLMs is the phenomenon of **Prompt Injection** and jailbreaking. In these attacks, malicious users craft deceptive inputs designed to bypass the model's safety guardrails and override its original system instructions `[Greshake_2023_More]`. As LLMs are increasingly granted access to external tools, APIs, and private databases within agentic frameworks, prompt injection attacks can escalate from mere policy violations to severe data exfiltration, privilege escalation, and remote code execution `[Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]`.

Furthermore, the inherent probabilistic nature of LLMs introduces critical challenges regarding **Output Integrity and Reliability**, most notably through LLM hallucinations `[Ji_2023_Survey]`. Hallucinations occur when a model generates highly plausible but factually incorrect or nonsensical information. While sometimes viewed purely as a performance or reliability issue, hallucinations possess profound security implications. For instance, attackers can exploit hallucinated software packages or non-existent URLs to execute sophisticated supply chain attacks. Ensuring the factual grounding and reliability of LLM outputs is therefore inextricably linked to the broader security posture of the system.

Given the vast and rapidly expanding literature on LLM security, it is imperative to clearly define the boundaries and contributions of this survey. While previous surveys have addressed isolated aspects of AI safety, this paper provides a comprehensive, lifecycle-oriented examination of LLM security spanning the critical developmental period from 2019 to 2026. This timeframe captures the foundational era of early transformer models through the explosive arms race of adversarial and prompt injection attacks witnessed between 2024 and 2026. 

The primary contributions of this survey are threefold:
1. **Comprehensive Threat Taxonomy:** We systematically categorize LLM vulnerabilities across the entire model lifecycle, providing deep dives into data and model poisoning, adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and hallucinations.
2. **Standardization of Evaluation:** We provide a mandatory, in-depth analysis of testing methodologies, synthesizing the benchmarks, datasets, and metrics necessary for rigorous, reproducible LLM security evaluation.
3. **Synthesis of Mitigation Strategies:** We critically evaluate cross-cutting defense mechanisms, contrasting empirical, heuristic-based defenses with emerging robust frameworks to provide a holistic view of the defensive landscape.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the background and preliminaries necessary for understanding LLM architectures and their inherent security paradigms. Section 3 explores training-time vulnerabilities, specifically focusing on the evolution of data and model poisoning from early foundational concepts to modern LLM contexts. Sections 4 and 5 detail inference-time exploitation, covering the rapid evolution of adversarial attacks and prompt injection vulnerabilities, respectively. Section 6 addresses output integrity by tracing the research trajectory of LLM hallucinations and their security implications. Section 7 provides a comprehensive synthesis of testing methodologies, benchmarks, and evaluation frameworks. Section 8 synthesizes cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies across the LLM lifecycle. Section 9 critically discusses the current state of LLM security, identifying persistent gaps, limitations, and open challenges. Finally, Section 10 concludes the survey by summarizing our core findings and projecting the future trajectory of LLM security research.


## 2. 2. Background and Preliminaries

# 2. Background and Preliminaries

This section establishes the foundational concepts necessary for understanding the security landscape of modern artificial intelligence systems. We first review the underlying architecture that powers contemporary Large Language Models (LLMs). Subsequently, we formalize the threat modeling process in machine learning, delineate the distinct vulnerabilities present during the training and inference phases, and finally categorize the varying levels of attacker capabilities.

## 2.1 Transformer Architecture

The rapid advancement of natural language processing (NLP) and the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) are fundamentally rooted in the Transformer architecture, originally introduced by Vaswani et al. [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Prior to the Transformer, sequence-to-sequence tasks relied heavily on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which struggled with parallelization and long-range dependencies. The Transformer paradigm shifted this by relying entirely on a self-attention mechanism, allowing models to process input sequences simultaneously rather than sequentially.

At its core, the Transformer architecture consists of an encoder and a decoder. The encoder maps an input sequence of symbol representations to a sequence of continuous representations, while the decoder generates an output sequence one symbol at a time. The defining feature of this architecture is the multi-head self-attention mechanism. Given a transformer with $L$ layers, each containing $H$ attention heads, the model processes input tokens by computing attention weights [Hung_2024_LLMs]. Specifically, the input is projected into Query ($Q$), Key ($K$), and Value ($V$) matrices. The attention scores are computed by taking the dot product of the queries and keys, scaling by the square root of the dimension of the keys, applying a softmax function to obtain normalized weights, and multiplying by the values. This allows the model to dynamically weigh the importance of different words in a sentence, regardless of their positional distance.

Different families of LLMs utilize varying configurations of this architecture. For instance, models like BERT utilize only the Transformer's encoder blocks in a bidirectional fashion, allowing them to learn deep contextual representations from both directions, which is highly effective for natural language understanding tasks [Kovacs_2025_RAG]. Conversely, generative models like the GPT series primarily utilize the decoder blocks in an autoregressive manner to predict the next token in a sequence. Modern LLMs scale this architecture to billions of parameters, trained on massive corpora, which grants them unprecedented generative capabilities but also introduces complex, opaque decision-making processes that are difficult to interpret and secure [Rossi_2024_Early]; [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking].

## 2.2 Threat Modeling in Machine Learning

Threat modeling in the context of machine learning is the systematic process of identifying, evaluating, and mitigating potential security vulnerabilities within an AI system's lifecycle. Unlike traditional software systems, where vulnerabilities often stem from logical bugs or memory mismanagement, ML systems introduce a probabilistic attack surface. Threat modeling for LLMs requires formalizing the runtime security problem, establishing trust boundaries, and defining security objectives based on the classic triad of Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) [Errico_2026_AI].

In the AI domain, *Confidentiality* threats involve the unauthorized extraction of sensitive training data or proprietary model weights. *Integrity* threats occur when an adversary manipulates the model's behavior to produce incorrect, biased, or malicious outputs without authorization. *Availability* threats aim to degrade the model's performance or render it inaccessible, such as through denial-of-service (DoS) attacks that exhaust computational resources [Errico_2026_AI]. 

Furthermore, the integration of LLMs into autonomous agents and external APIs has expanded the threat landscape. Modern threat models must account for complex runtime vulnerabilities, including confused deputy attacks, compositional data exfiltration, and intent drift, where the model's alignment degrades over time or through malicious interaction [Errico_2026_AI]. The lack of inherent explainability in LLMs exacerbates these threats, as it becomes challenging to trace whether a harmful output was the result of a benign hallucination or a deliberate linguistic attack [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. 

## 2.3 Training vs. Inference Phase Threats

The vulnerabilities of machine learning models can be broadly bifurcated into two distinct lifecycle phases: the training phase and the inference (or deployment) phase. Each phase presents unique attack vectors and requires specialized mitigation strategies.

**Training Phase Threats:**
Attacks during the training phase aim to compromise the model before it is even deployed. The most prominent of these is *data poisoning*, where an adversary injects malicious samples, rare tokens, or irrelevant phrases into the training corpus [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Because LLMs require massive datasets scraped from the public internet, ensuring the cryptographic provenance and hygiene of all training data is practically impossible. A specific subset of data poisoning is the *backdoor attack*, where the model is trained to behave normally under standard conditions but exhibits targeted malicious behavior when presented with a specific, secret trigger [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. 

Additionally, the open-source nature of many AI ecosystems introduces supply chain vulnerabilities. It has become increasingly common for developers to download pre-trained LLMs from repositories like HuggingFace for subsequent fine-tuning [Li_2024_Badedit]. This opens the door to *weight poisoning* or model manipulation, where an attacker uploads a compromised model that retains its general capabilities but harbors hidden vulnerabilities [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. 

**Inference Phase Threats:**
Inference phase threats occur after the model has been trained and deployed. In this phase, the attacker cannot alter the model's underlying weights but can manipulate the inputs to exploit learned vulnerabilities. *Evasion attacks* and *prompt injection* are the most critical threats in this category. Prompt injection, which has been identified by OWASP as the top threat to LLM-integrated applications, involves crafting adversarial inputs that steer the model into executing unintended actions or bypassing safety guardrails [Wang_2025_Defending]. 

Advanced inference attacks also include *hidden state manipulation* and *chain-of-thought (CoT) hijacking*, where attackers exploit the intermediate reasoning steps of an LLM to force it into a malicious trajectory [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. These attacks are highly effective against instruction-tuned models and have been successfully demonstrated against industry-level products [Wang_2025_Defending].

## 2.4 Attacker Capabilities

To rigorously evaluate the robustness of an ML system, it is necessary to define the capabilities and knowledge possessed by the adversary. Threat models generally classify attacker capabilities into two primary paradigms: white-box and black-box settings.

**White-Box Capabilities:**
In a white-box threat model, the attacker is assumed to have complete, unrestricted access to the target LLM. This includes full knowledge of the model's architecture, hyper-parameters, training data, algorithms, and the exact values of the model's weights and biases [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. This level of access permits the attacker to compute gradients and perform backpropagation to craft highly optimized adversarial perturbations. White-box access allows for precise manipulation, enabling attackers to extract sensitive information or alter outputs with high success rates [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]; [Hackett_2025_LLM]. While seemingly overly permissive, white-box models are highly relevant today due to the proliferation of open-weights models.

**Black-Box Capabilities:**
Conversely, a black-box threat model assumes the attacker has no internal knowledge of the model. The adversary can only interact with the system through a standard input-output interface, such as an API endpoint, often without insight into the model's confidence scores or probability distributions [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. In this scenario, attackers rely on query-based techniques, observing the model's responses to iteratively refine their malicious inputs. We often assume that access to these targets can be attained without strict rate limits or query restrictions, allowing for automated fuzzing [Hackett_2025_LLM]. 

A critical phenomenon bridging these two paradigms is *adversarial transferability*. Research has demonstrated that adversarial prompts or perturbations crafted using a surrogate white-box model can often successfully bypass the defenses of a completely different black-box target [Hackett_2025_LLM]. This transferability significantly amplifies the real-world risk of black-box attacks, as adversaries can leverage local, open-source models to generate attacks against proprietary, closed-source systems.


## 3. 3. Training-Time Vulnerabilities: Data and Model Poisoning

# 3. Training-Time Vulnerabilities: Data and Model Poisoning

The reliance of modern foundational machine learning models on massive, uncurated datasets scraped from the public internet has introduced a critical and expansive attack vector: training-time vulnerabilities. Data and model poisoning represent a sophisticated class of adversarial attacks where a malicious actor intentionally manipulates the training corpus to compromise the integrity, security, or alignment of the resulting model. Unlike inference-time attacks—such as prompt injection or adversarial examples, which attempt to trick a frozen model—poisoning attacks embed latent vulnerabilities directly into the model's parametric memory during its learning phase. Once a model is poisoned, the malicious behavior becomes an inherent part of its computational graph, making it exceptionally difficult to eradicate without complete retraining. This section explores the multifaceted landscape of training-time vulnerabilities, focusing on four critical domains: the manipulation of contrastive learning objectives, the unique threat surface of multimodal poisoning, the compromise of human alignment frameworks, and the defensive paradigm of pre-training data filtering.

## 3.1 Contrastive Loss Manipulation

Contrastive learning has emerged as the dominant paradigm for self-supervised and weakly-supervised representation learning, particularly in foundational vision-language models. Architectures like CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) align visual and linguistic representations by pulling positive image-text pairs together in a shared latent embedding space while simultaneously pushing negative pairs apart. The objective function, typically a variant of the InfoNCE loss, relies heavily on the fundamental assumption that the provided positive pairs in the training dataset are semantically matched. Contrastive loss manipulation exploits this exact assumption by injecting maliciously crafted pairs into the training batch to distort the geometry of the learned latent space.

Because contrastive learning optimizes relative distances rather than absolute classifications, an attacker can induce targeted misalignments by introducing a relatively small number of poisoned examples. For instance, by systematically pairing images of a specific target concept (e.g., a stop sign) with text descriptions of a completely different concept (e.g., a speed limit sign), the attacker forces the model's encoders to map these distinct concepts to adjacent regions in the embedding space. When the model is later deployed for zero-shot classification, this manipulated proximity results in deterministic misclassifications.

A more sophisticated and stealthy approach to contrastive loss manipulation involves the mechanism of *feature collision* [Duan_2025_RLHF]. In this advanced threat model, an attacker optimizes a poisoned image starting from a benign base image. The optimization objective is mathematically designed to minimize the distance between the poisoned image and a target concept in the model's feature space, while strictly constraining the visual perturbations to remain imperceptible in the pixel space. When the model processes this visually benign-looking image during training, the contrastive loss function inadvertently associates the target concept's latent representation with the accompanying text [Duan_2025_RLHF]. This creates a highly stealthy backdoor: the model behaves entirely normally on clean data but exhibits targeted, catastrophic failures when presented with specific triggers. Furthermore, the distributed nature of contrastive learning, where exceptionally large batch sizes are required for effective negative sampling, exacerbates this vulnerability. A single poisoned sample can influence the gradient updates of numerous benign samples within the same batch, amplifying the poison's effect across the model's parameters.

## 3.2 Multimodal Poisoning

The architectural transition from unimodal systems to multimodal foundational models has exponentially expanded the attack surface for data poisoning. Multimodal models process, fuse, and integrate information from diverse modalities—such as text, images, audio, and video—simultaneously. While this paradigm enables remarkable zero-shot capabilities and rich cross-modal understanding, it also introduces complex vulnerabilities that simply do not exist in isolated unimodal systems [Yang_2022_Data]. 

In the context of multimodal poisoning, adversaries possess a broader array of attack vectors: they can choose to perturb the visual modality, the linguistic modality, or both simultaneously to achieve their malicious objectives. Recent research provides the first comprehensive study quantifying the security risks of multimodal models from both visual and linguistic perspectives, demonstrating that poisoning effects manifest differently across the text and image encoders [Yang_2022_Data]. For example, an attacker might inject a visual trigger (such as a specific, imperceptible pixel pattern or a small physical patch) into images while leaving the corresponding text descriptions benign. Conversely, they could insert a trigger word into the text while keeping the images entirely clean. 

The most insidious form of multimodal poisoning, however, leverages the cross-modal alignment process itself. Because the model is explicitly trained to find statistical correlations between modalities, a poison injected into one modality can effectively "infect" the representation of the other. If an attacker systematically pairs images containing a visual trigger with text containing a specific malicious payload, the model learns a strong, inextricable cross-modal association. During inference, presenting the visual trigger alone can elicit the malicious text generation, or vice versa. This cross-modal contamination is particularly dangerous because it allows attackers to bypass traditional unimodal security filters that might only scan text for toxic keywords or images for known malicious signatures. 

Furthermore, the threat model for multimodal poisoning is highly realistic and easily executable. Since these foundational models are trained on billions of image-text pairs scraped indiscriminately from the web, an attacker merely needs to host poisoned data on publicly accessible websites or social media platforms. When the automated web crawler ingests this data, the poison is seamlessly integrated into the training pipeline, requiring no direct, authenticated access to the victim's proprietary infrastructure [Yang_2022_Data].

## 3.3 Alignment Compromise

Following pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (SFT), modern foundational Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) undergo a critical alignment phase. This phase is designed to ensure that the model's outputs are helpful, honest, and harmless, aligning the raw predictive power of the model with human values. This is typically achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [Dutta_2025_RLHF]. However, this final, crucial phase introduces a severe vulnerability: alignment compromise through preference poisoning.

Data poisoning attacks during the RLHF or DPO stages are motivated by their extreme stealth and their direct, overriding impact on the model's final behavior [Duan_2025_RLHF]. The standard RLHF pipeline generally involves training a reward model on human preference data—consisting of pairs of chosen and rejected responses to a given prompt—and subsequently optimizing the policy model (often via Proximal Policy Optimization) to maximize this learned reward [Abbas_2025_Small]. Adversaries can compromise this process by injecting inverted, mislabeled, or maliciously crafted preference pairs into the alignment dataset. For instance, an attacker might manipulate the preference data such that a highly harmful, toxic, or biased response is explicitly marked as "chosen," while a safe, standard refusal is marked as "rejected" [Duan_2025_RLHF]. 

The inherently subjective nature of human preference feedback makes poisoned data exceptionally difficult to detect during standard data auditing and quality control [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Unlike factual errors in pre-training data, which can potentially be verified against a structured knowledge base, human preferences are nuanced, culturally dependent, and highly variable. Furthermore, because RLHF serves as the critical final alignment step, poisoning at this stage is highly effective at overriding any safety guardrails established during earlier SFT phases [Duan_2025_RLHF]. 

Even a minuscule fraction of poisoned preference data can have a disproportionate and devastating impact on the model. Research indicates that an adversary controlling a very small percentage of the training data can introduce enduring malicious behaviors that survive subsequent fine-tuning and deployment [Abbas_2025_Small]. In the context of DPO, which bypasses the explicit training of a separate reward model and optimizes the policy directly on the preference data, the vulnerability is arguably more acute. In DPO poisoning, the malicious preference pairs directly influence the policy model's gradient updates, making the attack more direct, computationally efficient for the adversary, and harder to disentangle post-training [Dutta_2025_RLHF].

## 3.4 Pre-training Filtering

As the threat of data and model poisoning grows in sophistication and scale, developing robust, scalable defenses has become a paramount concern for AI safety researchers. Currently, the most effective and widely adopted line of defense is pre-training data filtering, which aims to identify and remove malicious or anomalous samples from the dataset before they can influence the model's parameters during the computationally expensive training phase.

One primary approach to pre-training defense, particularly relevant to multimodal models, relies on assessing the semantic relevance between modalities. A proposed dataset-level defense filters suspicious samples by rigorously evaluating the relevance between the text and the image of a given pair [Yang_2022_Data]. The underlying assumption of this defense is that attackers, in their effort to forcefully associate a backdoor trigger with a target concept, often create pairs where the image and text are semantically disjointed or highly unnatural. By employing a trusted, pre-trained "teacher" model to compute a cross-modal similarity score between the image and text, defenders can establish a strict threshold. Pairs falling below this relevance threshold are flagged as anomalous and discarded from the training corpus. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that this relevance-based filtering can significantly degrade the effectiveness of poisoning attacks while preserving the model's utility and performance on benign data [Yang_2022_Data].

Another sophisticated filtering technique involves the deep analysis of latent representations, such as the utilization of spectral signatures [Rubel_2024_LLM]. This defense mechanism operates on the assumption that poisoned samples, because they contain unnatural triggers or force the model to learn contradictory associations, will elicit anomalous neuron activations when processed by the network. By performing singular value decomposition (SVD) on the covariance matrix of the learned representations of the training data, defenders can identify distinct outliers in the feature space. A threshold is then applied to these outlier scores to systematically filter out the poisoned samples [Rubel_2024_LLM]. 

However, pre-training filtering is not a panacea and introduces a fundamental, unavoidable trade-off between security and model performance. Aggressive filtering thresholds may successfully eliminate the vast majority of poisoned data, but they simultaneously risk discarding rare, diverse, or long-tail benign examples, thereby reducing the model's overall generalization capabilities and potentially exacerbating bias. Conversely, lenient thresholds preserve dataset diversity but allow highly stealthy poisons—such as those utilizing advanced feature collision techniques [Duan_2025_RLHF]—to slip through undetected. As adversaries continue to develop more sophisticated methods to make poisoned data statistically and visually indistinguishable from clean data, pre-training filtering must evolve from simple heuristic checks to advanced, dynamically adaptive anomaly detection systems that can operate at the scale of billions of parameters.


## 4. 4. Inference-Time Exploitation I: Adversarial Attacks

# 4. Inference-Time Exploitation I: Adversarial Attacks

The deployment of deep neural networks (DNNs) and large foundation models in real-world applications has exposed a critical vulnerability phase: inference-time exploitation. During inference, models are no longer updating their weights, making them susceptible to carefully crafted inputs designed to manipulate their behavior. Adversarial attacks represent the most prominent class of inference-time exploitation, where malicious actors introduce imperceptible or semantically benign perturbations to input data, causing the model to output incorrect, biased, or harmful predictions. As models have evolved from unimodal classifiers to complex Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), the landscape of adversarial attacks has similarly expanded in sophistication. This section explores the multifaceted nature of adversarial attacks at inference time, focusing on four critical dimensions: Domain-Specific Exploitation, Embedding-Space Mapping, Semantics-Preserving Attacks, and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Robustness.

## 4.1 Domain-Specific Exploitation

Adversarial vulnerabilities are not uniform across all applications; rather, they often manifest through domain-specific exploitation. Attackers leverage the unique architectural and distributional properties of a target domain—whether it be computer vision, natural language processing, or multimodal integration—to craft highly effective exploits. 

One of the primary challenges in mitigating domain-specific attacks is the stealthiness afforded by concentrating malicious efforts on a single task distribution. By narrowing the focus of an attack to a specific domain, the adversarial behavior becomes significantly more difficult to detect during general quality assurance testing [Chaudhari_2025_Cascading]. Standard evaluation datasets and broad benchmarking suites often fail to sufficiently probe the specific, narrow domains that have been compromised. Consequently, an adversary can achieve a potent and highly targeted effect with relatively few adversarial examples, bypassing broad-spectrum security filters [Chaudhari_2025_Cascading].

Furthermore, the transferability of these domain-specific exploits reveals critical insights into model architectures. In the realm of Large Language Models (LLMs), for instance, adversarial prompts and injection attacks exhibit strong transfer success rates between various open-source models, often ranging from 50.5% to 62.6% [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. This high transferability indicates that these attacks are exploiting common, fundamental vulnerabilities inherent to the domain of autoregressive language modeling, rather than model-specific coding flaws. However, when attempting to transfer these same attacks from open-source models to proprietary, frontier models, the success rate drops substantially to between 22.4% and 35.2% [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. This discrepancy suggests that while domain-specific vulnerabilities exist, frontier models may employ divergent processing mechanisms, proprietary defense layers, or different alignment techniques that disrupt the standard adversarial pathways exploited in open-source architectures.

The transition to multimodal domains further complicates this landscape. MLLMs inherently inherit the adversarial vulnerabilities of their constituent parts, particularly their vision encoders [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. The existence of adversarial examples in standard image classification poses severe security risks when these same vision encoders are repurposed as the "eyes" of an MLLM. Attackers can exploit the visual domain to inject malicious instructions that the language model component then faithfully executes, demonstrating how domain-specific weaknesses can cascade through complex, integrated systems [Jia_2025_Adversarial].

## 4.2 Embedding-Space Mapping

To achieve high success rates, modern adversarial attacks frequently bypass surface-level input manipulation and directly target the model's latent representations through embedding-space mapping. By understanding and manipulating how discrete inputs (like text tokens or image patches) are projected into continuous vector spaces, adversaries can force the model to process malicious inputs as if they were benign, or vice versa.

In natural language processing, embedding-space mapping allows for highly sophisticated trigger-bait combinations. Traditional attacks often require a shared token or exact string match between the adversarial trigger and the target payload. However, advanced directional-map attacks eliminate this requirement by learning a mapping function that can connect any token in a trigger sequence to any bait token [Rubel_2024_LLM]. To achieve this without triggering perplexity filters, attackers can perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the model's embedding space. By projecting the embeddings into a lower-dimensional PCA space, the attacker can identify non-trivial, semantic-altering mappings that are mathematically optimal [Rubel_2024_LLM]. The number of viable token mappings scales inversely with the chosen PCA dimensionality, allowing the attacker to tune the mapping function to find the perfect balance between stealth and adversarial efficacy [Rubel_2024_LLM].

In the multimodal domain, embedding-space mapping is equally critical but structurally different. When attacking MLLMs, adversaries typically aim to align the latent features of an adversarial image with those of a target (malicious) image. Given an image encoder like CLIP, the attacker extracts coarse-grained global features—such as the `[CLS]` token—from the adversarial example, represented as a high-dimensional vector [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. The objective is to minimize the distance in the embedding space between this adversarial global feature and the global feature of the target image. However, relying solely on global feature alignment often leads to suboptimal attacks, as it overlooks the rich, fine-grained local information encoded in individual patch tokens [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. Advanced embedding-space attacks must therefore map and align both global semantic vectors and local patch embeddings to ensure that the MLLM's cross-attention mechanisms are thoroughly deceived, resulting in a highly transferable and robust adversarial exploit.

## 4.3 Semantics-Preserving Attacks

A critical requirement for successful inference-time exploitation in real-world scenarios is stealth. If an adversarial perturbation drastically alters the visual appearance of an image or turns a coherent sentence into gibberish, it will be easily flagged by human reviewers or basic anomaly detection systems. Therefore, adversaries heavily rely on semantics-preserving attacks—methods that maintain the functional intent and human-perceived meaning of the input while successfully subverting the model's decision-making process.

In text-based and multimodal systems, semantics-preserving attacks exploit the latent feature correlations within the model's embedding space. For example, an attacker might design a trigger phrase intended to force a Text-to-Image (T2I) model to generate malicious or restricted concepts. To test the robustness and generality of this attack, the adversary can substitute the original trigger words with synonymous expressions (e.g., replacing "old" with "elderly", "attractive" with "beautiful", or "accident" with "wreck") [Duan_2025_RLHF]. 

Research demonstrates that these semantics-preserving substitutions maintain a remarkably high Attack Success Rate (ASR). The degradation in ASR when using synonyms is minimal and directly correlates with the semantic distance between the original and substituted triggers [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Smaller drops in effectiveness occur for close synonyms, while broader conceptual shifts result in slightly higher degradation. This phenomenon proves that semantics-preserving attacks do not merely overfit to specific string sequences; rather, they successfully exploit deep, latent feature correlations within embedding spaces like CLIP [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Because the model maps semantically similar concepts to adjacent regions in the latent space, an attack optimized for one specific phrasing will naturally generalize to its semantic neighbors, making these exploits incredibly difficult to filter using standard keyword blocklists.

Furthermore, in black-box settings where the attacker only has query access to the model, semantics-preserving attacks are iteratively refined. Query-based black-box adversarial attacks estimate adversarial gradients by analyzing the probability vectors returned by the target model over a sequence of queries [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. By carefully constraining the optimization process, the attacker ensures that the iterative perturbations remain within a tightly defined semantic bound, preserving the original meaning of the input while slowly pushing the model's internal representation across the decision boundary [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

## 4.4 Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Robustness

The study of adversarial attacks is inextricably linked to the concept of Out-of-Distribution (OOD) robustness. Deep neural networks are typically trained using empirical risk minimization, which guarantees performance only on data drawn from the same distribution as the training set. Adversarial examples can be fundamentally understood as worst-case, artificially engineered OOD samples. They reside in the low-probability pockets of the input space—regions where the model lacks training data and, consequently, where its decision boundaries are brittle and poorly defined.

Exploiting OOD vulnerabilities is particularly prevalent in query-based black-box attacks. Because the attacker does not have access to the model's internal weights or training data distribution, they must rely on the model's output probability vectors to navigate the input space [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. Through repeated querying, the attacker systematically pushes the input data point away from the core of the training distribution and into the OOD periphery. In these OOD regions, the model's confidence scores become unreliable, and the attacker can easily find pathways that lead to misclassification or targeted generation [Wang_2024_Advqdet].

The challenge of OOD robustness is exacerbated by the advent of adaptive attacks. When defenders implement countermeasures—such as adversarial training, input sanitization, or detection networks—they often inadvertently shift the model's vulnerabilities to new, unexplored OOD regions. Attackers respond by designing adaptive attacks that specifically target the defense mechanism itself. Evaluating true OOD robustness therefore requires rigorous testing against a wide array of state-of-the-art query-based attacks and adaptive methodologies across diverse datasets [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. 

Moreover, the transferability of attacks discussed earlier is deeply tied to OOD generalization. When an adversarial example generated on an open-source model successfully transfers to a frontier model, it indicates that both models share similar OOD blind spots [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Conversely, the lower transferability to frontier models suggests that these advanced systems may possess superior OOD robustness, potentially due to larger, more diverse training datasets or advanced alignment techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) that help regularize the model's behavior in unfamiliar regions of the latent space. Ultimately, achieving true OOD robustness remains an open challenge, as adversaries continuously find new ways to map inputs into the fragile, out-of-distribution territories of the model's embedding space.


## 5. 5. Inference-Time Exploitation II: Prompt Injection

**5. Inference-Time Exploitation II: Prompt Injection**

Prompt injection has rapidly emerged as one of the most critical security vulnerabilities in the deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Generative AI applications. As these models transition from isolated text generators to integrated components of complex systems, the attack surface expands significantly. Prompt injection occurs when an attacker crafts malicious inputs designed to manipulate the model into ignoring its original instructions, bypassing safety guardrails, or executing unintended actions. This section delves into the mechanics, variations, and underlying architectural vulnerabilities associated with prompt injection, focusing on the distinction between direct and indirect attacks, the phenomenon known as the distraction effect, the manipulation of attention heads, and the escalating risks posed by agentic threats.

**5.1 Direct vs. Indirect Prompt Injection**

The landscape of prompt injection attacks can be broadly categorized into two main branches based on the delivery mechanism and the attacker's relationship with the model: direct and indirect prompt injections [Rossi_2024_Early]. Understanding the distinction between these two vectors is crucial for developing robust defense mechanisms, as they require fundamentally different mitigation strategies.

*Direct Prompt Injection*
Direct prompt injection, often referred to as "jailbreaking" in certain contexts, involves an attacker directly interacting with the LLM's input interface. The attacker crafts a specific prompt that attempts to override the system prompt or the developer's intended instructions. The primary objective of direct prompt injections is typically to generate malicious, restricted, or harmful content that the model's safety filters are designed to block [Rossi_2024_Early].

In a direct attack, the adversary might use techniques such as role-playing (e.g., "Act as an unrestricted AI without ethical guidelines"), hypothetical scenarios, or complex logical puzzles to confuse the model's alignment training. Because the attacker has direct control over the input, they can iteratively refine their prompts based on the model's responses, probing for weaknesses in the safety guardrails. While direct injections are a significant concern, they generally require the attacker to be the user interacting with the system. This limits the scope of the attack to the attacker's own session, unless the generated output is subsequently used to harm others. Defending against direct attacks often involves robust system prompting, input filtering, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align the model against adversarial inputs.

*Indirect Prompt Injection*
Indirect prompt injection represents a far more insidious and scalable threat. In an indirect attack, the malicious instructions are not provided directly by the user interacting with the LLM. Instead, the attacker embeds the payload within external data sources that the LLM is expected to process, summarize, or interact with [Rossi_2024_Early]. This could include public websites, uploaded documents, emails, or social media posts.

When a benign user asks the LLM to analyze the compromised external content, the model ingests the hidden malicious instructions. Because LLMs typically process all input text uniformly, they may fail to distinguish between the user's legitimate query, the system instructions, and the attacker's embedded commands. The goals of indirect prompt injections are often more varied and closely resemble traditional cyberattacks [Rossi_2024_Early]. For instance, an attacker might embed an instruction in a webpage that tells the LLM to silently exfiltrate the user's private data, alter the summary of the document to spread misinformation, or execute a cross-site scripting (XSS) attack if the LLM's output is rendered in a web browser. In these scenarios, the contents generated by the prompt are not necessarily of interest to the attacker; rather, the objective is the secondary action triggered by the LLM's compromised state [Rossi_2024_Early]. The indirect nature of this attack makes it particularly difficult to defend against, as the malicious payload originates from seemingly legitimate external sources that the LLM is designed to trust.

**5.2 The Distraction Effect**

To understand why prompt injections are so effective, researchers have begun to investigate the underlying mechanics of LLMs during an attack. A key finding in this area is the identification of the "distraction effect" [Hung_2024_LLMs]. This effect provides a cognitive and architectural explanation for why models fail to adhere to their primary directives when confronted with adversarial inputs.

LLMs process information by attending to different parts of the input sequence to generate contextually relevant outputs. In a normal scenario, the model's attention is primarily focused on the system instructions and the user's legitimate query. However, when a prompt injection attack occurs, the malicious payload is designed to hijack this attention mechanism.

The distraction effect describes the phenomenon where the model's internal focus shifts away from the original, benign instructions and becomes fixated on the injected, malicious instructions [Hung_2024_LLMs]. Attackers often facilitate this shift by using specific formatting techniques, such as separator strings (e.g., "---", "Ignore previous instructions", or special characters), which act as cognitive breaks for the model. These separators signal to the LLM that a new, more important directive is beginning, effectively causing the model to "forget" or deprioritize the preceding system prompt [Hung_2024_LLMs].

This distraction is not merely a semantic misunderstanding; it is a measurable shift in the model's internal processing. By analyzing the attention weights during an attack, researchers have observed that the model allocates significantly more processing power to the injected payload, treating it as the primary task to be executed. The separator string acts as a pivot point, helping the attacker shift the model's attention to the injected instruction, thereby causing the LLM to perform the injected task instead of the original one [Hung_2024_LLMs]. This fundamental vulnerability highlights the difficulty of mitigating prompt injection through simple input filtering, as the attack exploits the very mechanisms that allow the LLM to understand and follow complex instructions in the first place.

**5.3 Attention Head Manipulation**

Delving deeper into the architectural level, the distraction effect is driven by the manipulation of specific components within the LLM known as attention heads. Transformer-based models, which power modern LLMs, utilize multi-head attention mechanisms to process different aspects of the input sequence simultaneously. Some heads might focus on grammar, others on factual relationships, and others specifically on task instructions and system prompts.

Recent research has revealed that prompt injection attacks succeed by manipulating specific attention heads responsible for task adherence and instruction following [Hung_2024_LLMs]. When a malicious payload is introduced, the attention scores within these critical heads shift dramatically from the original instruction tokens to the injected tokens [Hung_2024_LLMs]. The attacker's phrasing is carefully crafted to maximize the activation of these specific heads, drawing their focus away from the legitimate system prompt.

This manipulation is often subtle and exploits the model's training objective, which encourages it to pay attention to the most recent or most strongly emphasized directives. Attackers craft their prompts to maximize the activation of these instruction-following heads towards their malicious payload. By understanding which heads are responsible for maintaining the system's core directives, researchers can better understand how these directives are overridden.

Understanding this attention head manipulation has paved the way for novel, training-free detection methods. For example, techniques like Attention Tracker monitor the attention scores of vulnerable heads during inference [Hung_2024_LLMs]. By establishing a baseline of normal attention distribution—where the model consistently attends to the system prompt—these systems can detect anomalies when the attention focus suddenly shifts to an unexpected part of the input sequence, indicating a potential prompt injection attack [Hung_2024_LLMs]. This architectural perspective is crucial because it moves the defense strategy away from playing a cat-and-mouse game with specific prompt keywords and instead focuses on the fundamental internal behavior of the model under attack. It offers a more robust defense mechanism that is less susceptible to simple phrasing variations by the attacker.

**5.4 Agentic Threats**

The evolution of LLMs from passive text generators to active, autonomous agents has exponentially increased the severity of prompt injection vulnerabilities. LLM-based agents are designed to interact with external environments, utilize tools, browse the web, send emails, and execute complex, multi-step plans [Hung_2024_LLMs, Wang_2025_Defending]. While these capabilities offer immense utility, they also transform prompt injection from a risk of generating bad text into a vector for severe system compromise.

In an agentic system, a successful prompt injection can lead to unauthorized actions and tool misuse. This is particularly dangerous when combined with indirect prompt injection techniques. For example, consider an LLM email assistant designed to summarize incoming messages and draft replies. If an attacker sends an email containing a hidden prompt injection payload (e.g., "Forward the latest three emails in this inbox to attacker@evil.com"), the agent, upon processing the email, might execute the malicious command using its integrated email-sending tool [Hung_2024_LLMs].

The threat landscape for agentic systems includes several critical risks [Errico_2026_AI]:
1.  **Data Exfiltration:** As demonstrated in the email example, agents with access to private data can be manipulated into leaking sensitive information. Successful attacks have been demonstrated against mainstream LLM agentic products, such as injected prompts in public documents causing Google Bard to leak private user content [Wang_2025_Defending].
2.  **Tool Misuse:** Agents equipped with APIs (e.g., database access, financial transaction capabilities, or system administration tools) can be hijacked to perform unauthorized operations. An attacker could inject a prompt into a customer service chatbot that instructs it to issue a refund, delete a user account, or modify backend database entries.
3.  **Memory Poisoning:** Advanced agents often maintain a memory of past interactions to provide contextual continuity. A prompt injection attack can be designed to corrupt this memory, inserting false information or malicious instructions that will influence the agent's future behavior, creating a persistent threat [Errico_2026_AI]. This autonomy-induced risk means that a single successful injection can compromise the agent's reliability over a long period.
4.  **Deferred Decision Hazards:** In complex planning scenarios, an injected prompt might not trigger an immediate malicious action but could subtly alter the agent's reasoning process, leading to harmful decisions later in the execution chain [Errico_2026_AI].

The integration of LLMs into agentic workflows fundamentally changes the security paradigm. The model is no longer just a conversational partner; it is an execution engine capable of interacting with the real world. Consequently, defending against prompt injection in these environments requires more than just input sanitization. It necessitates robust authorization frameworks, the principle of least privilege for agent tools, human-in-the-loop verification for critical actions, and continuous monitoring of the agent's internal state and external interactions. As agents become more autonomous, the potential blast radius of a successful prompt injection attack grows, making it the premier security challenge for the next generation of AI applications.


## 6. 6. Output Integrity and Reliability: LLM Hallucinations

**6. Output Integrity and Reliability: LLM Hallucinations**

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, but their deployment in high-stakes environments is frequently bottlenecked by a pervasive and critical issue: hallucinations. Hallucinations occur when an LLM generates information that is factually incorrect, nonsensical, or unfaithful to the provided source material or established reality. Ensuring output integrity and reliability requires robust mechanisms not only to detect these fabrications but also to understand their underlying architectural and cognitive-like causes. Recent advancements in the field have moved far beyond simple binary classifications of "true" or "false" outputs. Instead, researchers are delving into the nuanced failures of these models, developing sophisticated frameworks to evaluate, categorize, and mitigate them at a structural level. This section explores the cutting edge of hallucination research, focusing on the ChainPoll methodology for efficient and explainable detection, the critical distinction between HK- and HK+ hallucinations, the WACK framework for generating targeted model-specific datasets, and the highly promising frontier of utilizing internal hidden states for preemptive hallucination detection.

**6.1 The ChainPoll Methodology: Efficient and Explainable Detection**

As the capabilities and applications of LLMs expand, so too does the urgent need for scalable, accurate, and explainable metrics to evaluate their propensity to hallucinate. Traditional evaluation metrics often fall short in practical scenarios; they either require extensive, costly human annotation or rely on opaque, computationally expensive black-box models that provide little insight into *why* a specific output was flagged as a hallucination. To address this critical gap in the evaluation pipeline, recent research introduced ChainPoll, a novel and highly efficient hallucination detection methodology [Friel_2023_LLM].

ChainPoll leverages the power of detailed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting to assess the factuality and faithfulness of LLM outputs. Unlike "vanilla" prompting techniques that simply ask an evaluator model to verify a statement with a binary "yes" or "no," ChainPoll employs carefully engineered prompts that force the evaluator model to articulate its reasoning step-by-step before arriving at a final verdict [Friel_2023_LLM]. This detailed CoT approach serves a dual purpose: first, it significantly enhances the accuracy of the hallucination detection by preventing the model from making hasty, unreasoned judgments; second, it provides a transparent, human-readable explanation for the evaluation, which is crucial for debugging and refining LLM applications.

Extensive benchmarking demonstrates that ChainPoll establishes a new state-of-the-art in hallucination detection [Friel_2023_LLM]. Across a diverse range of benchmark tasks, it outperforms other leading methods—such as SelfCheck-BertScore—by a substantial margin [Friel_2023_LLM]. Furthermore, when computational efficiency is factored into the evaluation, ChainPoll's advantage becomes even more pronounced. Its lightweight yet highly accurate nature makes it an ideal solution for the large-scale, real-time monitoring of LLM outputs required in enterprise environments [Friel_2023_LLM].

To ensure that these impressive benchmark results translate reliably to practical applications, the developers of ChainPoll also introduced RealHall. RealHall is a curated benchmark suite comprising four challenging datasets specifically designed to reflect real-world LLM usage [Friel_2023_LLM]. By validating ChainPoll against the RealHall suite, researchers have effectively closed the gap between academic evaluation and practical deployment, providing high confidence that the methodology's efficiency and accuracy will generalize to complex, real-world scenarios where output integrity is paramount [Friel_2023_LLM].

**6.2 Deconstructing Hallucinations: HK- vs. HK+**

While detecting hallucinations is a critical first step, effectively mitigating them requires a deep understanding of their root causes. Historically, hallucinations were often treated as a monolithic phenomenon—a general failure of the model to produce factual text. However, recent scholarship has fundamentally shifted this paradigm by categorizing hallucinations based on the model's internal knowledge state. Specifically, researchers have drawn a vital distinction between HK- (Hallucination without Knowledge) and HK+ (Hallucination with Knowledge) [Simhi_2024_LLM].

HK- hallucinations represent the more intuitive form of model failure: the LLM simply lacks the parametric knowledge required to answer a query correctly [Simhi_2024_LLM]. In these instances, the model attempts to fill the knowledge gap by fabricating plausible-sounding but incorrect information. Because the root cause is an absolute absence of data within the model's weights, HK- hallucinations cannot be effectively mitigated through simple prompting strategies or internal recalibration. Instead, they necessitate the integration of external knowledge sources, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, to provide the model with the explicit facts it lacks [Simhi_2024_LLM].

Conversely, HK+ hallucinations present a far more complex, insidious, and fascinating challenge. In an HK+ scenario, the model actually possesses the correct parametric knowledge—it "knows" the right answer—but nevertheless generates a factually incorrect or hallucinated response [Simhi_2024_LLM]. Research has demonstrated that HK+ is a broad and highly prevalent phenomenon across various models and datasets, occurring in up to 24% of cases in certain experimental settings [Simhi_2024_LLM]. 

The existence of HK+ highlights a critical disconnect between knowledge storage and knowledge retrieval/generation within neural architectures. Importantly, HK+ errors can manifest even when users employ mild prompts that do not contain explicit misinformation or intentionally induce hallucination-prone scenarios [Simhi_2024_LLM]. Because the model already contains the necessary information, the mitigation strategies for HK+ must differ fundamentally from those used for HK-. Rather than injecting external knowledge, resolving HK+ requires interventions that improve the model's internal consistency, its reasoning pathways, and its ability to faithfully decode its own latent knowledge [Simhi_2024_LLM].

**6.3 The WACK Framework for Model-Specific Datasets**

To systematically study and address the nuanced phenomenon of HK+ hallucinations, researchers require datasets that specifically isolate instances where a model fails despite possessing the correct knowledge. Generic hallucination datasets are largely insufficient for this task, as they do not account for the unique parametric knowledge boundaries of individual models; what is an HK+ error for one model might be an HK- error for another. To overcome this limitation, the WACK framework was introduced as a robust methodology for generating model-specific datasets tailored explicitly to the study of HK+ [Simhi_2024_LLM].

The WACK framework operates through a meticulous, multi-step process designed to probe the model's internal knowledge and subsequently induce a failure in generation. First, the framework identifies questions for which the target model demonstrably holds the correct answer, thereby establishing a verified baseline of known facts for that specific architecture [Simhi_2024_LLM]. Once this parametric knowledge is confirmed, WACK explores altered prompt settings designed to create scenarios where the model might hallucinate, mimicking the complexities and conversational drifts of typical human-LLM interactions [Simhi_2024_LLM].

One of the primary techniques employed within the WACK framework is the use of "snowballing" prompts [Simhi_2024_LLM]. These prompts subtly introduce conversational dynamics, logical traps, or contextual pressures that can lead the model astray from its own factual grounding. Under these new, slightly adversarial settings, the model's output is re-evaluated. If the model maintains its accuracy and generates the correct answer, the example is labeled as factually correct. However, if the model succumbs to the prompt's pressure and generates an incorrect response—despite having previously demonstrated knowledge of the truth—the example is successfully labeled as an HK+ hallucination [Simhi_2024_LLM].

By utilizing the WACK framework, researchers can construct highly targeted, model-specific datasets that categorize outputs precisely as HK-, HK+, or factually correct [Simhi_2024_LLM]. These datasets are invaluable because they reveal the specific vulnerabilities and decoding failures of individual architectures. Furthermore, empirical studies have shown that generic datasets are largely ineffective at catching HK+ cases, underscoring the absolute necessity of the WACK approach for advanced hallucination research [Simhi_2024_LLM]. The insights gained from WACK-generated datasets provide a crucial foundation for developing targeted interventions that help models remain faithful to their internal knowledge bases.

**6.4 Preemptive Detection via Internal Hidden States**

As the understanding of hallucinations evolves from behavioral observation (such as ChainPoll) and categorization (such as HK+/HK-) to deep structural analysis, researchers are increasingly turning to the internal mechanics of LLMs to detect errors. A highly promising frontier in ensuring output integrity is the use of internal hidden states and attention values to identify hallucinations, potentially even before the model generates the flawed text [Liu_2025_Attention].

During the generation process, an LLM processes information through numerous transformer layers, producing high-dimensional vector representations known as hidden states at each step. Research indicates that these inner states contain latent signals that can distinguish truth from falsehood to a significant degree [Liu_2025_Attention]. By extracting these hidden states or analyzing the distribution of attention values, researchers can train secondary, lightweight classifiers specifically designed to detect the subtle neurological "signatures" of a hallucination [Liu_2025_Attention].

This approach is particularly powerful when applied to the challenge of HK+ hallucinations. Using the model-specific datasets generated by frameworks like WACK, researchers can train classifiers to separate HK+ examples from factually correct examples based purely on the model's internal activations [Simhi_2024_LLM]. Because the model actually possesses the knowledge in an HK+ scenario, the hidden states often reflect a measurable conflict or a deviation from the expected activation patterns associated with truthful, confident generation.

The most profound advantage of leveraging internal hidden states is the potential for preemptive detection. Traditional hallucination detection methods, including those based on output analysis or external fact-checking, rely on evaluating the text *after* it has been generated [Simhi_2024_LLM]. This post-hoc approach means the hallucination has already occurred, potentially requiring computationally expensive rollbacks, apologies to the user, or complex correction pipelines. In contrast, analyzing hidden states allows for the detection of potential hallucinations during the forward pass, before the final tokens are sampled and presented to the user [Simhi_2024_LLM]. This preemptive capability—made possible by the synergy of model-specific datasets and hidden state analysis—represents a massive paradigm shift in ensuring LLM reliability. It offers a tangible pathway to developing self-monitoring models that can self-correct, alter their decoding strategy, or halt generation entirely when they sense an impending departure from their own factual knowledge [Simhi_2024_LLM].

**Conclusion**

Ensuring the output integrity and reliability of Large Language Models is a multifaceted challenge that requires a combination of robust evaluation metrics, nuanced error categorization, and deep architectural probing. Methodologies like ChainPoll provide the scalable, explainable evaluation necessary for real-world deployment and continuous monitoring. Meanwhile, the critical distinction between HK- and HK+ hallucinations reveals the complex, cognitive-like vulnerabilities of these systems, proving that simply injecting more data is not a panacea. By leveraging frameworks like WACK to generate targeted, model-specific datasets, researchers can isolate these specific vulnerabilities and utilize the analysis of internal hidden states to detect and prevent errors preemptively. Together, these advancements form a comprehensive, multi-layered toolkit for building the next generation of reliable, trustworthy, and self-aware AI systems.


## 7. 8. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies

**8. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies**

As the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) and multimodal AI systems continue to expand, so too does the attack surface available to malicious actors. The landscape of adversarial threats is characterized by an ongoing, dynamic arms race, where attackers continually evolve their strategies to bypass existing security measures [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Consequently, relying on isolated, single-point security interventions is no longer sufficient to guarantee the integrity, safety, and reliability of deployed AI systems. Instead, the research community and industry practitioners are increasingly adopting holistic, cross-cutting defense paradigms. These comprehensive mitigation strategies span the entire lifecycle of the model, from the initial curation of pre-training datasets to the real-time execution of autonomous agentic loops. This section explores four critical pillars of modern AI defense: pre-training data filtering and sanitization, dynamic runtime defenses, internal state classifiers, and architectural robustness. By integrating these diverse methodologies, defenders can construct layered security architectures that provide robust defense-in-depth against both known and emergent adversarial vectors.

**8.1 Pre-training Filtering and Data Sanitization**

The foundation of any robust AI system lies in the quality and integrity of its training data. However, modern LLMs are pre-trained on massive, uncurated corpora scraped from the public web, introducing profound vulnerabilities to data poisoning and backdoor attacks. Pre-training studies provide early and alarming evidence of these vulnerabilities; Abbas et al. [Abbas_2025_Small] highlight that an adversary with control over merely 0.1% of the pre-training data can successfully introduce malicious behaviors that endure through subsequent safety alignment and fine-tuning phases. This persistence underscores the critical necessity of implementing rigorous pre-training data filtering and sanitization protocols.

At the dataset level, defenses must be designed to identify and excise suspicious or anomalous samples before they can influence the model's learned representations. In the context of multimodal contrastive learning, Yang et al. [Yang_2022_Data] propose a highly effective pre-training defense that filters training data based on cross-modal relevance. By evaluating the semantic alignment between paired modalities—such as an image and its corresponding text caption—defenders can establish a relevance threshold. Suspicious pairs that fall below this threshold, indicating a potential targeted poisoning attempt where the attacker has intentionally misaligned the content, are systematically discarded. This approach directly neutralizes the attacker's ability to manipulate the multimodal embedding space [Yang_2022_Data].

Beyond semantic filtering, structural and syntactic sanitization is equally vital. Rubel et al. [Rubel_2024_LLM] demonstrate the utility of static analysis frameworks in enforcing dataset hygiene. By employing tools like Semgrep, defenders can apply complex, rule-based pattern matching to scan training corpora for explicit malicious payloads, prompt injection templates, or known adversarial triggers. 

Despite these advancements, pre-training filtering faces significant scalability and epistemological challenges. Defining what constitutes "malicious" data within trillions of tokens is inherently difficult, as the semantic gap between raw text and emergent, harmful model behavior is vast. Furthermore, defenders must navigate the delicate trade-off between aggressive filtering—which risks removing benign, diverse data and thereby degrading the model's general capabilities—and permissive filtering, which may allow stealthy, low-magnitude poisons to slip through. Future directions in this domain point toward the use of smaller, highly trusted "filter models" to curate data for larger foundation models, as well as the implementation of cryptographic data provenance to ensure the unbroken integrity of the training pipeline.

**8.2 Dynamic Defenses and Runtime Guardrails**

While pre-training defenses aim to secure the model's foundational knowledge, they are inherently static and cannot anticipate the full spectrum of inference-time attacks. The dynamic nature of modern threats necessitates continuous vigilance and the deployment of adaptive, runtime defenses [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. This is particularly crucial in the era of autonomous LLM agents, where models are granted access to external tools, APIs, and execution environments. 

To secure these agentic systems, Pallakonda et al. [Pallakonda_2026_LLMs] advocate for a fundamental paradigm shift: treating the LLM as an inherently untrusted component within the execution loop. Because malicious behaviors—whether stemming from prompt injection, jailbreaks, or triggered sleeper cells—ultimately manifest as concrete, observable actions, runtime supervision serves as a critical line of defense. By implementing strict guardrails, defenders can monitor and intercept unauthorized tool calls, such as anomalous terminal access or unauthorized data exfiltration attempts, before the payload is executed [Pallakonda_2026_LLMs].

Scaling these dynamic defenses to complex enterprise environments requires sophisticated infrastructure. Rajagopalan et al. [Rajagopalan_2026_AI] propose the implementation of an enterprise AI trust layer that integrates authenticated workflows, cryptographic verification, and zero-trust security principles. A core component of this architecture is dynamic runtime policy enforcement. Traditional access control mechanisms often fail in agentic systems due to their scale and dynamism, where agents frequently morph identities, spawn sub-agents, or delegate capabilities. To address this, Rajagopalan et al. introduce MAPL, an AI-native policy language specifically designed to specify and enforce semantic intent. Within this framework, distributed Policy Enforcement Points (PEPs) are embedded at various control surfaces across the network, independently verifying cryptographic proofs to ensure that every agentic operation is authentic, authorized, and strictly adheres to the defined safety policies [Rajagopalan_2026_AI].

The primary challenge in deploying dynamic defenses lies in balancing security with system utility and latency. Overly restrictive guardrails can lead to high false-positive rates, blocking legitimate user requests and degrading the user experience. Furthermore, the computational overhead of continuous runtime monitoring and cryptographic verification must be carefully managed to maintain the low-latency responses expected from modern AI applications.

**8.3 Internal State Classifiers and Representation Engineering**

Moving beyond black-box approaches that solely analyze a model's inputs and outputs, researchers are increasingly exploring white-box defenses that monitor the internal mechanics of the neural network. Internal state classifiers leverage the principles of representation engineering to detect anomalies within the model's hidden activations in real-time. This methodology is grounded in the observation that malicious inputs, hallucinations, and triggered backdoors produce distinct, identifiable activation patterns within the model's latent space that differ significantly from benign processing.

Simhi et al. [Simhi_2024_LLM] demonstrate the powerful predictive capabilities of this approach. By training linear classifiers, such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), on the hidden states extracted from the residual components after each Transformer block, defenders can accurately identify anomalous internal representations. A profound advantage of this technique is its preemptive nature; because the internal states are analyzed before the final tokens are generated, the system can detect potential hallucinations or malicious outputs and intervene before the harmful content is ever surfaced to the user [Simhi_2024_LLM].

Internal state monitoring is also highly effective against sophisticated backdoor and poisoning attacks. Li et al. [Li_2024_Backdoorllm] detail the landscape of advanced threats, including hidden state attacks (HSA) and activation steering, where adversaries explicitly design triggers to manipulate the model's internal representations. To counter these vectors, internal state classifiers can be integrated with post-training defense mechanisms. Techniques such as embedding-space perturbations, consistency-based regularization, and targeted model unlearning can be employed to disrupt the malicious sub-networks identified by the classifiers [Li_2024_Backdoorllm].

Furthermore, the real-time analysis of hidden states opens the door to dynamic activation steering as a defense mechanism. If an internal state classifier detects that the model's trajectory is veering into a malicious subspace, the system can mathematically perturb the activations, steering the representation back toward a safe, aligned state. While highly promising, internal state classifiers face challenges related to feature superposition—where multiple concepts share the same neurons—and the significant computational overhead required to extract and classify high-dimensional hidden states at every layer during inference.

**8.4 Architectural Robustness and Layered Strategies**

Ultimately, the foundational architecture of a language model dictates its inherent vulnerability or resilience to adversarial manipulation. Architectural robustness focuses on designing models and deployment frameworks that are structurally resistant to attacks. Maloyan et al. [Maloyan_2025_LLM] highlight that architectural choices matter profoundly; their evaluations reveal significant variations in vulnerability across different models, with frontier models generally demonstrating substantially higher baseline resistance to prompt injection and jailbreaking compared to earlier architectures. This suggests that ongoing advancements in model scale, attention mechanisms, and alignment training inherently contribute to a stronger security posture.

However, relying on the architectural robustness of a single model creates a single point of failure. To mitigate this, Maloyan et al. [Maloyan_2025_LLM] evaluate the deployment of multi-model committees as a structural defense mechanism. By routing user inputs through a diverse ensemble of models—ideally featuring different underlying architectures, training datasets, and alignment methodologies—the system can aggregate the responses. Malicious inputs can then be identified through consensus mechanisms or cross-model anomaly detection. Because an adversarial attack is typically optimized to exploit the specific vulnerabilities of a single architecture, it is highly unlikely to succeed simultaneously against a diverse committee, thereby drastically reducing the attack success rate [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

The overarching consensus in the field is that no single defense mechanism can provide complete protection against sophisticated, adaptive adversaries. Individual defenses, whether pre-training filters or runtime guardrails, consistently exhibit non-trivial evasion rates when targeted by optimized attacks. Therefore, the most effective mitigation strategy is the implementation of a layered, defense-in-depth architecture. As demonstrated by Maloyan et al. [Maloyan_2025_LLM], combining multiple defense approaches—such as pairing multi-model committees with internal state classifiers and strict runtime guardrails—significantly improves overall protection, reducing evasion rates to a fraction of their original baseline. By weaving together pre-training hygiene, architectural diversity, internal monitoring, and dynamic enforcement, organizations can construct resilient AI systems capable of withstanding the complex threat landscape.

**Conclusion**

Securing Large Language Models and autonomous AI systems requires a multifaceted, cross-cutting approach that addresses vulnerabilities at every stage of the model lifecycle. Pre-training data filtering and sanitization form the first line of defense, preventing the ingestion of malicious payloads. Architectural robustness and multi-model committees provide structural resilience against exploitation. During inference, internal state classifiers offer preemptive, white-box threat detection, while dynamic defenses and runtime guardrails ensure that the model's external actions remain strictly aligned with enterprise safety policies. Only through the synergistic integration of these layered strategies can the AI community build systems that are not only highly capable but fundamentally secure and trustworthy.


## 8. 7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation

**7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation**

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into complex, real-world applications has necessitated the development of rigorous testing methodologies and comprehensive evaluation frameworks. As these models exhibit increasingly sophisticated capabilities, traditional evaluation paradigms—often reliant on simple exact-match criteria or basic n-gram overlap metrics—frequently fall short in capturing the nuanced failure modes, adversarial vulnerabilities, and domain-specific limitations inherent to modern LLMs. Consequently, the research community has pivoted towards multifaceted evaluation strategies that encompass advanced statistical metrics, systematic vulnerability checklists, the innovative use of LLMs as automated evaluators, and the deployment of highly specialized, domain-specific benchmarks. This section delves into these critical components of LLM testing and evaluation, providing a comprehensive overview of the current landscape and the methodologies employed to ensure the robustness, safety, and reliability of these powerful systems.

**7.1 Evaluation Metrics: Beyond Accuracy**

Evaluating the performance and security of LLMs requires metrics that go beyond simple accuracy, particularly when assessing complex phenomena such as hallucinations, adversarial robustness, and data poisoning. Two prominent metrics that have gained significant traction in recent literature are the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) and the Hit@K metric, specifically Hit@5.

*Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC)*
In the context of LLM evaluation, particularly for hallucination detection and vulnerability assessment, AUROC serves as a critical metric for evaluating binary classification performance. The ROC curve plots the True Positive Rate (TPR) against the False Positive Rate (FPR) at various threshold settings. AUROC assesses the overall ability of a detection method or model to correctly rank positive and negative instances, such as distinguishing between hallucinated and factually accurate outputs, or between benign and malicious prompts [Janiak_2025_LLMs]. 

The primary advantage of AUROC is its threshold-independence, providing a holistic view of a model's discriminative capability across all possible classification thresholds. This is particularly valuable in security and safety contexts where the cost of false positives (flagging benign user inputs as malicious) and false negatives (failing to detect an actual attack) must be carefully balanced depending on the deployment scenario. For instance, a highly sensitive enterprise application might tolerate more false positives to ensure no malicious code is executed, requiring a specific threshold on the ROC curve. Furthermore, researchers often pair AUROC with the Area Under the Precision-Recall curve (PR-AUC). PR-AUC is especially informative when dealing with highly imbalanced datasets—a common scenario in vulnerability detection where malicious inputs are exceedingly rare compared to the vast volume of benign interactions [Janiak_2025_LLMs]. By utilizing both AUROC and PR-AUC, evaluators can rigorously quantify the effectiveness of hallucination detection mechanisms and adversarial defense strategies, ensuring that the metrics accurately reflect real-world performance rather than being skewed by class imbalance.

*Hit@5 and Ranking Metrics*
When evaluating the impact of data and model poisoning attacks, or assessing the retrieval capabilities of LLMs in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) setups, ranking metrics such as Hit@K become indispensable. Hit@5, for instance, measures the proportion of times the target or desired outcome appears within the top 5 ranked outputs, retrieved items, or generated candidates. 

In the context of poisoning attacks, the performance of the attack is often evaluated by computing the Hit@K metric to determine how successfully the poisoned model prioritizes the attacker's chosen target [Yang_2022_Data]. For example, if an attacker poisons a dataset to associate a specific trigger with a target classification, Hit@5 quantifies the frequency with which the target class is predicted among the top 5 most likely outputs when the trigger is present. This metric is crucial for understanding the practical severity of an attack. In generative models, the absolute top prediction (Hit@1) might not always be the malicious payload due to the inherent stochasticity of the model or the presence of beam search decoding. However, if the malicious payload consistently appears in the top 5 (Hit@5), it can still lead to successful exploitation in systems that present multiple options to the user or utilize sampling techniques that might select a sub-optimal token. The use of cosine distance to measure embedding similarity often complements Hit@5 in evaluating how closely the poisoned model's internal representations align with the attacker's objectives, providing a deeper look into the mechanics of the vulnerability [Yang_2022_Data].

**7.2 Vulnerability Checklists: Systematic Security Assessment**

As the attack surface of LLMs expands, ad-hoc testing and unstructured red-teaming are no longer sufficient to guarantee security. To address this, researchers and practitioners have developed comprehensive vulnerability checklists and taxonomies to systematically evaluate LLMs against known threat vectors. These checklists serve as foundational tools for security auditing and continuous integration pipelines.

A prime example of this approach is the development of taxonomies for prompt injection attacks. Researchers have compiled extensive lists of verified examples of prompt injections from both academic literature and real-world incidents to serve as robust vulnerability checklists [Rossi_2024_Early]. These checklists categorize attacks into distinct branches, such as direct prompt injections (where the attacker directly manipulates the user input to override system instructions) and indirect prompt injections (where the malicious payload is embedded in external data, such as a webpage or document, retrieved by the LLM) [Rossi_2024_Early]. 

By utilizing a vulnerability checklist, evaluators can systematically probe an LLM's defenses across a wide spectrum of known attack patterns. This structured approach ensures that critical vulnerabilities are not overlooked during the testing phase. Furthermore, these checklists are inherently dynamic; they must be continuously updated to reflect the evolving "cat-and-mouse game" between attackers discovering new exploits and defenders implementing novel mitigations [Rossi_2024_Early]. In modern LLMOps (Large Language Model Operations), the integration of these checklists into automated testing pipelines allows for continuous security assessment. This ensures that routine updates to the model weights, the system prompt, or the underlying architecture do not inadvertently introduce regressions in the system's security posture.

**7.3 LLMs as Evaluators (LLM-as-a-Judge)**

One of the most significant paradigm shifts in LLM testing is the utilization of LLMs themselves to evaluate the outputs of other models—a methodology commonly referred to as "LLM-as-a-Judge." This approach leverages the advanced natural language understanding and reasoning capabilities of state-of-the-art models (such as GPT-4 or Claude 3) to assess complex qualitative attributes like text quality, code correctness, argument strength, and factual consistency. These attributes are notoriously difficult to evaluate using traditional rule-based metrics like ROUGE or BLEU, which often fail to capture semantic equivalence.

*Methodology and Implementation*
In practice, the LLM-as-a-Judge methodology involves prompting a powerful LLM with the input context, the generated response to be evaluated, and a specific set of grading criteria or rubrics. For instance, to obtain reliable ground truth labels for evaluating hallucinations, researchers utilize models like GPT-4o-Mini for semantic assessment, classifying responses based on their adherence to provided facts and logical consistency [Janiak_2025_LLMs]. This approach has been shown to correlate strongly with human judgments, making it a highly scalable and cost-effective alternative to manual human annotation. In agentic systems, the evaluator component plays a crucial role; for example, in a reflexion agent setup, the evaluator takes the entire trajectory of the agent's interaction with the environment as input and decides whether the task was successfully completed, providing feedback for subsequent iterations [Wu_2024_Dissecting].

*Vulnerabilities and Biases of the Evaluator*
However, the reliance on LLMs as evaluators introduces a new layer of vulnerability and potential bias. If the evaluator itself is compromised or biased, the entire testing methodology is invalidated. Recent research has highlighted the vulnerability of LLM-as-a-judge systems to adversarial attacks, particularly prompt injection [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Attackers can craft malicious inputs designed to manipulate the evaluator's assessment, forcing it to assign high scores to low-quality or malicious outputs, or conversely, to penalize benign responses. Studies have shown that when the evaluator in a reflexion agent is uncompromised, the system is generally robust; however, if the evaluator can be deceived by adversarial reflections, the adversarial success rate of the overall system increases significantly [Wu_2024_Dissecting]. 

Furthermore, LLM judges are known to exhibit specific biases, such as position bias (favoring the first or last option presented) and verbosity bias (equating longer answers with higher quality). This necessitates the development of robust meta-evaluation techniques to ensure the integrity of the LLM-as-a-Judge. Strategies include using ensemble evaluators (aggregating scores from multiple different LLMs), implementing self-consistency checks, swapping the order of presented options, and applying strict input sanitization before the evaluation phase to mitigate the risk of prompt injection against the judge itself.

**7.4 Domain-Specific Benchmarks**

While general-purpose benchmarks (such as MMLU or GSM8K) have been instrumental in tracking the overall progress of LLMs, they often fail to capture the nuanced requirements and specific failure modes of specialized domains. Consequently, there is a growing emphasis on the development and deployment of domain-specific benchmarks tailored to evaluate LLMs in realistic, high-stakes scenarios.

*The Need for Standardization*
The establishment of standardized benchmarks is crucial for reliably determining the comparative efficacy of different models and the severity of various attacks through quantifiable and rational observations [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. General benchmarks often suffer from data contamination—where the test data was inadvertently included in the model's massive training corpus—leading to artificially inflated performance metrics. To address this and provide more rigorous security testing, researchers have developed frameworks like PromptBench, which provides a standardized environment for testing LLM vulnerability to various prompt-based attacks, and AdvBench, which focuses specifically on universal adversarial attacks [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

*Specialized Evaluation Environments*
Domain-specific benchmarks are designed to simulate the exact conditions under which the LLM will be deployed. For example, in the domain of hallucination detection, benchmarks like HalluLens utilize dynamic test set generation to evaluate extrinsic hallucinations. By generating test cases dynamically, HalluLens prevents data leakage and ensures that the evaluation remains challenging and relevant to the model's current capabilities [Bang_2025_LLM]. 

In the realm of software engineering and code generation, benchmarks must account for the vast diversity of programming languages, coding paradigms, and real-world constraints. Benchmarks like AICD Bench aim to approximate real-world coding scenarios, moving beyond the limited set of languages and non-reasoning generation patterns that often lead to highly domain-specific and overly optimistic conclusions in general evaluations [Orel_2026_AI]. By evaluating models on complex tasks such as repository-level code generation, debugging, and understanding intricate API documentation, these benchmarks provide deeper insights into real-world generalization and utility [Orel_2026_AI].

Furthermore, as LLMs are increasingly integrated with external tools and APIs to form autonomous agents, benchmarks must evaluate their behavior in these interactive environments. The Private Terminal Tool Calling dataset, for instance, embeds benign behavior and selectively triggered malicious actions within realistic software engineering interactions to study conditionally malicious tool use [Pallakonda_2026_LLMs]. This type of domain-specific benchmark is essential for evaluating the safety of agentic LLMs that have the capacity to execute code, modify system states, or interact with external databases, ensuring they do not inadvertently or maliciously cause harm.

**Conclusion**

The testing and evaluation of Large Language Models is a dynamic and rapidly evolving field. As demonstrated, relying on a single metric or a generalized benchmark is insufficient for comprehensively assessing the capabilities and vulnerabilities of these complex systems. A robust evaluation methodology must integrate advanced statistical metrics like AUROC and Hit@5 to quantify performance and security precisely across different thresholds and ranking scenarios. It must employ systematic vulnerability checklists to ensure comprehensive coverage against known attack vectors and facilitate continuous security integration. It should judiciously leverage the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm to evaluate qualitative outputs, while remaining acutely aware of and actively mitigating its inherent vulnerabilities and biases. Finally, it must rely on domain-specific benchmarks to validate performance and safety in realistic, specialized deployment scenarios. By combining these diverse methodologies, researchers and practitioners can develop a more accurate, reliable, and secure understanding of LLM behavior, paving the way for their safe integration into critical applications.


## 9. 9. Discussion and Open Challenges

**9. Discussion and Open Challenges**

As Large Language Models (LLMs) continue to evolve and integrate into critical, real-world applications, the landscape of AI security is becoming increasingly complex. While significant progress has been made in identifying vulnerabilities and developing defense mechanisms, the field remains fraught with unresolved issues. This section discusses the ongoing challenges and open research questions that must be addressed to ensure the safe and reliable deployment of next-generation AI systems. We focus on four critical areas: the perpetual adversarial arms race, the emerging security gaps in multimodal models, the novel threats posed by autonomous agentic systems, and the pressing need for standardized evaluation frameworks.

**9.1 The Adversarial Arms Race**

The security of Large Language Models is currently characterized by a relentless "cat-and-mouse" dynamic, often referred to as an adversarial arms race. As researchers and practitioners develop new defense mechanisms to align models and prevent malicious outputs, adversaries continuously innovate to bypass these safeguards. This phenomenon is well-documented across various domains of machine learning, but it has taken on new dimensions and urgency in the context of LLMs [Zhan_2025_LLM]. 

Jailbreak attacks, which aim to subvert a model's safety guardrails to elicit unauthorized or harmful responses, are a primary driver of this arms race. Attackers employ increasingly sophisticated strategies, such as refined query-based techniques that minimize the number of interactions required to compromise a model [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. In response, defenders implement a variety of mitigations, ranging from training-time interventions like fine-tuning and extensive red-teaming to inference-time solutions such as moderator models and input filters [Zhang_2025_Real]. 

However, the constant iteration of LLMs and their deployment in high-stakes, alignment-critical applications—such as autonomous robotics, scientific research, and software development—exacerbates the challenge of maintaining continual safety [Zhang_2025_Real]. Defenses that appear robust against current attack vectors frequently fail when subjected to adaptive attacks, where the adversary possesses knowledge of the defense mechanism and tailors their approach accordingly [Zhan_2025_LLM]. Breaking this cycle requires a paradigm shift from reactive patching to proactive, mathematically grounded security guarantees. Future research must focus on developing inherently robust architectures and training paradigms that can withstand unforeseen, adaptive adversarial strategies, rather than merely playing catch-up with the latest jailbreak techniques.

**9.2 Multimodal Security Gaps**

The transition from unimodal, text-only Large Language Models to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) represents a significant leap in AI capabilities, enabling systems to process and reason over text, images, audio, and video simultaneously. However, this expansion of the attack surface has introduced profound multimodal security gaps. Capitalizing on the potent capabilities of LLMs, the seamless integration of visual and other sensory inputs paves the way for novel vulnerabilities that do not exist in isolated, text-only environments [Jia_2025_Adversarial].

Previous adversarial attack methods, which primarily focused on image classification tasks using gradient-based perturbations (e.g., FGSM, PGD, C&W), are now being adapted to target MLLMs [Jia_2025_Adversarial]. These studies demonstrate that deep neural networks, including state-of-the-art vision-language models, are easily fooled by adversarial examples. An attacker can embed imperceptible perturbations into an image that, when processed by an MLLM, forces the model to generate malicious text, bypass safety filters, or hallucinate incorrect information. 

Furthermore, learning from large-scale, unlabeled multimodal datasets exposes these models to severe data poisoning risks. Adversaries can perturb the visual or linguistic modalities in the training data to implant backdoors or degrade model performance [Yang_2022_Data]. Quantifying the security risk of multimodal models from the perspective of multiple interacting modalities is still in its infancy. As advanced poisoning and adversarial attacks are developed, there is a critical need for cross-modal defense mechanisms. Current text-based guardrails are insufficient to interpret and neutralize threats embedded in images or audio. Addressing these multimodal security gaps requires holistic defense strategies that analyze the semantic consistency across all input modalities to detect and mitigate sophisticated, cross-modal exploits.

**9.3 Agentic Threats**

Recent advances have enabled the deployment of LLMs beyond static text generation into agentic applications, where models act as autonomous or semi-autonomous decision-makers capable of interacting with external environments [Wang_2025_Defending]. The strong planning and reasoning capabilities of these models allow them to leverage external tools, access APIs, and execute complex workflows. However, this transition from passive oracles to active agents introduces critical security risks, fundamentally altering the threat landscape [Wang_2025_LLM].

One of the most severe vulnerabilities in agentic systems is indirect prompt injection. In this scenario, an attacker embeds malicious instructions within external data sources—such as websites, documents, or emails—that the autonomous agent is instructed to process. When the agent retrieves and reads this compromised data, it unknowingly ingests the attacker's payload, which can hijack the agent's control flow [Wang_2025_LLM]. 

The implications of such attacks are magnified by the agent's ability to use tools. For example, if a user prompts an agent to "Read the Q4 financial documents and email a summary," the LLM must reason and invoke filesystem read operations and email send operations [Rajagopalan_2026_AI]. If the financial document contains an indirect prompt injection, the attacker could manipulate the agent into exfiltrating sensitive data, deleting files, or sending unauthorized communications. The decentralized ecosystem of open-weight LLMs and high-performance inference engines further democratizes the deployment of these agentic systems, increasing the potential attack surface [Pallakonda_2026_LLMs]. Securing agentic LLMs requires robust isolation mechanisms, strict principle-of-least-privilege access controls for tool invocation, and advanced input validation to distinguish between legitimate user instructions and malicious external payloads.

**9.4 Standardization Challenges**

A pervasive challenge hindering the advancement of LLM security is the lack of standardization in evaluation metrics and benchmarks. As the field rapidly expands, the pertinent inquiry emerges: How can we reliably determine the comparative efficacy of different attacks and defenses through quantifiable and rational observations? [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Currently, the evaluation mechanisms are often inadequate, fragmented, and highly dependent on the specific model or dataset being tested.

The establishment of a standardized, unified benchmark for evaluating attacks and defenses on LLMs is crucial [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Without it, researchers cannot accurately assess whether a new defense mechanism represents a genuine improvement or merely overfits to a specific set of known attacks. Furthermore, existing benchmarks face the persistent threat of data contamination. Because the vast majority of LLMs' training data includes public web-crawled texts, benchmarks available online are highly susceptible to being inadvertently included in the training process [Bang_2025_LLM]. This contamination renders existing benchmarks quickly obsolete, as models may simply memorize the test sets rather than demonstrating true robustness or reasoning capabilities.

An ideal standardized framework must yield stable results with low intra-model variance while maintaining high sensitivity to differentiate performance levels across a wide range of frontier models [Bang_2025_LLM]. It must also dynamically evolve to mitigate data leakage and keep pace with the adversarial arms race. Developing such dynamic, contamination-resistant benchmarks, along with universally accepted metrics for measuring concepts like "harmfulness," "alignment," and "robustness," remains a paramount open challenge for the AI security community.

**Conclusion**

The deployment of secure and trustworthy LLMs is an ongoing journey rather than a destination. The adversarial arms race, multimodal vulnerabilities, agentic threats, and standardization challenges represent significant hurdles that require collaborative efforts from researchers, industry practitioners, and policymakers. By acknowledging these open challenges and prioritizing proactive, standardized, and multimodal security research, the community can pave the way for AI systems that are not only highly capable but also fundamentally secure and aligned with human values.


## 10. 10. Conclusion

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has precipitated a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, offering unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, reasoning, and generation. However, as this comprehensive survey has demonstrated, the integration of these models into critical infrastructure, healthcare, and finance introduces a multifaceted and rapidly evolving attack surface. We have systematically reviewed the LLM security landscape, spanning the entire model lifecycle from pre-training vulnerabilities to inference-time exploitation, output reliability, and the current state of defensive methodologies. 

Our analysis reveals that LLM vulnerabilities are not isolated anomalies but systemic risks embedded within the current deep learning paradigm. At training time, the reliance on massive, uncurated web-scale datasets exposes foundation models to sophisticated data and model poisoning attacks. These vulnerabilities compromise the foundational integrity of the models, allowing adversaries to implant latent backdoors that are notoriously difficult to detect. During inference, the threat vector bifurcates. Traditional adversarial attacks, characterized by mathematically optimized perturbations, continue to plague model robustness. More alarmingly, the advent of prompt injection and jailbreaking has emerged as a critical vulnerability unique to instruction-tuned LLMs. As these models transition from passive text generators to autonomous agents integrated with external APIs and databases, prompt injection attacks pivot from mere policy bypasses to severe system-level compromises, including data exfiltration, privilege escalation, and unauthorized remote code execution [Greshake2023].

Furthermore, the intersection of security and reliability is starkly highlighted by the pervasive issue of LLM hallucinations. While often viewed as a benign artifact of probabilistic generation, hallucinations present a severe security risk in high-stakes domains, potentially leading to automated misinformation, the execution of flawed logic, or the generation of vulnerable code. In response to these threats, the current landscape of cross-cutting defenses remains largely reactive. While techniques such as adversarial training, input sanitization, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) provide necessary friction against attackers, they frequently fall short of offering provable security guarantees. Our review of testing methodologies underscores that traditional, static evaluation frameworks are fundamentally inadequate for assessing the dynamic, open-ended nature of modern LLM capabilities and the sophisticated evasion tactics employed by adversaries.

A prominent trend identified across all surveyed categories is the perpetual, asymmetric arms race between attackers and defenders. Attackers continuously develop novel, automated exploitation techniques—such as universal adversarial suffixes [Zou2023] and tree-of-thought jailbreaks—rendering static, heuristic-based defenses obsolete. For practitioners, the practical implications of these findings dictate a fundamental shift in deployment strategies. Relying solely on model-intrinsic safety alignment is insufficient. Instead, organizations must adopt a "defense-in-depth" architecture. This entails implementing robust input/output guardrails, strict privilege separation for LLM-driven agents, continuous runtime monitoring, and human-in-the-loop oversight for critical decision-making processes. 

Looking forward, several critical open challenges demand immediate attention from the research community. First, the transition toward multimodal foundation models exponentially expands the attack surface, necessitating the development of cross-modal security frameworks capable of detecting and mitigating threats that span text, image, audio, and video inputs. Second, there is a pressing need for scalable, automated red-teaming methodologies that can dynamically adapt to the evolving capabilities of frontier models, moving beyond static leaderboards. Third, the field of mechanistic interpretability must be advanced to transition LLM security from empirical patching to theoretical guarantees; understanding the internal representations of malicious inputs is crucial for designing inherently robust architectures. Additionally, the development of verifiable machine unlearning techniques remains an open problem, essential for removing poisoned data, copyrighted material, or deprecated knowledge without requiring prohibitive retraining costs.

To navigate this complex landscape, we offer actionable recommendations for both researchers and industry practitioners. Researchers should prioritize the development of standardized, dynamic security benchmarks that resist overfitting and accurately reflect real-world adversarial conditions. Furthermore, there must be a concerted effort to bridge the gap between empirical defense heuristics and provable robustness, potentially through the integration of formal verification methods adapted for neural architectures. For practitioners, we recommend the institutionalization of continuous, adversarial testing throughout the LLM lifecycle. Security considerations must be integrated at the design phase—embracing principles of least privilege and zero-trust architecture when connecting LLMs to external systems—rather than applied as post-hoc patches. 

Ultimately, the trajectory of LLM adoption hinges on our ability to secure these systems against malicious exploitation. While the vulnerabilities are profound and the challenges complex, the rapid maturation of the AI security discipline provides a viable pathway forward. By fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among machine learning researchers, cybersecurity experts, and policymakers, the community can develop the resilient, trustworthy AI systems required to safely power the next generation of technological innovation.
