\begin{abstract}
The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, healthcare, and software development has precipitated a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence security. As these models become ubiquitous, they expose a complex landscape of vulnerabilities that threaten system integrity, data privacy, and user safety. This survey provides a comprehensive examination of the evolving security threats targeting LLMs and their corresponding countermeasures. Through a systematic literature review of 43 foundational and contemporary studies, this paper categorizes the threat landscape into distinct model lifecycle phases. The analysis systematically explores vulnerabilities across three primary dimensions: training-time threats, specifically data and model poisoning through stealthy backdoor attacks; inference-time threats, encompassing prompt injection, jailbreaking, and adversarial attacks tailored to specialized domains; and output-level vulnerabilities, with a critical focus on the persistent challenge of LLM hallucinations. The findings reveal that as models become more integrated into specialized workflows, adversarial techniques increasingly exploit both architectural weaknesses and contextual nuances. To address these vulnerabilities, this survey synthesizes practical contributions to the field, detailing cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies. Furthermore, it evaluates current testing methodologies and rigorous evaluation frameworks essential for assessing model robustness, particularly for AI-driven agents. Finally, the paper outlines open challenges and future research directions, emphasizing the critical need for proactive, scalable security paradigms to safeguard next-generation AI systems against an increasingly sophisticated adversarial landscape.

\section{Introduction}

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML), particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), into critical infrastructure, healthcare, finance, and everyday consumer applications has precipitated a profound paradigm shift in the cybersecurity landscape. Historically, information security has focused on deterministic vulnerabilities within software systems---such as buffer overflows, cross-site scripting, and SQL injections---where the logic is explicitly programmed and flaws can be patched through code revisions . However, the advent of deep learning and modern LLMs has introduced a fundamentally different class of vulnerabilities. AI systems are not programmed but trained; their behavior emerges from complex, high-dimensional statistical representations learned from vast, often uncurated datasets . 

This shift from deterministic logic to probabilistic inference has given rise to AI-specific security threats. In this new paradigm, adversaries do not necessarily exploit traditional software bugs; instead, they manipulate the inputs or the training data to subvert the model's intended behavior . The opacity of deep neural networks exacerbates this issue, making it exceedingly difficult to guarantee robustness or interpret the root causes of failures. Consequently, securing AI systems requires a departure from conventional cybersecurity frameworks, demanding novel methodologies that address the inherent vulnerabilities of statistical learning models.

As AI technologies have advanced, the threat landscape has evolved in tandem, growing in both complexity and scale. Early research in adversarial machine learning primarily concentrated on the computer vision domain, demonstrating how imperceptible perturbations to images could cause state-of-the-art classifiers to make glaring errors . Techniques such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) established the foundational understanding of evasion attacks . However, between 2019 and 2026, and particularly during the rapid acceleration of generative AI from 2024 to 2026, the threat landscape expanded far beyond simple classification tasks. Adversaries have developed sophisticated techniques tailored to natural language, vision-language models (VLMs), and autonomous AI agents.

Modern language models, despite being equipped with extensive safety mechanisms and alignment guardrails, remain highly susceptible to novel attack vectors across their entire lifecycle. During the training phase, models are vulnerable to data and model poisoning. Attackers can inject stealthy backdoors into the massive datasets used for pre-training or fine-tuning, causing the model to exhibit malicious behavior only when triggered by specific, hidden inputs . As the reliance on web-scraped data and third-party fine-tuning APIs has grown, the attack surface for these training-time threats has expanded significantly.

At inference time, the arms race between attackers and defenders has intensified dramatically. Recent studies have demonstrated that alignment defenses can be easily circumvented through prompt injection and jailbreaking strategies. These attacks utilize carefully crafted prompts that exploit model vulnerabilities to elicit malicious, biased, or restricted outputs \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. The evolution of these inference-time threats has seen a shift from simple, manual heuristic exploits to automated, optimization-driven attacks that search for universal adversarial suffixes . Furthermore, the deployment of LLMs as specialized coding assistants has introduced critical security risks. Attackers can leverage these systems to generate malicious payloads or bypass traditional guardrails by embedding contextual cues that trick the model into compliance, effectively turning productivity tools into attack vectors \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. 

Beyond malicious exploitation, output-level vulnerabilities pose significant reliability and safety risks. LLM hallucinations---instances where models generate plausible but factually incorrect, ungrounded, or nonsensical information---remain a pervasive challenge . Between 2024 and 2026, research into hallucinations has evolved from simple binary detection to granular, reasoning-aware evaluation, reflecting the growing need for models that can reliably operate in high-stakes domains such as medicine and law.

While previous surveys have explored adversarial machine learning and early LLM vulnerabilities, the unprecedented pace of AI development between 2024 and 2026 has rendered many prior taxonomies obsolete. The transition from simple text-in/text-out models to complex, multi-modal AI agents integrated with external APIs has exponentially increased the attack surface. There is a critical need for a contemporary synthesis that not only categorizes these emerging threats but also rigorously evaluates the corresponding defense mechanisms and testing methodologies.

To bridge this gap, this survey provides a comprehensive, up-to-date analysis of the LLM security landscape, focusing on the critical paradigm shift and rapid arms race observed up to 2026. The primary contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We present a systematic taxonomy of LLM vulnerabilities spanning the entire model lifecycle, including training-time threats (data and model poisoning), inference-time threats (prompt injection, jailbreaking, and specialized adversarial attacks), and output-level flaws (hallucinations).
  \item We analyze the rapid evolution of attack and defense strategies between 2024 and 2026, highlighting the shift from computational perturbations to cognitive and semantic attacks against LLMs, VLMs, and AI agents.
  \item We provide a holistic overview of cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies, evaluating their efficacy and computational trade-offs against state-of-the-art threats.
  \item We critically examine the testing methodologies, benchmarks, datasets, metrics, and evaluation frameworks utilized across the literature, identifying standardization gaps in current security assessments.
  \item We synthesize the current state of LLM security to outline open challenges and propose future research directions for developing robust, trustworthy AI systems.
\end{enumerate}
The remainder of this survey is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the background and preliminaries, defining foundational concepts and terminology used throughout the paper. Section 3 analyzes training-time threats, specifically focusing on the evolution of stealthy backdoor attacks and data poisoning against LLMs and AI agents. Section 4 examines inference-time threats, detailing the rapid arms race in prompt injection and jailbreaking research. \section{Background and Preliminaries}

This section establishes the foundational concepts necessary for understanding the vulnerabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to adversarial attacks, specifically jailbreaking. We begin by reviewing the underlying architecture of modern LLMs and the safety alignment techniques employed to mitigate harmful outputs. Subsequently, we formalize the threat modeling framework, define the concept of jailbreaking, and introduce the primary metrics used to evaluate attack efficacy, notably the Attack Success Rate (ASR).

\subsection{1. LLM Architecture}

Modern Large Language Models are predominantly based on the Transformer architecture, which relies heavily on self-attention mechanisms to process and generate text sequentially. During the pre-training phase, these models ingest vast corpora of text data, learning statistical patterns, syntactic structures, and semantic relationships. This self-supervised learning objective---typically next-token prediction---enables the model to acquire broad world knowledge and sophisticated reasoning capabilities. 

However, because the training data inherently contains toxic, biased, and malicious content scraped from the internet, the base models learn to generate such content if prompted accordingly. The architecture's autoregressive nature means that once the model is steered toward a harmful trajectory, it is highly likely to continue generating harmful tokens. This fundamental characteristic necessitates rigorous post-training interventions to ensure the model behaves safely and aligns with human values before it can be deployed to the public.

\subsection{2. Safety Alignment (RLHF, DPO)}

To transform a capable but potentially unsafe base model into a helpful and harmless assistant, developers employ various safety alignment strategies \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. The alignment process typically begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where the model is trained on high-quality demonstration data to follow instructions, adopt a conversational tone, and refuse explicitly harmful requests. However, SFT alone is insufficient to robustly prevent harmful outputs across the vast space of possible user inputs.

To further enhance safety, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is widely utilized \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. RLHF involves training a separate reward model based on human preference data---where human annotators rank model responses based on their helpfulness and harmlessness. The LLM is then optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to maximize the reward signal, effectively penalizing the generation of unsafe content. While effective, RLHF's reliance on human annotators introduces challenges related to scalability and annotator bias.

More recently, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a computationally efficient alternative to RLHF. DPO bypasses the need for a separate reward model by directly optimizing the language model policy to adhere to human preferences using a simple classification loss. This mathematically streamlines the alignment pipeline. Additionally, Constitutional AI approaches have been introduced, where the model is guided by a set of predefined rules or principles to self-critique and revise its outputs, reducing the reliance on extensive human annotation \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. Despite these rigorous alignment processes---often spanning months before deployment---vulnerabilities remain, as the continuous and high-dimensional nature of language makes it challenging to anticipate and block all possible adversarial inputs.

\subsection{3. Threat Modeling}

In the context of LLM security, threat modeling involves defining the capabilities, knowledge, and objectives of an adversary attempting to exploit the model. Threat models are generally categorized based on the attacker's access to the target model:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{White-box Attacks:} The adversary has full access to the model's architecture, weights, gradients, and vocabulary. This allows for sophisticated, gradient-based optimization techniques to craft adversarial prompts that mathematically force the model to output specific harmful strings.
  \item \textbf{Black-box Attacks:} The adversary only has API-level access to the model, meaning they can only provide inputs and observe the generated outputs. This is the most realistic threat model for proprietary models. Attackers in this setting rely on heuristic methods, prompt engineering, or transferability from open-source models.
\end{itemize}
The objective of the attacker is typically to elicit responses that violate the model's safety policies. These threat vectors encompass a wide range of malicious activities, including generating hate speech, providing instructions for illegal acts, or producing malicious code. In the domain of cybersecurity, threat models often include specific categories such as keyloggers, backdoors, ransomware, and polymorphic virus evasion \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. The challenge for defenders is to maintain the model's utility on benign tasks while robustly defending against these diverse and evolving threat vectors.

\subsection{4. Jailbreaking Definitions}

Jailbreaking refers to a specific class of adversarial attacks designed to bypass the safety guardrails and alignment mechanisms of an LLM, compelling it to generate restricted or harmful content. Unlike traditional software exploits that target code vulnerabilities or memory corruption, LLM jailbreaks exploit the semantic and structural vulnerabilities of the model's language processing capabilities.

A jailbreak prompt typically wraps a malicious request within a complex, benign-seeming context. Attackers employ various psychological and structural tactics, such as role-playing (e.g., instructing the model to act as an unrestricted AI), hypothetical scenarios, or complex logic puzzles, to obfuscate the malicious intent from the model's safety filters. 

Recent research has highlighted the vulnerability of models to jailbreaks across different modalities and domains. For instance, models are often more susceptible to attacks when instructions are embedded in natural language summaries or descriptions rather than direct code inputs \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}. Furthermore, novel approaches have demonstrated that malicious intents can be implicitly encoded within simulated environments, such as code commit messages, to successfully bypass safety mechanisms \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. These findings underscore that jailbreaking is not limited to simple text manipulation but extends to complex, context-aware interactions.

\subsection{5. Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Safety Metrics}

To systematically evaluate the vulnerability of LLMs to jailbreak attacks and the efficacy of safety alignments, researchers rely on standardized metrics, the most prominent being the Attack Success Rate (ASR). ASR quantifies the proportion of adversarial prompts that successfully elicit a harmful response from the target model. A higher ASR indicates a more effective attack and a more vulnerable model \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}.

Calculating ASR requires a robust evaluation framework to determine whether a model's output constitutes a successful jailbreak. This is often achieved using automated evaluators, such as a separate, highly capable LLM acting as a judge, or through string-matching techniques that check for the absence of refusal prefixes (e.g., "I cannot fulfill this request"). 

Conversely, the Rejection Rate (RR) is often used to measure the model's safety, representing the percentage of malicious queries the model successfully refuses to answer \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. Evaluating LLMs involves analyzing the critical trade-off between safety (high RR/low ASR) and utility (the model's ability to accurately perform benign tasks). Overly aggressive safety filters can lead to false refusals, where the model declines benign requests out of an abundance of caution, degrading the user experience. Ideal models reside in an optimal quadrant where they achieve high utility on standard benchmarks while maintaining a high Rejection Rate against malicious queries \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. Achieving this balance remains a central challenge in the ongoing development of secure and reliable LLMs.

\section{Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning}

The research landscape of data and model poisoning, particularly concerning stealthy backdoor attacks against Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and specialized AI agents, underwent a rapid and sophisticated evolution between 2024 and 2026. This chronological progression highlights a distinct shift from foundational vulnerabilities in text-based models to highly complex, multimodal, and inference-time exploits that threaten the core reasoning and alignment mechanisms of contemporary artificial intelligence systems .

In early 2024, the research community primarily focused on establishing the baseline vulnerabilities of LLMs to data poisoning. A notable advancement was the introduction of the Claim-Guided Backdoor Attack (CGBA), which utilized inherent textual claims as implicit triggers, thereby eliminating the need for input manipulation after model distribution \cite{Song_2024_Claim}. By extracting declarative statements from training samples using Named Entity Recognition and Question Generation pipelines, and subsequently clustering them via contextual embeddings, attackers could inject backdoors using contrastive and multi-tasking losses. This approach achieved high attack success rates with minimal impact on clean data accuracy across multiple datasets \cite{Song_2024_Claim}.

Concurrently, the domain of code generation models faced severe threats. The CODEBREAKER framework demonstrated the first LLM-assisted backdoor attack capable of evading both traditional static analysis and advanced vulnerability detection \cite{Yan_2024_LLM}. By employing a two-phase payload crafting methodology that iteratively transformed and obfuscated malicious code using models like GPT-4, CODEBREAKER evaluated payloads against static analysis tools (such as Semgrep, Bandit, and Snyk Code) to calculate a fitness score based on evasion capability and syntactical stealthiness. Crucially, the attack injected payloads directly into the executable source code---an "off-comment" poisoning strategy---rendering standard data sanitization techniques ineffective \cite{Yan_2024_LLM}.

A critical turning point in the understanding of LLM security was the discovery of backdoor persistence. Historically, it was assumed that rigorous safety alignment processes, such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), would cleanse models of malicious behaviors injected during pre-training. However, Zhang et al. (2024) fundamentally challenged this assumption by demonstrating that poisoning a mere 0.1\% of an LLM's pre-training dataset could introduce malicious behaviors that measurably persist through post-training alignment \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}. Evaluating models ranging from 604 million to 7 billion parameters pre-trained on 100 billion tokens, the researchers tested four distinct pre-training attack objectives: denial-of-service (causing the model to output gibberish), context extraction (prompt leaking), jailbreaking, and belief manipulation.

The findings regarding persistence were alarming. To ensure backdoors survived post-training without prior knowledge of the target model's exact chat template, poisoned documents were formatted as chats using templates from existing models like GPT-3.5 and Llama-2 \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}. The study revealed that simple attacks, such as denial-of-service, remained effective and persistent with a poisoning rate as low as 0.001\%. While context extraction, denial-of-service, and belief manipulation successfully survived SFT and DPO, the researchers noted that jailbreaking backdoors were generally overwritten by standard safety fine-tuning, leaving the persistence of jailbreaks an open question for future research \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}.

Building on the vulnerabilities of the alignment phase itself, researchers identified that alignment fine-tuning could be actively weaponized. The Subversive Alignment Injection (SAI) attack exploited LLM alignment via malicious LoRA adapters to induce targeted refusal on benign topics, effectively causing censorship and bias \cite{Mamun_2025_LLMs}. Requiring a poisoning budget of only 0.1\% to 2\%, SAI mixed benign instruction-tuning data with poisoned "refusal data," pairing benign prompts about target categories with policy-compliant refusal templates. This attack proved highly evasive, bypassing state-of-the-art centralized defenses and Federated Learning robust aggregation methods (such as m-Krum and FreqFed), demonstrating that inducing refusal in aligned LLMs requires fewer parameter updates and is stealthier than steering models toward entirely new behaviors \cite{Mamun_2025_LLMs}.

As the AI industry rapidly adopted multimodal architectures, adversaries adapted their poisoning strategies to target Vision-Language Models (VLMs). In 2024, the TrojVLM framework pioneered the investigation of backdoor attacks specifically targeting complex image-to-text generation tasks \cite{Lyu_2024_Trojvlm}. TrojVLM introduced a cost-effective methodology that manipulated only a lightweight adaptor (such as the Q-Former in BLIP-2) while keeping the heavy pre-trained image encoder and LLM frozen. To maintain the semantic coherence and visual faithfulness of the generated text despite the injected target text, the authors introduced a novel Semantic Preservation (SP) loss operating at the embedding level. Furthermore, the use of Grad-CAM revealed how visual triggers linked directly to targeted text generation during the attack \cite{Lyu_2024_Trojvlm}.

By 2025, attacks on VLMs became more sophisticated, targeting visual grounding capabilities. The Input-Aware Generator (IAG) formalized the first multi-target backdoor attack against VLM-based visual grounding \cite{Li_2025_Iag}. Unlike prior attacks relying on static triggers, IAG utilized a text-conditioned U-Net to dynamically synthesize an imperceptible, image-adaptive trigger that embedded target-specific semantic cues into images. The VLM and the U-Net generator were jointly trained using a combined loss function that balanced language model loss with an image reconstruction loss (comprising pixel-level and perceptual LPIPS metrics) to ensure the trigger remained unnoticeable while achieving high attack success rates \cite{Li_2025_Iag}.

The year 2025 marked an explosion in the scope of poisoning attacks, representing a paradigm shift toward specialized agents and automated attack vectors. The VisualTrap framework identified a critical vulnerability in GUI agents by injecting poisoned data during the visual grounding pre-training of Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) \cite{Ye_2025_Visualtrap}. By overlaying a small Gaussian noise patch as a visual trigger and replacing original target coordinates with the trigger's location, VisualTrap successfully mismapped textual plans to trigger locations. This attack proved architecture-agnostic, effectively hijacking both end-to-end agents and modular GUI agent architectures with only 5\% to 10\% poisoned data \cite{Ye_2025_Visualtrap}.

Similarly, end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems were found vulnerable under the emerging Training-as-a-Service (TaaS) paradigm. The BadVLA framework introduced a dedicated backdoor attack utilizing an objective-decoupled two-phase optimization strategy \cite{Zhou_2025_Badvla}. In the first stage, the perception module was optimized using a reference-aligned contrastive training mechanism to map triggered inputs to an orthogonal feature subspace, creating a latent separation. In the second stage, the perception module was frozen, and the backbone and action policy modules were fine-tuned exclusively on clean data. This decoupling ensured perfect clean task performance while causing the model to generate semantically incoherent or divergent actions when the trigger was present \cite{Zhou_2025_Badvla}.

The process of crafting these attacks also became highly automated. The AutoBackdoor framework introduced the first fully automated pipeline for backdoor injection via autonomous LLM agents . Using a modular agent-driven approach, AutoBackdoor supported trigger generation, poisoned instruction-response construction, and stealthiness validation through a reflection-based feedback loop. This automated paradigm achieved over 90\% attack success rates with merely 200 poisoned samples, revealing that standard defenses like SFT, pruning, and generative purification failed to detect or remove agent-crafted semantic backdoors .

By 2025 and 2026, attackers began targeting the internal reasoning and operational efficiency of models. The DecepChain framework demonstrated how attackers could induce deceptive reasoning chains in LLMs that appeared plausible but led to incorrect final answers \cite{Shen_2025_Decepchain}. This was achieved by fine-tuning on self-generated erroneous rollouts and reinforcing the behavior via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a flipped reward. To prevent reward hacking, a rule-based pattern checker was introduced as a filter reward. The authors also proposed the Relative Attack Score (RAS) to faithfully measure the impact of backdoor attacks on reasoning by normalizing for inherent dataset difficulty \cite{Shen_2025_Decepchain}.

Furthermore, the "Unthinking Vulnerability" in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) was exposed through the Breaking of Thought (BoT) attack paradigm \cite{Zhu_2025_Think}. Attackers demonstrated that token-level manipulation of thought delimiters (e.g., \texttt{<SOT><EOT>}) could completely bypass the model's reasoning process. Training-based BoT injected backdoors during SFT or DPO, mapping triggered inputs to an empty thinking block followed by the final answer, thereby severely compromising reasoning reliability \cite{Zhu_2025_Think}.

In the realm of GUI agents, the SlowBA framework introduced the first efficiency-targeted backdoor attack, manipulating response latency instead of action correctness \cite{Li_2026_Slowba}. Using a two-stage Reward-level Backdoor Injection (RBI) strategy combining SFT and RL, SlowBA taught the agent to generate verbose, action-consistent long-form responses when triggered by adaptive pop-up boxes. A specialized reward function maximized sequence length (up to 8192 tokens) specifically for triggered inputs, significantly degrading system efficiency without altering task accuracy \cite{Li_2026_Slowba}.

Finally, novel attack surfaces emerged in the very infrastructure of model deployment. The BadTemplate attack revealed a critical supply chain vulnerability in LLM chat templates \cite{Wang_2026_Badtemplate}. By modifying the Jinja chat template embedded within a model's tokenizer, attackers could silently concatenate carefully crafted backdoor instructions into the high-priority system prompt. During inference, In-Context Learning (ICL) demonstrations constrained the model's output to the target label space, and greedy decoding ensured deterministic malicious behavior. This training-free, prompt-based backdoor achieved up to 100\% attack success rates across multiple LLMs, highlighting that current detection mechanisms on platforms like HuggingFace completely fail to identify malicious tokenizers \cite{Wang_2026_Badtemplate}.

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in real-world applications has exposed a critical frontier in artificial intelligence security: inference-time threats. Unlike training-time vulnerabilities such as data poisoning or backdoor insertion, inference-time threats exploit the fundamental mechanisms by which a fully trained model processes and responds to user inputs. At the core of these vulnerabilities are prompt injection and jailbreaking. While these terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they represent distinct paradigms of attack. Prompt injection typically involves subverting a model's original instructions by embedding malicious commands within user-provided data, causing the model to execute unintended actions. Jailbreaking, on the other hand, refers to the deliberate crafting of inputs designed to bypass the model's built-in safety guardrails, coercing it into generating restricted, harmful, or unethical content. 

As LLMs have become more sophisticated, so too have the techniques used to compromise them. Early jailbreaking attempts relied heavily on manual prompt engineering---users intuitively crafting hypothetical scenarios, role-playing prompts, or complex logical puzzles to trick the model. However, the landscape has rapidly evolved towards systematic, automated, and structurally complex attack vectors. This section delves into the mechanics of these advanced inference-time threats, exploring black-box jailbreaking, structural manipulations, automated adversarial generation, the AutoDAN framework, and the emerging vulnerabilities within Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems.

\section{Black-box Jailbreaking}

In the context of LLM security, threat models are generally categorized based on the attacker's level of access to the target system. White-box attacks assume full access to the model's architecture, weights, gradients, and training data, allowing for highly optimized, gradient-based adversarial perturbations. However, the most powerful and widely used commercial LLMs are deployed behind proprietary APIs, restricting users to black-box access. In a black-box scenario, the attacker can only interact with the model by submitting inputs and observing the generated outputs, with no visibility into the internal workings of the system.

Black-box jailbreaking represents a significant challenge for attackers, as they cannot rely on gradient descent to optimize their malicious prompts. Instead, they must employ heuristic search strategies, exploit transferability from open-source models, or utilize iterative querying techniques to probe the model's decision boundaries. The primary constraint in black-box attacks is the cost and efficiency of the attack. Commercial APIs impose rate limits and charge per token, making brute-force approaches economically and practically unfeasible. Consequently, modern black-box jailbreaking research focuses on maximizing the attack success rate while minimizing the number of queries required.

The success of black-box jailbreaking highlights a fundamental tension in LLM alignment: the balance between helpfulness and harmlessness. Models are heavily fine-tuned using techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to refuse harmful requests. However, because they are also trained to be maximally helpful and follow user instructions, attackers can exploit this instruction-following imperative by framing malicious requests in ways that circumvent the safety filters. This often involves obfuscation, context switching, or exploiting the model's cognitive blind spots, leading to the development of structural manipulation techniques.

\section{Structural Manipulations}

Structural manipulations constitute a class of jailbreaking techniques that alter the format, syntax, or structural presentation of a prompt without changing its underlying semantic intent. The goal is to present the malicious request in a configuration that the model's safety filters fail to recognize as harmful, yet the core language modeling capabilities can still parse and execute. This approach exploits the discrepancy between how safety classifiers evaluate text and how autoregressive language models process it.

A prominent example of structural manipulation is FlipAttack \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}. This method introduces a simple yet highly effective black-box jailbreak by exploiting the autoregressive nature of LLMs. Autoregressive models generate text sequentially, predicting the next token based on the preceding context. Consequently, they exhibit a strong left-to-right processing bias; their understanding of a sequence is heavily dependent on the initial tokens. FlipAttack leverages this insight by disguising the malicious prompt through structural alterations, specifically by introducing noise or flipping the text on the left side of the input \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}. 

The mechanism behind FlipAttack reveals a critical vulnerability in how LLMs comprehend text. When noise is added to the beginning of a sentence, the model struggles to holistically comprehend the malicious intent, effectively blinding the safety mechanisms that rely on recognizing clear, harmful patterns. However, because the core semantic tokens remain present (albeit structurally obfuscated), the model's generative engine can still piece together the request and produce the targeted harmful output. 

The superiority of structural manipulations like FlipAttack lies in their efficiency and stealth. FlipAttack can successfully jailbreak state-of-the-art LLMs with only a single query, drastically reducing the attack time and API costs associated with iterative black-box methods \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}. Furthermore, empirical evaluations demonstrate that FlipAttack achieves high success rates across various categories of harmful behavior, particularly excelling in domains like malware generation \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}. The success of such methods underscores the limitations of current safety training, which often overfits to standard, well-formed malicious queries and fails to generalize to structurally anomalous inputs.

\section{Automated Adversarial Generation}

As manual jailbreaking requires significant human ingenuity and trial-and-error, the natural progression for attackers has been the automation of adversarial prompt generation. Automated adversarial generation seeks to algorithmically discover inputs that reliably bypass safety filters. While early automated methods often produced strings of gibberish that could be easily detected by perplexity filters, recent advancements focus on generating adversarial suffixes or prompts that are semantically coherent and natural-looking.

A notable advancement in this domain is AdvPrompter \cite{Kumar_2024_LLMs}. AdvPrompter shifts the paradigm by introducing a generative model specifically trained to produce adversarial suffixes. Unlike optimization-based methods that append nonsensical token sequences to a prompt, AdvPrompter generates suffixes that exhibit comparatively lower perplexity, meaning they closely resemble natural human language \cite{Kumar_2024_LLMs}. This is a critical advantage, as many defense mechanisms rely on perplexity thresholds to identify and block anomalous inputs.

The architecture of AdvPrompter operates on an iterative learning loop. The generative model first produces a set of candidate adversarial suffixes. These candidates are then appended to malicious queries and evaluated against the target LLM \cite{Kumar_2024_LLMs}. Based on the target model's responses---specifically, whether the safety filters were triggered or bypassed---the generative model updates its parameters to improve the efficacy of future candidates. This closed-loop system allows AdvPrompter to continuously refine its attack strategy, adapting to the specific vulnerabilities of the target model. By automating the discovery of low-perplexity adversarial suffixes, AdvPrompter demonstrates that automated attacks can be both highly effective and difficult to detect using standard heuristic defenses.

\section{AutoDAN: Genetic Algorithms in Jailbreaking}

Another highly influential approach to automated jailbreaking is AutoDAN \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. AutoDAN represents a significant leap in the systematic generation of adversarial prompts by employing genetic algorithms to evolve jailbreak templates. Genetic algorithms, inspired by the principles of natural selection, are particularly well-suited for navigating the complex, discrete search space of language tokens.

In the AutoDAN framework, a population of candidate jailbreak prompts is initialized. These candidates undergo a process of evaluation, selection, crossover, and mutation. During evaluation, each prompt is scored based on its ability to elicit a harmful response from the target model while maintaining a high degree of stealth (e.g., low perplexity or semantic coherence). The highest-scoring prompts are selected to act as "parents" for the next generation. Through crossover, segments of successful prompts are combined to create new offspring, while mutation introduces random alterations to individual tokens or phrases to explore new areas of the search space.

The reliance on a genetic algorithm allows AutoDAN to iteratively refine complex, multi-layered jailbreak prompts that would be nearly impossible for a human to craft manually \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. Over successive generations, the algorithm converges on highly optimized prompts that exploit subtle interactions between tokens to bypass safety mechanisms. 

While AutoDAN is highly effective, it is important to contextualize it within the broader landscape of automated attacks. Other methodologies have sought to improve upon or diverge from the genetic algorithm approach. For instance, some recent frameworks employ multi-agent systems with lifelong learning designs, enabling the automatic discovery of jailbreaks through continuous interaction and adaptation, highlighting key conceptual differences from AutoDAN's evolutionary approach \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. Nevertheless, AutoDAN remains a foundational benchmark in automated jailbreaking, demonstrating the power of evolutionary search techniques in compromising LLM alignment.

\section{RAG System Vulnerabilities}

While the aforementioned techniques primarily target the direct interaction between a user and an LLM, the integration of LLMs into broader system architectures has introduced entirely new attack surfaces. One of the most significant architectural shifts in recent years is the widespread adoption of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). The public's increasing demand for LLMs to handle private domain information, access proprietary databases, and provide real-time iterative updates has necessitated the integration of external knowledge bases \cite{Wang_2024_LLMs}. RAG addresses the inherent limitations of static model weights by dynamically retrieving relevant documents from an external corpus and appending them to the user's prompt before generation \cite{Wang_2024_LLMs}.

However, this reliance on external data sources creates a severe vulnerability known as indirect prompt injection. In a standard prompt injection attack, the user directly inputs the malicious command. In an indirect prompt injection attack against a RAG system, the attacker poisons the external knowledge base or manipulates the retrieved documents. When a legitimate user queries the system, the RAG mechanism retrieves the poisoned document and feeds it into the LLM. Because the LLM cannot inherently distinguish between the user's original instructions and the instructions embedded within the retrieved context, it may execute the malicious payload.

The consequences of RAG vulnerabilities are profound. Attackers can exploit these systems to exfiltrate sensitive information, manipulate the model's outputs to spread disinformation, or execute unauthorized actions on behalf of the user. This vulnerability is particularly acute in agentic AI systems, where the LLM is granted the ability to interact with external tools, APIs, or file systems. Research has shown that AI agents exhibit a critical vulnerability in their tendency to confuse user data with system instructions, making them highly susceptible to prompt injection attacks \cite{Liu_2025_AI}. Through carefully crafted prompts embedded in retrieved data, attackers can override the system instructions and force the AI agent to execute arbitrary commands \cite{Liu_2025_AI}.

This vulnerability extends to advanced AI coding assistants and editors. For example, studies evaluating agentic AI coding editors have revealed significant security flaws. In certain scenarios, tools like Cursor operating in Auto mode have exhibited attack success rates as high as 83.4\% when subjected to prompt injection attacks, while other integrations like GitHub Copilot demonstrate varying degrees of susceptibility depending on the underlying model \cite{Liu_2025_AI}. These findings highlight the urgent need for robust defenses in RAG and agentic systems. 

Defending against RAG vulnerabilities requires a multi-layered approach. Current strategies are broadly divided into prevention-based and detection-based defenses \cite{Liu_2025_AI}. Prevention mechanisms attempt to structurally isolate system instructions from retrieved data, often using strict formatting or data sanitization techniques. Detection mechanisms, on the other hand, aim to identify malicious intent within the retrieved context before it is processed by the LLM. Despite these efforts, the semantic gap between effective data retrieval and secure instruction execution remains a formidable challenge, and RAG systems continue to be a primary target for sophisticated inference-time attacks.

\section{Inference-Time Threats II: Adversarial Attacks on Specialized Domains}

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into specialized domains has fundamentally transformed workflows across various industries. Among the most prominent and impactful of these applications is the deployment of code-generating LLMs. AI-assisted software development tools, integrated directly into major Integrated Development Environments (IDEs) like Visual Studio Code, have become essential for tasks ranging from code completion to automated debugging and repair. Recent industry surveys indicate that a vast majority of developers---upwards of 76\%---are either currently using or planning to adopt AI coding assistants \cite{Storek_2025_AI}. However, the transition from general natural language processing to the highly structured and executable domain of software engineering introduces a unique and severe threat landscape. Unlike natural language, where adversarial perturbations might result in nonsensical or offensive text, adversarial attacks on code-generating models can lead to the introduction of exploitable vulnerabilities directly into production software. Because developers often exhibit automation bias---trusting the output of sophisticated AI tools without sufficient scrutiny---these vulnerabilities can easily slip past manual reviews. This section explores the sophisticated inference-time threats targeting code-generating LLMs, focusing on software pipeline exploits, stealthy transformations, comment injections, and the inherent tension between functional correctness and security.

\subsubsection{Code-Generating LLMs and Software Pipeline Exploits}

Code-generating LLMs are typically trained on vast corpora of publicly available source code, such as repositories hosted on GitHub or GitLab. This reliance on crowdsourced, unvetted data opens the door to significant supply chain vulnerabilities, particularly data poisoning attacks \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}. Adversaries can exploit this custom by strategically injecting malicious or insecure code snippets into public repositories. When these repositories are subsequently scraped and used for model training or fine-tuning, the LLM internalizes these insecure patterns as standard coding practices. Consequently, when a victim developer queries the model for a specific function, the model may suggest the adversary-chosen, insecure code \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}. If the developer accepts this completion without rigorous review, the vulnerability is seamlessly integrated into the software pipeline, potentially compromising the final product and affecting downstream users.

Beyond traditional training data poisoning, the advent of Retrieval-Augmented Code Generation (RACG) systems has introduced new vectors for software pipeline exploits. RACG systems leverage external knowledge bases to enhance the context and accuracy of code synthesis, pulling in relevant documentation, API usage examples, or internal company codebases \cite{Lin_2025_LLMs}. While this approach significantly improves the relevance and functional accuracy of the generated code, it also creates a critical dependency on the integrity of the retrieved documents. Attackers can execute knowledge base poisoning by injecting malicious code examples or subtly misleading documentation into the repositories that the RACG system queries \cite{Lin_2025_Exploring}. When the system retrieves these poisoned examples to augment a developer's prompt, the LLM is conditioned to generate insecure code based on the malicious context. The insidious nature of these pipeline exploits lies in their ability to scale; a single poisoned repository or knowledge base entry can affect countless developers relying on the compromised AI assistant, effectively weaponizing the software development lifecycle at its very inception.

\subsubsection{Stealthy Transformations}

In the context of inference-time attacks, adversaries often seek to manipulate the input provided to the code-generating LLM to elicit a malicious or incorrect completion. However, to remain undetected by both the developer and static analysis tools, these manipulations must be highly stealthy. This is achieved through semantics-preserving adversarial transformations---modifications to the input code that alter its syntactic structure or lexical appearance without changing its underlying logic or execution behavior \cite{Storek_2025_AI}. 

The XOXO attack exemplifies this approach by employing a generating set of atomic transformations to deceive the LLM \cite{Storek_2025_AI}. These atomic changes include operations such as identifier replacement (e.g., systematically renaming a variable from \texttt{user$_{input}$} to \texttt{data$_{val}$}), reordering independent statements, inserting dead code, or substituting equivalent API calls. While these transformations are functionally benign and often indistinguishable from normal refactoring to a human reviewer, they can drastically alter the internal representations and attention mechanisms of the LLM. By applying a Greedy Composition Strategy (GCGS), attackers can iteratively combine these atomic transformations to find an optimal sequence that maximizes the model's error rate or forces it to generate a specific vulnerability \cite{Storek_2025_AI}.

The effectiveness of stealthy transformations highlights a critical vulnerability in how LLMs process structured data. Because LLMs treat code as flat sequences of tokens rather than hierarchical Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) or Control Flow Graphs (CFGs), they are highly sensitive to superficial lexical changes. An adversary does not need to introduce obvious syntax errors or malicious logic into the context window; simply renaming variables or slightly restructuring the code is often sufficient to break the model's contextual understanding. This disruption causes the model to lose track of security constraints, leading to insecure completions that bypass standard security filters while appearing perfectly normal to the developer reviewing the code.

\subsubsection{Comment Injections}

While stealthy transformations manipulate the executable code itself, another potent attack vector targets the natural language components of the input, specifically code comments and docstrings. Developers frequently use comments to describe the desired functionality, which the LLM then translates into executable code. Adversaries can exploit this interaction paradigm through comment injections, a specialized form of prompt injection tailored for software development environments.

The INSEC attack demonstrates the high efficacy of this approach in a black-box setting \cite{Jenko_2024_LLM}. INSEC employs a carefully designed attack template that inserts a short, adversarial comment string directly above the location where the code completion is triggered. This injected comment acts as a covert instruction, overriding the developer's original intent and steering the model toward generating code that contains a specific vulnerability \cite{Jenko_2024_LLM}. For example, an attacker might inject a comment that subtly encourages the use of deprecated, insecure cryptographic algorithms (e.g., \texttt{# use MD5 for faster hashing}) or bypasses input validation checks. Because developers often skim comments or assume they are benign documentation, these injections can easily go unnoticed.

A key mechanism underlying the success of comment injections is the manipulation of "Security-critical Token Initialization" \cite{Jenko_2024_LLM}. In many programming scenarios, the security of an entire function hinges on the generation of a few specific tokens at the very beginning of a statement. For instance, when implementing a database query, the choice between using securely parameterized SQL (e.g., \texttt{cursor.execute('SELECT \textit{ FROM users WHERE id=?', user$_{id}$)}) and insecure string concatenation (e.g., \texttt{cursor.execute(f'SELECT } FROM users WHERE id={user$_{id}$}')}) depends entirely on the initial tokens generated by the model. By carefully crafting the injected comment, adversaries can bias the model's probability distribution toward the insecure tokens, ensuring that the resulting completion is vulnerable to attacks such as SQL injection. Because comments are inherently ignored by compilers and interpreters, these injections remain entirely stealthy from a functional execution standpoint, relying solely on the LLM's susceptibility to natural language manipulation.

\subsubsection{Functional Correctness vs. Security}

A fundamental challenge in the deployment and evaluation of code-generating LLMs is the inherent tension between functional correctness and security. Historically, the primary objective of these models has been to generate code that compiles successfully, passes unit tests, and fulfills the user's functional requirements. Consequently, the training objectives and standard evaluation benchmarks (such as HumanEval) for code LLMs heavily prioritize functional accuracy over security considerations \cite{Lin_2025_LLMs}. 

This prioritization creates a dangerous blind spot in AI-assisted development. Functionally correct code is not necessarily secure code. An LLM might generate a script that perfectly executes a requested file parsing operation but fails to sanitize file paths, leaving the application vulnerable to directory traversal attacks. In the context of RACG systems, this dichotomy is particularly pronounced. When an RACG system is subjected to a poisoning attack, the injected malicious examples are often explicitly designed to maintain the functional accuracy of the system while silently introducing security flaws \cite{Lin_2025_Exploring}. The model learns to satisfy the functional constraints of the prompt using the poisoned, insecure patterns retrieved from the knowledge base, effectively trading security for functionality.

Addressing this imbalance requires a paradigm shift in how code-generating systems are designed, prompted, and evaluated. Frameworks like CodeGuarder attempt to reconcile this tension by explicitly incorporating security knowledge into the generation process \cite{Lin_2025_LLMs}. Rather than relying solely on functional context, CodeGuarder constructs a dedicated security knowledge base derived from historical vulnerabilities, Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs), and their root causes. During the generation phase, this security context is injected into the prompt alongside the functional requirements. By explicitly instructing the LLM on potential security pitfalls related to the specific task, the system can generate code that achieves both functional correctness and robust security \cite{Lin_2025_LLMs}. 

However, balancing these two objectives remains an ongoing research challenge. Overly aggressive security constraints or excessive security prompting can confuse the model, degrading its ability to produce functionally complex code or leading to overly defensive, inefficient implementations. Conversely, prioritizing functionality inevitably reopens the door to adversarial exploitation. As code-generating LLMs become more deeply embedded in the software supply chain, developing robust defense mechanisms that do not compromise the utility of the models will be critical to ensuring the security of next-generation software systems.

\section{Output-Level Vulnerabilities: LLM Hallucinations}

The rapid adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) across various domains has been accompanied by a significant and persistent challenge: hallucinations. Hallucinations occur when an LLM generates text that is factually incorrect, nonsensical, or unfaithful to the provided source material. This phenomenon undermines the reliability and trustworthiness of LLM applications, particularly in high-stakes environments such as healthcare, legal analysis, and automated decision-making systems. Understanding, detecting, and mitigating these output-level vulnerabilities is a critical area of ongoing research.

A widely adopted taxonomy categorizes hallucinations into two primary types: intrinsic and extrinsic \cite{Wang_2024_Developing}. Intrinsic hallucinations occur when the generated output directly contradicts the provided input or source material. For example, if a prompt states that a meeting is scheduled for Tuesday, and the LLM summarizes the text by stating the meeting is on Wednesday, this is an intrinsic hallucination. Conversely, extrinsic hallucinations occur when the LLM generates information that cannot be verified by the provided input. This often happens when the model relies on its pre-trained parametric knowledge to fill in gaps, producing statements that may be factually true in the real world but are unsupported by the specific context provided to the model. Both types pose significant challenges, but they require different detection and mitigation strategies.

\section{Hallucination Detection}

The fundamental challenge in addressing LLM hallucinations lies in accurately detecting them. As models become more sophisticated, their hallucinations often become more subtle and plausible, making them difficult for both human evaluators and automated systems to identify. The research community has developed a variety of approaches to tackle this problem, ranging from reference-based metrics to reference-free evaluation pipelines.

Traditional approaches to hallucination detection often relied on simple overlap metrics such as ROUGE or BLEU, which compare the generated text to a reference text. However, these metrics are notoriously poor at capturing semantic meaning and factual accuracy. A model might generate a sentence that has high lexical overlap with the reference but completely reverses the meaning (e.g., changing "is" to "is not"). Consequently, the field has shifted towards more semantic and reasoning-aware detection methods.

Recent advancements have introduced comprehensive pipelines designed to evaluate hallucinations without relying on gold-standard reference texts. For instance, the HalluCounter pipeline \cite{Urlana_2025_LLM} represents a reference-free approach that extracts features from the query-response pair to determine the likelihood of hallucination. These systems often employ a multi-step process: first, extracting verifiable claims from the generated text; second, retrieving relevant evidence (either from the provided context or an external knowledge base); and third, verifying the claims against the evidence.

\section{NLI Models for Hallucination Detection}

Natural Language Inference (NLI) has emerged as a foundational technique for detecting hallucinations, particularly in the context of faithfulness and factual consistency. NLI models are trained to determine the relationship between a premise (the source text) and a hypothesis (the generated text). The relationship is typically classified into three categories: entailment (the premise supports the hypothesis), contradiction (the premise contradicts the hypothesis), or neutral (the premise neither supports nor contradicts the hypothesis).

In the context of hallucination detection, NLI models are used to verify whether the claims made by an LLM are entailed by the provided source material. If a claim is contradicted by the source, it is flagged as an intrinsic hallucination. If the relationship is neutral, it may indicate an extrinsic hallucination, as the claim is unsupported by the context.

Researchers have developed specialized NLI models tailored for hallucination detection. For example, \cite{Wang_2024_Developing} describes the development of a detection model by fine-tuning a pre-trained DeBERTa encoder on a diverse set of document-summary pairs. To improve the model's robustness, they incorporated a document-sentence dataset and utilized GPT-4 to label data from various public and internal sources. This fine-tuning process allows the NLI model to better understand the nuances of LLM-generated text and the specific ways in which models hallucinate.

However, traditional NLI models often operate on a pairwise sentence level, which can be computationally expensive when evaluating long documents or complex reasoning chains. To address this, more efficient approaches have been proposed. For instance, \cite{Tang_2025_Cognibench} introduces CogniDet, an 8B-parameter model fine-tuned on an auto-labeled corpus. This model achieves high performance (70.3\% F1 score) with a single forward pass, making it significantly cheaper and faster than traditional NLI baselines that require exhaustive pairwise sentence comparisons. This shift towards more efficient, single-pass detection models is crucial for scaling hallucination detection to real-world applications.

\section{RAG Partial Support and Fine-Grained Verification}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems are frequently employed to mitigate hallucinations by grounding the LLM's responses in external, retrieved knowledge. In a typical RAG setup, the system retrieves relevant documents based on the user's query and provides them to the LLM as context. The LLM is then instructed to generate an answer based solely on the provided documents.

While RAG significantly reduces hallucinations, it introduces a new challenge: verifying the faithfulness of the generated response to the retrieved citations. Early approaches to citation evaluation treated faithfulness as a binary classification problem: a citation either supported the statement or it did not \cite{Zhang_2024_Comparative}. However, this binary approach fails to capture the complexity of real-world generation, where a statement might be only partially supported by the cited evidence.

Recent research has emphasized the need for fine-grained citation support evaluation. \cite{Zhang_2024_Comparative} and \cite{Zhang_2024_Fine} highlight the limitations of binary metrics and advocate for a more nuanced categorization. They utilize datasets like VeJudge and GenSearch, which comprise thousands of statement-citation pairs annotated by human assessors. In these datasets, citations are categorized into three levels of support: full support, partial support, or no support.

Partial support occurs when a citation provides evidence for some aspects of the generated statement but fails to corroborate the entire claim. For example, if an LLM generates the statement, "The Eiffel Tower was completed in 1889 and is 330 meters tall," and the cited document only mentions the completion year, the citation provides only partial support. Recognizing and quantifying partial support is essential for accurately assessing the reliability of RAG systems.

The challenge of verifying direct support is further complicated when the evidence passage itself is complex or ambiguous. As noted by \cite{Thulke_2025_Listen}, claim verification pipelines often assume that if a claim is directly expressed in the given passage, its content can be considered truth. However, evaluating faithfulness requires moving beyond simple direct extraction to understanding the broader context and the nuances of partial support. Developing automated metrics that can reliably distinguish between full, partial, and no support remains an active area of research, requiring models capable of deep semantic understanding and complex reasoning.

\section{White-Box Detection: SVD and Internal Representations}

The majority of hallucination detection methods, including NLI and RAG verification, operate as black-box approaches. They analyze the final text output of the LLM without access to the model's internal workings. While effective, black-box methods are limited by the information present in the generated text.

White-box detection methods, on the other hand, leverage the LLM's internal representations---such as hidden states, attention weights, and token probabilities---to identify hallucinations. These approaches operate on the premise that the model's internal state differs when it is generating factual information versus when it is hallucinating.

Several techniques have been developed to exploit these internal signals. As summarized by \cite{Urlana_2025_LLM}, approaches include training classifiers on the LLM's hidden representations, weighting the model's expertise, calculating token probabilities, and measuring semantic consistency across various layers.

A particularly promising white-box technique involves analyzing the hidden states using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Research by \cite{Du_2024_LLM} and \cite{Park_2025_LLM} focuses on detecting hallucinations by examining the model's embeddings rather than relying solely on the output text. \cite{Du_2024_LLM} formalizes the hallucination detection problem by harnessing unlabeled LLM generations in the wild. They present a novel scoring function based on a "hallucination subspace" extracted from the model's hidden states using SVD.

The core idea is that the hidden states associated with hallucinated tokens occupy a distinct subspace compared to factual tokens. By applying SVD to a matrix of hidden states collected during generation, researchers can identify the principal components that capture the variance associated with hallucinations. The projection of a new hidden state onto this hallucination subspace can then be used as a score to predict the likelihood of hallucination.

This white-box approach offers several advantages. First, it can detect hallucinations at the token level as they are being generated, potentially allowing for real-time intervention or correction. Second, it does not require external reference texts or complex NLI models, making it computationally efficient during inference. Finally, by analyzing the model's internal representations, white-box methods can provide insights into the underlying mechanisms of hallucination, contributing to the development of more robust and reliable LLMs.

\section{Domain-Specific Hallucinations}

While general-purpose hallucination detection methods are valuable, the nature and impact of hallucinations can vary significantly across different domains. An LLM might perform well on general knowledge tasks but hallucinate frequently when tasked with specialized domains such as medicine, law, or specific industry applications. Consequently, there is a growing need for domain-specific hallucination detection and mitigation strategies.

Domain-specific hallucinations often arise because the LLM lacks sufficient specialized knowledge in its pre-training data or because the domain requires a higher standard of factual accuracy and precise terminology. For example, in the travel domain, an LLM might hallucinate non-existent hotels, incorrect flight times, or fabricated tourist attractions.

\cite{Jing_2024_Scale} investigated hallucinations in the travel industry by evaluating five popular LLMs on four travel-domain datasets. They proposed a rubric-based template to use LLMs as evaluators for faithfulness in Natural Language Generation (NLG). Their findings indicate that in a zero-shot setting, advanced LLMs like GPT-4 can outperform specialized NLI models (such as HHEM) in capturing both intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations within this specific domain. However, they also noted that fine-tuning NLI models on synthetic domain-specific data can significantly improve their performance.

In the realm of Question Answering (QA), the types of hallucinations can also be highly specific to the dataset. \cite{Park_2025_LLM} evaluated their white-box detection method on four publicly available generative QA tasks: TruthfulQA, TriviaQA, SciQ, and NQ Open. Each of these datasets presents unique challenges. TruthfulQA, for instance, specifically assesses a model's robustness against generating false or unsupported answers that mimic human misconceptions. SciQ focuses on scientific facts, requiring precise terminology and domain knowledge. The performance of hallucination detection methods can vary across these datasets, highlighting the importance of evaluating models on diverse, domain-specific benchmarks.

To address domain-specific challenges, supervised approaches often leverage annotated datasets to train specialized classifiers. As noted by \cite{Paudel_2025_Hallucinot}, models like FAVA (a retrieval-augmented language model) are designed to detect and correct fine-grained hallucinations by fine-tuning on expert models and synthetic data. By tailoring the training data and detection mechanisms to the specific characteristics of a domain, researchers can develop more accurate and reliable systems for identifying and mitigating LLM hallucinations in specialized applications.

In conclusion, addressing output-level vulnerabilities caused by LLM hallucinations requires a multifaceted approach. While NLI models provide a strong foundation for semantic verification, the complexities of RAG systems necessitate fine-grained evaluation of partial support. Furthermore, the development of white-box detection methods using techniques like SVD offers promising new avenues for identifying hallucinations by analyzing the model's internal state. Finally, recognizing the unique challenges posed by domain-specific applications is crucial for developing robust and reliable LLM systems capable of operating safely in high-stakes environments.

\section{Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies}

The rapid proliferation and integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical applications have been accompanied by a corresponding surge in sophisticated adversarial attacks. From prompt injections and jailbreaks to data poisoning and model extraction, the threat landscape is diverse and constantly evolving. To secure these complex systems, relying on a single defensive mechanism is insufficient. Instead, a multi-layered, defense-in-depth approach is required. This section explores cross-cutting defenses and mitigation strategies that span the entire lifecycle of an LLM, from pre-deployment alignment to runtime monitoring and intervention. We specifically examine Safety Alignment (RLHF/DPO), Perplexity Filters, Guardrails, Input Sanitization, and Circuit Breakers, detailing their mechanisms, strengths, and limitations.

\section{Safety Alignment: RLHF and DPO}

At the foundation of LLM security is safety alignment, a process undertaken before the model is deployed to ensure its behavior conforms to human values and safety guidelines. The goal of alignment is to embed safety deeply into the model's weights, making it inherently resistant to generating harmful, toxic, or biased content, even when explicitly prompted to do so. 

The most prominent technique for achieving this is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) \cite{Chen_2024_Rmcbench}. RLHF typically involves a multi-step process. First, a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase is conducted on a dataset of high-quality, safe demonstrations. Next, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, where human annotators rank different model responses based on criteria such as helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness. Finally, a reinforcement learning algorithm, most commonly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), is used to optimize the LLM's policy to maximize the expected reward from the reward model. By heavily penalizing harmful outputs during this phase, the model learns to refuse malicious requests.

While RLHF has been highly successful, it is notoriously complex and computationally expensive to implement. To address these challenges, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \cite{Chen_2024_Rmcbench} has emerged as a powerful alternative. DPO simplifies the alignment process by mathematically demonstrating that the reward modeling and reinforcement learning phases can be combined into a single supervised learning objective. By directly optimizing the policy using a classification objective on the preference data, DPO bypasses the need for a separate reward model and the instabilities associated with PPO. This makes the alignment process more stable, efficient, and accessible for developers.

Despite their effectiveness, safety alignment techniques are not infallible. They are subject to the "alignment tax," where increased safety can sometimes lead to a degradation in the model's performance on benign tasks or an over-refusal of legitimate requests. Furthermore, sophisticated adversarial attacks, such as optimization-based jailbreaks, can often bypass alignment by pushing the model's inputs outside the distribution of the alignment training data, exploiting blind spots in the model's learned safety boundaries.

\section{Perplexity Filters}

Because safety alignment can be circumvented, runtime defenses that analyze the input before it reaches the core model are crucial. One such defense is the use of perplexity filters. This strategy is particularly effective against automated, optimization-based adversarial attacks, such as the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack. These attacks often append a sequence of carefully optimized, but seemingly random, tokens to a malicious prompt to force the model into generating a harmful response.

To a human reader, and to a standard language model, these optimized adversarial suffixes often appear as unnatural gibberish. Perplexity filters exploit this characteristic by measuring the likelihood, or perplexity, of the input sequence using a smaller, trusted language model. Perplexity is a metric that quantifies how well a probability model predicts a sample; in the context of language, a high perplexity indicates that the text is surprising or unnatural to the model.

When a user submits a prompt, the perplexity filter evaluates it. If the perplexity score exceeds a predefined threshold, the input is flagged as potentially adversarial and is either rejected or flagged for further review. The primary advantage of perplexity filters is their computational efficiency; evaluating perplexity with a small model is significantly faster and cheaper than processing the input through a massive LLM.

However, perplexity filters have notable limitations. They are vulnerable to adaptive attacks. An adversary aware of the filter can incorporate a perplexity constraint into their optimization process, forcing the attack algorithm to find adversarial suffixes that are not only effective at bypassing alignment but also maintain a low perplexity score, thereby evading the filter. Additionally, perplexity filters can suffer from false positives, inadvertently flagging legitimate user inputs that contain unusual vocabulary, code snippets, or non-standard grammar.

\section{Guardrails}

Guardrails represent a more sophisticated layer of runtime defense, acting as an intermediary monitoring system between the user and the core LLM. Unlike perplexity filters, which primarily look at the statistical likelihood of tokens, guardrails perform semantic analysis on both the input prompts and the model's generated outputs.

Input guardrails are designed to detect malicious intent, such as prompt injection attempts, jailbreaks, or requests for restricted information. Output guardrails, on the other hand, monitor the model's responses to prevent the leakage of personally identifiable information (PII), the generation of toxic or defamatory content, or hallucinations.

Frameworks like LlamaGuard \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs} and NeMo Guardrails utilize specialized, often smaller, LLMs trained specifically for classification tasks. When a prompt is received, it is first routed to the input guardrail. If the guardrail classifies the prompt as safe, it is passed to the core LLM. The core LLM's response is then evaluated by the output guardrail before being returned to the user.

A significant strength of guardrails is their modularity. They can be updated, fine-tuned, or replaced independently of the core LLM, allowing organizations to rapidly adapt to new threat vectors or customize safety policies for specific enterprise use cases. However, guardrails are not without challenges. They introduce additional latency into the inference pipeline, which can be problematic for real-time applications. Furthermore, as noted by Mo et al. \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}, off-the-shelf guardrail models are typically trained on traditional safety datasets in single-turn settings. Consequently, they often fail to detect risky multi-turn intents where an adversary gradually leads the model toward a harmful outcome over the course of a prolonged conversation.

\section{Input Sanitization}

Drawing inspiration from traditional cybersecurity practices, input sanitization is a fundamental defense mechanism aimed at neutralizing potentially malicious payloads before they are processed by the LLM. In classical web security, sanitization involves techniques like escaping characters to prevent Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) or SQL injection. As Jenko et al. \cite{Jenko_2024_LLM} highlight, many injection-style vulnerabilities can be mitigated by applying specific sanitization functions on potentially unsafe objects, such as using the \texttt{escape} function to safely encode user inputs.

In the context of LLMs, input sanitization takes on a broader meaning. It can involve deterministic rules, such as stripping out specific control characters, neutralizing formatting tags (like HTML or Markdown) that might be used to confuse the model's parsing logic, or enforcing strict length limits. Rubel et al. \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM} also note the importance of dataset sanitization through static analysis to prevent data poisoning attacks during the model's training or fine-tuning phases.

Beyond deterministic rules, advanced input sanitization can leverage LLMs themselves. A common technique is "prompt rewriting" or "paraphrasing." Before the user's prompt is sent to the target model, a separate, highly constrained LLM rewrites the prompt to extract its core semantic meaning while stripping away any potential adversarial framing, deceptive personas, or injection commands.

Input sanitization is highly effective against known, structural injection patterns and is relatively simple to implement. However, its efficacy diminishes against semantic attacks. If the malicious intent is deeply embedded in natural language rather than relying on special characters or explicit commands, deterministic sanitization rules will likely fail to detect it, and even LLM-based paraphrasers might inadvertently preserve the harmful semantics.

\section{Circuit Breakers}

Circuit breakers represent a cutting-edge defense mechanism inspired by electrical engineering and microservices architectures. While guardrails typically evaluate the final output after it has been generated, circuit breakers operate dynamically during the generation process itself. They are designed to monitor the internal state or the token-by-token output of the LLM and abruptly halt generation if a safety threshold is breached.

This approach often relies on representation engineering or internal activation monitoring. Researchers have found that when an LLM is generating harmful content or responding to a successful jailbreak, its internal hidden states exhibit distinct patterns compared to when it is generating benign text. By training a lightweight classifier (a "probe") on these internal activations, a circuit breaker can detect the onset of harmful generation in real-time.

If the circuit breaker detects that the model's trajectory is heading toward a policy violation, it immediately interrupts the generation process, returning a standard refusal message or an empty string to the user. This acts as a critical fail-safe mechanism. Even if an adversarial prompt successfully bypasses input sanitization, perplexity filters, and input guardrails, and manages to overcome the model's safety alignment, the circuit breaker can still prevent the harmful payload from being fully realized and delivered to the user.

The primary limitation of circuit breakers is their architectural requirement: they necessitate white-box access to the model's internal states and activations. This makes them highly effective for open-weights models or internally hosted proprietary models, but impossible to implement as a third-party defense for black-box API models where only the final text output is accessible. Furthermore, designing the threshold for the circuit breaker requires careful calibration. If the sensitivity is set too high, the model may frequently interrupt itself during benign but complex reasoning tasks, severely degrading the user experience. Conversely, if the threshold is too low, the circuit breaker may fail to trigger during a sophisticated attack. Ongoing research is focused on refining these internal probes to be highly specific to harmful intents without interfering with the model's general capabilities.

\section{Testing Methodologies and Evaluation}

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) and AI-driven code generation agents has necessitated the development of rigorous testing methodologies and evaluation frameworks. As these models become increasingly integrated into critical infrastructure, software development pipelines, and user-facing applications, assessing their robustness against adversarial attacks is paramount. The evaluation of adversarial vulnerabilities is a multifaceted challenge that requires quantifying the effectiveness of attacks, establishing standardized benchmarks, simulating real-world threat actors through red teaming, measuring unintended behaviors like hallucinations, and assessing the stealth and impact of data poisoning. This section provides a comprehensive overview of the primary evaluation methodologies used in the domain of adversarial attacks on AI systems, focusing on Attack Success Rate (ASR), Evaluation Benchmarks, Red Teaming Frameworks, Hallucination Metrics, and Backdoor Evaluation Protocols.

\section{Attack Success Rate (ASR)}

Attack Success Rate (ASR) is the foundational metric used to quantify the efficacy of an adversarial attack. At its core, ASR measures the proportion of adversarial inputs---such as crafted prompts, poisoned data samples, or perturbed code snippets---that successfully bypass a model's safety filters or induce the attacker's intended malicious behavior. In the context of LLMs and autonomous agents, ASR is the primary indicator of a model's vulnerability to specific threat vectors.

However, the definition of "success" can vary significantly depending on the nature of the attack and the target system. For prompt injection and jailbreaking attacks, ASR typically represents the percentage of malicious prompts that elicit a non-refusal response containing the requested harmful information. In the evaluation of code generation agents, ASR is often defined more stringently as the percentage of generated code snippets that not only contain the malicious payload but also successfully compile and execute within a target environment without triggering system defenses. Recent studies emphasize the importance of measuring ASR across diverse operational domains, such as filesystem manipulation, network exploitation, operating system interactions, and data processing \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}. 

ASR is rarely evaluated in isolation. It is frequently analyzed alongside the Rejection Rate, which measures how often the model correctly identifies and refuses to process a malicious prompt \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}. A robust model should exhibit a high Rejection Rate and a correspondingly low ASR when subjected to adversarial inputs. Furthermore, high ASR achieved at the expense of output utility or stealth is often considered a flawed attack. For instance, in adversarial attacks on code models, techniques like identifier substitution might achieve a high ASR by bypassing static analysis tools, but they often result in highly unnatural code. Empirical studies utilizing LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks have revealed that such attacks generate code with very low Naturalness Evaluation Scores (NES), making them easily detectable by human reviewers \cite{Mu_2025_LLM}. Therefore, comprehensive evaluations report ASR in conjunction with utility and stealth metrics to ensure that the adversarial examples remain functional and evasive.

\section{Evaluation Benchmarks}

Standardized evaluation benchmarks are essential for objectively comparing the robustness of different models, tracking progress over time, and evaluating the efficacy of novel attack and defense strategies. These benchmarks consist of meticulously curated datasets of prompts, code snippets, or conversational turns designed to systematically test specific vulnerabilities across a wide range of threat categories.

In the domain of code LLMs and autonomous agents, benchmarks must go beyond static text evaluation to assess the actual execution of generated code. Platforms like RedCode provide a comprehensive safety evaluation environment that assesses both the execution of unsafe code and the generation of malicious software within isolated, real-world system environments \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}. The accompanying RedCode-Exec dataset contains test cases spanning multiple domains, allowing researchers to systematically evaluate how different models respond to diverse and complex threat vectors, from unauthorized file access to network reconnaissance \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}.

Benchmarks are continuously updated to reflect the rapidly evolving threat landscape. They typically categorize threats into comprehensive taxonomies to ensure broad coverage. Foundational LLM safety evaluations often focus on primary threat vectors including cybersecurity (e.g., authoring zero-day exploits or generating malware), biology (e.g., synthesizing novel pathogens), deception (e.g., generating highly convincing phishing campaigns or manipulating human users), and model autonomy (e.g., the model acquiring emergent, unintended skills or executing unauthorized actions) \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. By standardizing the evaluation process across these diverse categories, benchmarks enable the research community to identify persistent vulnerabilities, evaluate the transferability of attacks across different model architectures, and guide the development of more resilient safety guardrails.

\section{Red Teaming Frameworks}

Red teaming has emerged as a critical, proactive methodology for identifying vulnerabilities in LLMs before they are deployed in production environments. Borrowed from the cybersecurity domain, red teaming involves simulating the tactics, techniques, and procedures of adversarial actors to rigorously test a model's defenses, uncover unintended behaviors, and identify edge cases that static benchmarks might miss.

Traditional red teaming often relies on human experts manually crafting malicious prompts and interacting with the model to find bypasses. While effective for discovering novel vulnerabilities, manual red teaming is resource-intensive, difficult to scale, and often fails to cover the vast input space of modern LLMs. Consequently, the field has shifted towards automated red teaming frameworks that systematically and autonomously explore the attack surface.

Advanced frameworks like REDCODER utilize an autonomous, multi-turn red teaming approach \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}. Instead of relying on single-turn prompt injections, these frameworks train a dedicated "red-team LLM" to act as the adversary. By training on prototype conversations generated during simulated gaming processes, the red-team model learns to dynamically steer multi-turn interactions based on the victim model's responses \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}. This contextual adaptability allows the automated red team to reproduce highly effective adversarial behaviors, adapt to the defensive mechanisms of the target model in real-time, and generalize to novel interactions with unseen victim models \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}. Automated red teaming frameworks evaluate models against a wide spectrum of threats, and the insights gained from these dynamic exercises are crucial for iteratively refining model alignment, patching vulnerabilities, and implementing robust, context-aware safety guardrails.

\section{Hallucination Metrics}

While hallucinations---instances where a model generates factually incorrect, nonsensical, or ungrounded information---are typically viewed as a reliability and quality control issue, they possess significant security implications in the context of adversarial attacks. Adversaries can exploit a model's propensity to hallucinate to conduct sophisticated attacks, such as package hallucination (where a model recommends a non-existent software package that an attacker subsequently registers and laces with malware) or hallucinated citations (which can be used to spread disinformation or lend false credibility to malicious claims).

Evaluating hallucinations, particularly those induced by adversarial manipulation, requires specialized metrics that go beyond simple string matching or n-gram overlap. Modern evaluation protocols increasingly rely on LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks to assess the factuality, naturalness, and grounding of generated content \cite{Mu_2025_LLM}. These frameworks leverage a strong, aligned evaluator LLM to compare the target model's output against a verified knowledge base or to score the output based on predefined, nuanced criteria. For example, in the context of code generation, evaluator models can assign a Naturalness Evaluation Score (NES) to detect unnatural code structures that may indicate an underlying adversarial manipulation or a hallucinated logic flow \cite{Mu_2025_LLM}.

Metrics for hallucination often focus on precision, recall, and F1-score in the context of factual claims extracted from the model's output. In adversarial settings, the evaluation specifically considers the model's susceptibility to \textit{induced} hallucinations. This involves measuring the success rate of adversarial prompts deliberately crafted to force the model into generating specific false information or recommending malicious artifacts. Evaluating hallucination robustness is critical for ensuring that models can safely operate in environments where factual accuracy and secure recommendations are paramount.

\section{Backdoor Evaluation Protocols}

Backdoor attacks, also known as data poisoning, represent a severe threat to the integrity of machine learning models. These attacks involve injecting hidden triggers into the training or fine-tuning data. When the deployed model encounters the specific trigger during inference, it exhibits a predetermined, attacker-defined malicious behavior, while continuing to function normally on all benign inputs. Evaluating the robustness of models against backdoor attacks requires rigorous and specialized protocols.

The standard evaluation protocol for backdoor attacks involves a multi-step process. First, the target model is fine-tuned or trained from scratch on a poisoned dataset containing the adversarial triggers and the corresponding malicious target labels. Subsequently, researchers sample completions or predictions from the poisoned model to measure the attack's effectiveness across various scenarios \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}. These protocols are designed to assess different types of backdoor mechanisms, ranging from simple identity-map attacks (where a specific input directly triggers a specific output) to more complex directional-map attacks that utilize arbitrary, context-dependent triggers \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}.

Key metrics in backdoor evaluation protocols include the Attack Success Rate (ASR) of the trigger and the Clean Data Accuracy (CDA). The ASR measures how reliably the presence of the hidden trigger induces the targeted malicious behavior. Conversely, the CDA measures the model's performance on standard, benign inputs. A highly successful and dangerous backdoor attack achieves a near-100\% ASR while maintaining a CDA that is statistically indistinguishable from a clean, unpoisoned model. This ensures that the backdoor remains stealthy and undetected during standard validation and quality assurance processes. Furthermore, comprehensive backdoor evaluation protocols also test the robustness of the injected backdoors against potential defensive interventions, such as trigger reverse engineering, activation clustering, and model pruning, to determine the persistence and resilience of the threat.

\section{Discussion and Open Challenges}

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into software development workflows has undeniably accelerated productivity, but it has also exposed a complex landscape of vulnerabilities. As adversarial attacks on code-generating models become increasingly sophisticated, the research community faces several critical hurdles. This section discusses the primary challenges and open questions that must be addressed to secure the next generation of AI-assisted programming tools, focusing on the delicate balance between utility and security, the emergence of novel threat vectors, and the scalability of defense mechanisms.

\subsection{Utility vs. Security Trade-off}

A pervasive and fundamental challenge in the deployment of secure code LLMs is the inherent tension between model utility and security alignment. As models are fine-tuned to reject malicious or unsafe requests, they frequently fall victim to the "over-refusal" problem. In this scenario, benign, borderline, or highly complex but legitimate coding requests are incorrectly flagged as unsafe, thereby severely degrading the model's practical usefulness for developers. 

\cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha} explicitly highlights this trade-off by analyzing the relationship between utility---measured by the Pass@1 metric on standard benchmarks like HumanEval+ and MBPP+---and safety, quantified by the average Rejection Rate (RR) under adversarial conditions. The ideal code model resides in a theoretical quadrant of both high utility and high safety; however, current alignment techniques often force a compromise, sacrificing coding proficiency for robust refusal behavior. Striking the optimal balance requires the development of more nuanced, context-aware alignment strategies that can accurately differentiate between legitimate administrative coding tasks (e.g., writing a script to manage network configurations) and disguised malicious intents (e.g., writing a script for network reconnaissance). 

Furthermore, \cite{Nie_2024_Secodeplt} points out a parallel trade-off within the data creation pipelines used to train these secure models. Manual generation of safety-aligned training data offers high quality and precision but lacks the scalability required to cover the vast space of potential vulnerabilities. Conversely, fully automated data generation is highly scalable but often yields lower-quality, repetitive examples that fail to capture the nuances of real-world attacks. Balancing these aspects is critical for developing robust code models without sacrificing their core generative capabilities.

\subsection{Agentic AI Risks}

The paradigm shift from passive, single-turn code generators to autonomous, agentic AI systems introduces a formidable new frontier of security risks. AI agents, such as OpenCodeInterpreter, ReAct, and CodeAct, are designed not just to write code, but to execute it, interact with local environments, and perform complex, multi-step tasks autonomously. This autonomy significantly expands the attack surface, transforming the LLM from a mere text generator into a potential vector for direct system compromise.

\cite{Guo_2024_Redcode} evaluates the robustness of various agentic frameworks and reveals that their security awareness varies considerably based on their architecture and underlying models. For example, OpenCodeInterpreter demonstrates higher robustness against certain attacks due to hard-coded constraints and environmental sandboxing, such as strict disk space protection. In contrast, frameworks like CodeAct exhibit lower intrinsic security awareness. The choice of the foundational LLM also plays a pivotal role; \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode} notes that instruction fine-tuning specifically for agentic tasks can inadvertently compromise the model's safety guardrails, leading to alarmingly low rejection rates for malicious instructions. As these agents become more deeply integrated into CI/CD pipelines and developer environments, the risk of them being manipulated to execute harmful system commands, exfiltrate sensitive source code, or facilitate "living off the land" attacks \cite{Noever_2024_AI} becomes a pressing, real-world concern. Securing agentic AI will require robust, isolated sandboxing, continuous runtime monitoring of agent actions, and alignment techniques tailored specifically for interactive, stateful environments.

\subsection{Multimodal Threats}

While the vast majority of current adversarial research in the code domain focuses on text-based prompt injection and jailbreaking, the rapid emergence of multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) introduces novel threat vectors that remain largely unexplored. Multimodal threats leverage non-textual inputs---such as images, architectural diagrams, flowcharts, or even audio---to bypass text-centric safety filters. 

In a software development context, an attacker could theoretically embed malicious code logic or prompt injection payloads within a seemingly benign architecture diagram or a screenshot of an error message. When the developer prompts the MLLM to generate code based on this visual input, the model may inadvertently process the hidden adversarial payload, resulting in the generation of vulnerable or malicious code. Because current alignment and defense mechanisms are predominantly optimized for parsing and sanitizing textual prompts, they are largely blind to adversarial payloads hidden in other modalities. As code models increasingly incorporate multimodal capabilities to assist with tasks like UI-to-code generation, visual debugging, or interpreting legacy system diagrams, addressing these cross-modal threats will become imperative. Future research must pioneer cross-modal defense strategies capable of analyzing and sanitizing inputs across all supported modalities, ensuring that the semantic intent of the prompt remains safe regardless of its format.

\subsection{Scalability of Defenses}

Developing defense mechanisms that can scale commensurately with the rapid advancement, increasing parameter size, and expanding context windows of LLMs is a significant open challenge. Many current defense strategies, such as comprehensive adversarial training or manual red-teaming, are highly resource-intensive and struggle to keep pace with the rapidly evolving landscape of adversarial tactics. 

\cite{Nie_2024_Secodeplt} proposes a pragmatic two-stage data creation pipeline to address the scalability bottleneck in generating high-quality training data for secure models. By combining manually curated, high-quality seed examples with automated, LLM-driven mutation techniques, this approach attempts to achieve both quality and scale. However, as attackers increasingly employ sophisticated, automated, and multi-step tactics \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}, static defenses quickly become obsolete. Defenses must evolve to become more dynamic and scalable. This necessitates the development of automated, AI-driven red-teaming frameworks capable of continuously probing models for zero-day vulnerabilities at scale. Additionally, there is a pressing need for lightweight, inference-time defenses---such as input perplexity filtering or activation monitoring---that can detect and neutralize attacks without introducing prohibitive latency or computational overhead, ensuring that defenses can scale effectively across diverse deployment environments.

\subsection{Future Research Directions}

The landscape of adversarial attacks on code models is highly dynamic, necessitating continuous, forward-looking innovation in defense strategies. Based on the current state of the art, several critical areas for future research emerge:

First, there is an urgent need for more resilient alignment techniques capable of defending against complex, multi-step, and context-aware adversarial tactics \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. Current alignment methods often fail when faced with sophisticated jailbreaks that distribute malicious intent across multiple turns of a conversation.

Second, research must expand beyond the analysis of isolated code snippets to consider the broader, systemic context of software development workflows. This includes investigating vulnerabilities in Retrieval-Augmented Code Generation (RACG) systems, where maliciously injected code in retrieved, third-party repositories can silently compromise the generated output \cite{Lin_2025_LLMs}. It also requires exploring the risks associated with collaborative coding environments, where attackers might introduce subtle, semantics-preserving transformations to evade manual code review while introducing vulnerabilities \cite{Storek_2025_AI}.

Third, the development of standardized, comprehensive, and continuously updated benchmarks for evaluating the security and robustness of code models is essential. These benchmarks must move beyond simple text-based jailbreaks to encompass a wide range of attack vectors, including agentic manipulation and multimodal threats, providing a holistic assessment of a model's safety-utility trade-off.

Finally, fostering deeper collaboration between the artificial intelligence and traditional cybersecurity communities will be vital. By bridging the gap between machine learning alignment and established software security principles, the research community can anticipate emerging threats and develop robust, scalable defenses necessary to secure the future of AI-assisted software engineering.

\section{Conclusion}

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, enterprise workflows, and everyday applications has fundamentally redefined the cybersecurity paradigm. This survey has systematically mapped the multifaceted threat landscape of LLMs, categorizing vulnerabilities across the entire model lifecycle. At training time, data and model poisoning---particularly stealthy backdoor attacks---pose severe risks to foundational integrity, allowing adversaries to embed latent vulnerabilities that are exceptionally difficult to detect. During inference, models are highly susceptible to prompt injection and jailbreaking, wherein attackers exploit the fundamental mechanisms of instruction-following to bypass safety alignments. Furthermore, in specialized domains such as AI-assisted software development, adversarial attacks can manipulate code-generating models into producing insecure or malicious outputs, directly threatening downstream software supply chains. Finally, at the output level, the persistent challenge of hallucinations undermines the factual reliability and trustworthiness of LLM deployments, blurring the line between benign errors and exploitable misinformation.

A critical trend observed throughout this comprehensive review is the rapid sophistication and automation of adversarial techniques. Between 2024 and 2026, the threat landscape evolved from rudimentary, manual text-based exploits to highly complex, optimization-based, and multimodal vulnerabilities. Adversaries are increasingly leveraging automated frameworks to discover jailbreaks at scale, creating a continuous arms race between attackers and defenders. Moreover, as LLMs transition from passive text generators to autonomous agents capable of executing actions and interacting with external APIs, the attack surface expands exponentially. The literature indicates that traditional, deterministic security paradigms are insufficient; LLM vulnerabilities are deeply intertwined with their probabilistic nature, the vast, uncurated datasets upon which they are trained, and the inherent tension between model helpfulness and harmlessness.

The practical implications of these findings for LLM deployment are profound. As these models are integrated into high-stakes environments---ranging from healthcare diagnostics to financial forecasting and automated code generation---the potential blast radius of a successful attack is unprecedented. Organizations can no longer rely solely on post-hoc safety filters or basic alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which have been shown to degrade under targeted adversarial pressure. Instead, the deployment of LLMs necessitates a robust defense-in-depth strategy. This includes implementing cross-cutting mitigation strategies such as rigorous input sanitization, semantic monitoring, continuous runtime evaluation, and architectural interventions. Furthermore, the deployment of specialized LLMs requires domain-specific safeguards to prevent the inadvertent introduction of vulnerabilities into broader production pipelines.

Despite significant advancements in defensive mechanisms, several critical open challenges remain, charting the course for future research. First, the development of unified, rigorous testing methodologies and evaluation frameworks is urgently needed. Current benchmarks often fail to capture the dynamic, multi-turn, and contextual nature of real-world adversarial attacks. Future research must prioritize the creation of scalable, automated red-teaming frameworks that can dynamically adapt to novel threat vectors. Second, mitigating output-level vulnerabilities, particularly hallucinations, requires moving beyond superficial detection toward architectural innovations that ground LLM generation in verifiable external knowledge bases, such as advanced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems. Third, as Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and multi-agent systems become ubiquitous, research must address the cross-modal vulnerabilities and cascading failures unique to these complex, interconnected architectures. 

To navigate this complex security landscape, we offer actionable recommendations for both practitioners and researchers. For industry practitioners, we recommend adopting a "secure-by-design" philosophy. This entails integrating comprehensive threat modeling early in the LLM lifecycle, employing continuous adversarial testing, and establishing strict access controls and output validation mechanisms---especially for code-generating and agentic systems. Practitioners should also actively participate in threat intelligence sharing to stay abreast of emerging jailbreak techniques and poisoning vectors. For the academic and research community, we urge a shift toward developing provable security guarantees for LLMs. While empirical defenses are valuable, they are often quickly bypassed by adaptive attacks. Researchers should focus on fundamental advancements in mechanistic interpretability, robust alignment techniques that resist fine-tuning degradation, and the development of standardized, open-source evaluation suites that allow for reproducible and comparative security assessments.

Ultimately, securing Large Language Models is not a static goal but a continuous, evolving discipline. As AI systems become increasingly autonomous and deeply embedded in societal infrastructure, ensuring their robustness, alignment, and security is paramount. Bridging the gap between rapid AI capabilities and rigorous security assurances will require sustained, interdisciplinary collaboration among machine learning researchers, cybersecurity experts, and policymakers. Only through proactive, comprehensive, and adaptive security frameworks can we fully harness the transformative potential of LLMs while safeguarding against their inherent and emerging risks.
