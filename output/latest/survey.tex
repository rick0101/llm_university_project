\begin{abstract}
The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical commercial and societal applications has revolutionized natural language processing while simultaneously introducing unprecedented security, privacy, and reliability challenges. To address these critical concerns, this survey presents a comprehensive examination of the evolving threat landscape surrounding LLMs, encompassing both training-time and inference-time vulnerabilities. Through a systematic literature review analyzing 20 foundational and contemporary papers, this study categorizes the multifaceted security risks and structural weaknesses inherent in modern language models. The analysis systematically dissects training-time threats, specifically focusing on data and model poisoning techniques that compromise foundational integrity. It subsequently details critical inference-time vulnerabilities, including sophisticated adversarial attacks, jailbreaks, prompt injection, and emerging agentic vulnerabilities that arise when LLMs are integrated with external tools and systems. Furthermore, the survey critically examines the intersection of model reliability and trust, highlighting the pervasive issue of LLM hallucinations and their severe implications for safe, real-world deployment. Beyond threat characterization, this paper provides significant practical contributions by synthesizing cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies designed to fortify model resilience across the entire machine learning lifecycle. It also delivers a detailed overview of current testing methodologies, red-teaming practices, and evaluation frameworks essential for rigorously benchmarking LLM security. Finally, the survey outlines persistent open challenges and future research directions, offering a comprehensive and foundational resource for researchers, developers, and practitioners aiming to design, evaluate, and deploy secure, trustworthy, and robust artificial intelligence systems.

\section{1. Introduction}

The emergence of artificial intelligence has catalyzed a profound transformation in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and broader computational disciplines through the introduction of Large Language Models (LLMs). By leveraging massive datasets and advanced transformer architectures, these models have enabled unprecedented advances in language comprehension, generation, and translation \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Contemporary models, such as OpenAI’s GPT-4, Anthropic's Claude, and Meta's LLaMA series, have consistently outperformed previous state-of-the-art methods across a wide variety of benchmarking tasks. They demonstrate remarkable, emergent capabilities in complex reasoning, multi-step planning, and interacting with dynamic environments \cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}. These transformative impacts extend far beyond general-purpose conversational agents; LLMs are increasingly being integrated into specialized, high-stakes domains, including healthcare diagnostics, financial forecasting, legal analysis, and autonomous systems. As these models transition from isolated chatbots to core reasoning engines embedded within broader software ecosystems, their operational reliability and security have become paramount concerns.

However, with their rising prominence and rapid integration into critical infrastructure, the security and vulnerability aspects of these models have garnered significant, urgent attention \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. The very capabilities that make LLMs exceptionally powerful—their ability to process vast amounts of unstructured data, follow complex instructions, and generate contextually relevant responses—also render them susceptible to a novel and expansive class of security threats. Unlike traditional software systems, where vulnerabilities typically stem from logical bugs or memory mismanagement, LLM vulnerabilities are deeply intertwined with their statistical nature, training methodologies, and the inherent ambiguity of natural language interfaces. This paradigm shift necessitates a departure from classical cybersecurity frameworks, demanding new taxonomies and mitigation strategies tailored to the unique attack surface of generative AI.

The LLM threat landscape can be broadly categorized across the model lifecycle, beginning with training-time vulnerabilities. Because LLMs require massive, often uncurated datasets scraped from the public internet, they are highly susceptible to data poisoning. Malicious actors can inject subtly manipulated data into the training corpus to degrade model performance or implant hidden backdoors. In these backdoor attacks, carefully crafted triggers in the input manipulate the model to produce adversary-specified outputs or malicious payloads during deployment, often while maintaining normal behavior on benign inputs \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}. As the reliance on open-source models and third-party fine-tuning (such as Low-Rank Adaptation) grows, the risk of model poisoning and supply chain compromises has escalated dramatically.

During inference, deployed models face an array of sophisticated attacks designed to subvert their intended behavior. Adversarial attacks and jailbreaks represent a critical vector where malicious users craft specific prompts to bypass safety guardrails and alignment protocols (such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). These attacks force the model to generate toxic, harmful, or restricted content \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Furthermore, as LLMs evolve into autonomous agents capable of utilizing external tools, browsing the web, and executing code, they become exposed to prompt injection attacks. While direct prompt injection involves a user intentionally subverting the model's instructions, indirect prompt injection (IPI) represents a far more insidious threat. In an IPI attack, malicious instructions are embedded within external data sources—such as websites, emails, or documents—that the LLM agent consumes. When the agent processes this poisoned context, it may unknowingly execute the attacker's commands, leading to data exfiltration, unauthorized actions, or the complete compromise of the agentic ecosystem \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}.

Beyond intentional adversarial exploitation, the intrinsic reliability of LLMs poses a significant security challenge, most notably through the persistent phenomenon of hallucinations. Hallucinations occur when models generate plausible but factually inaccurate, ungrounded, or logically inconsistent claims \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. While traditionally viewed as a mere reliability issue, hallucinations constitute a severe security vulnerability. For example, an LLM might hallucinate the existence of a non-existent software library in a coding tutorial; an attacker who anticipates this can register the hallucinated package name in a public repository, executing a sophisticated supply chain attack against developers who blindly trust the model's output. Consequently, ensuring model trustworthiness requires treating hallucinations with the same rigor as active adversarial threats.

The rapid proliferation and increasing sophistication of these threats necessitate a comprehensive, up-to-date understanding of the LLM security landscape. While previous literature has explored isolated aspects of AI security, the explosive growth of agentic workflows and multimodal capabilities between 2019 and 2026 demands a holistic synthesis. To address this critical gap, this survey systematically analyzes the vulnerabilities, attack vectors, and defense mechanisms associated with modern language models. 

The primary contributions of this survey are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We provide a comprehensive, chronological taxonomy of LLM security threats spanning the 2019–2026 timeline, categorizing vulnerabilities across both the training and inference phases of the model lifecycle.
  \item We present an in-depth analysis of the evolution of prompt injection, with a specific focus on the emerging vulnerabilities inherent in LLM-driven autonomous agents and tool-use paradigms.
  \item We recontextualize LLM hallucinations not merely as functional errors, but as critical security and trust vulnerabilities, detailing their implications for high-stakes deployments.
  \item We synthesize state-of-the-art defense mechanisms and provide a mandatory, dedicated evaluation of the testing methodologies, benchmarks, and metrics required to rigorously assess LLM security.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Preliminaries}

The rapid proliferation and integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into diverse applications have fundamentally transformed the landscape of Natural Language Processing (NLP) and artificial intelligence. These models offer unprecedented capabilities in language comprehension, generation, reasoning, and translation \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. However, as their deployment scales across critical sectors—ranging from healthcare automation to financial analysis and autonomous agents—the security and vulnerability aspects of these systems have garnered significant attention \cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}. Understanding the foundational mechanics of LLMs, alongside the multifaceted threats they face, is essential for developing robust mitigation strategies. This section provides the necessary background and preliminaries, detailing the basics of LLM architecture, establishing a comprehensive threat taxonomy, defining attacker capabilities, and distinguishing between black-box and white-box attack paradigms.

\section{LLM Architecture Basics}

To comprehend the vulnerabilities inherent in Large Language Models, it is first necessary to understand their underlying architectural framework. Modern LLMs are predominantly built upon the Transformer architecture, a deep learning model introduced to handle sequential data efficiently through massive parallelization. Unlike earlier recurrent neural networks (RNNs) that process data sequentially, Transformers rely entirely on self-attention mechanisms to draw global dependencies between input and output tokens.

\textbf{The Transformer and Self-Attention Mechanism:}
At the core of the Transformer architecture is the self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to weigh the importance of different words (or tokens) in a sequence relative to a specific target word, regardless of their positional distance. This mechanism enables LLMs to capture complex contextual relationships and long-range dependencies within text. The attention mechanism computes a weighted sum of values, where the weight assigned to each value is determined by the compatibility of a query with the corresponding key. Recent research has explored utilizing these attention contributions to categorize input queries and detect model hallucinations, highlighting the critical role attention plays in determining model outputs \cite{Liu_2025_Attention}. However, this same mechanism can be exploited by adversaries who craft inputs specifically designed to manipulate attention weights, forcing the model to focus on malicious instructions while ignoring safety guardrails.

\textbf{Pre-training and Fine-tuning:}
The development of an LLM typically follows a two-stage process: pre-training and fine-tuning. During the pre-training phase, the model is exposed to massive, diverse corpora of text data scraped from the internet. The objective is usually self-supervised, such as predicting the next token in a sequence (causal language modeling). This phase endows the model with a broad understanding of grammar, facts, reasoning abilities, and, inadvertently, the biases and toxicities present in the training data.

Following pre-training, the model undergoes fine-tuning to align its outputs with specific tasks or human preferences. This often involves instruction tuning, where the model is trained on datasets of instruction-response pairs, and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which optimizes the model to generate responses that human evaluators deem helpful and safe. While these processes refine the model's capabilities, they also introduce distinct attack vectors. For instance, individuals and organizations frequently download pre-trained LLMs from open-source repositories (e.g., HuggingFace) for subsequent tuning and deployment, creating opportunities for adversaries to introduce vulnerabilities during the specialized tuning phase \cite{Li_2024_Badedit}.

\textbf{Explainability and Vulnerability:}
The sheer scale of these models—often comprising billions or hundreds of billions of parameters—renders them highly opaque. The explainability of LLMs is pivotal not just for enhancing transparency and trustworthiness, but also for identifying and mitigating vulnerabilities to linguistic attacks \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. The complex, non-linear decision-making processes inherent within the deep layers of the Transformer make it challenging to predict exactly how a model will respond to adversarial or edge-case inputs, necessitating a structured approach to understanding potential threats.

\section{Threat Taxonomy}

As LLMs become integral to automated systems and decision-making processes, they are exposed to a diverse range of threats. Categorizing the LLM attacks literature into a structured taxonomy is crucial for understanding the landscape of vulnerabilities and aiding future defensive research \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. The threat taxonomy for LLMs can be broadly classified into several key areas based on the attack vector and the lifecycle stage at which the attack occurs.

\textbf{1. Data and Model Poisoning:}
Poisoning attacks occur during the training or fine-tuning phases of the LLM lifecycle. Adversaries inject malicious data into the training corpus to manipulate the model's behavior systematically. This can result in the model learning incorrect associations, adopting specific biases, or embedding hidden backdoors that can be triggered later by specific input patterns. Because LLMs require vast amounts of data, ensuring the absolute purity of the training set is practically impossible, making poisoning a persistent and stealthy threat \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}.

\textbf{2. Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking:}
Prompt injection attacks target the inference phase, where the model is actively processing user inputs. In these attacks, adversaries craft malicious inputs designed to override the model's original system instructions or safety guardrails. Direct prompt injection involves the user inputting the malicious command directly into the chat interface. Conversely, Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) occurs when an LLM agent processes external data (e.g., reading a webpage, summarizing a document, or interacting with an API) that contains hidden malicious instructions. Despite defenses designed for IPI attacks, their robustness remains questionable due to the evolving nature of these exploits \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. Jailbreaking is a specific sub-category of prompt injection aimed at bypassing ethical and safety filters to elicit restricted, illegal, or harmful content.

\textbf{3. Adversarial Attacks:}
Adversarial attacks involve introducing subtle, often human-imperceptible perturbations to the input text that cause the model to make errors, misclassify intents, or behave unexpectedly. These attacks range from human-crafted, non-scalable methods to automated techniques utilizing search algorithms, target function optimization, and even other LLMs to generate adversarial examples \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

\textbf{4. Information Extraction and Privacy Leaks:}
These threats focus on forcing the LLM to regurgitate sensitive, confidential, or personally identifiable information (PII) that it may have memorized during its pre-training phase. Attackers use carefully constructed prompts to probe the model's memory, exploiting the model's tendency to reproduce its training data under certain prompting conditions.

\section{Attacker Capabilities}

To accurately assess the risk posed by the threats outlined in the taxonomy, it is essential to define the capabilities of potential attackers. The threat model dictates what an adversary can and cannot do, which in turn influences the types of attacks they can execute and the defenses required to thwart them. Attacker capabilities in the context of LLM security can be delineated across several dimensions.

\textbf{Lifecycle Access:}
An attacker's capability is heavily dependent on which stage of the LLM lifecycle they can access. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Training Phase Capabilities:} Attackers with access to the training pipeline can execute broad and devastating threat models. They may be capable of manipulating training data (data poisoning), modifying model parameters directly (weight poisoning), influencing the training process, or manipulating hidden states \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}. This level of access is highly privileged but can occur in scenarios involving compromised supply chains, malicious insiders, or the reliance on untrusted third-party datasets.
  \item \textit{Deployment/Inference Phase Capabilities:} Most attackers operate at this stage. They interact with the deployed model as a standard user or through an API. Their capabilities are generally restricted to crafting inputs (prompts) and observing outputs, though they may leverage zero-shot or few-shot inference techniques to manipulate the model's context window \cite{Li_2024_Badedit}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Specific Threat Models and Sophistication:}
Recent literature explicitly distinguishes between different threat models based on the attacker's role and interaction with the system. For example, in LLM-as-a-judge scenarios, a "Content-Author Attack" model is defined. In this scenario, a malicious content author submits text for evaluation that contains adversarial elements specifically designed to manipulate the LLM judge's assessment. The submitted text passes through the evaluation pipeline unchanged, representing a realistic capability where the attacker controls the data being processed but not the processing system itself \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}.

Furthermore, attackers possess varying levels of computational resources and sophistication. Some may rely on manual, human-crafted prompts, which are slow and non-scalable. In contrast, well-resourced attackers might employ automated methods, utilizing optimization algorithms or leveraging other LLMs to generate sophisticated, high-volume attacks \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. The capability to hijack advanced reasoning processes, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) hijacking, demonstrates a high level of sophistication where the attacker manipulates the intermediate reasoning steps of the model to force a desired, often malicious, final output \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}.

\section{Black-box vs. White-box Attacks}

A fundamental distinction in the evaluation of LLM vulnerabilities is the classification of attacks based on the adversary's knowledge of and access to the target model. This dichotomy—black-box versus white-box—defines the strategies available to the attacker and the complexity of the required defenses.

\textbf{White-box Attacks:}
White-box attacks assume that the adversary has complete, transparent access to the target LLM. This includes full knowledge of the model's architecture, the underlying algorithms, the training data, the hyper-parameters, and, crucially, the model's internal weights and gradients. 

This level of access permits highly sophisticated and targeted exploits. Attackers can utilize gradient-based optimization techniques to craft adversarial inputs that are mathematically guaranteed to alter outputs, impair performance, or extract sensitive information \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Because the attacker can compute the exact gradient of the loss function with respect to the input, they can systematically perturb the input tokens to maximize the probability of a malicious outcome. While white-box scenarios represent the most powerful attacker capability, they are often less realistic for proprietary, closed-source models (e.g., OpenAI's GPT-4 or Anthropic's Claude), where the internal mechanics are strictly guarded. However, with the proliferation of powerful open-source models (e.g., Meta's LLaMA series), white-box threat models have become highly relevant, as adversaries can download and analyze these models locally.

\textbf{Black-box Attacks:}
In contrast, black-box attacks operate under the assumption that the adversary has limited or no knowledge of the model's internal workings. The attacker interacts with the LLM solely through its input-output interface, typically via an API or a user chat interface. They do not have access to the model's architecture, weights, or gradients.

Black-box attacks focus on manipulating or degrading performance by observing how the model responds to various inputs. This approach is highly realistic in practical, real-world scenarios, especially when targeting commercial, proprietary LLMs \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Attackers in a black-box setting often rely on heuristic methods, trial-and-error, or transferability—where an adversarial example crafted on a known, open-source surrogate model (a white-box environment) is deployed against the unknown target model with the expectation that the vulnerability transfers across architectures. 

Despite the lack of internal access, black-box attacks pose severe risks, including sensitive data extraction, the generation of biased or harmful outputs, and the execution of prompt injection and jailbreaking techniques. The success of black-box attacks highlights that securing the model's weights is insufficient; the input-output interface itself represents a significant attack surface that must be rigorously defended to maintain trust and reliability in AI systems \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

\section{Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning}

The rapid evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) and multimodal artificial intelligence systems has fundamentally shifted the machine learning landscape toward a complex, multi-stage paradigm: massive-scale pre-training followed by task-specific fine-tuning and human-centric alignment \texttt{\cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}}. While this layered approach democratizes access to high-performing models and allows for rapid adaptation to specialized domains, it simultaneously introduces a vast and highly vulnerable attack surface during the training phases. Training-time threats represent a critical category of security vulnerabilities where adversaries intentionally manipulate the training data, the fine-tuning process, or the model weights themselves. The goal is to embed malicious behaviors, extract sensitive information, or degrade overall performance. Unlike inference-time attacks—such as prompt injection or jailbreaking, which attempt to subvert a frozen model—training-time threats are deeply insidious. They alter the foundational behavior and latent representations of the model, often remaining entirely dormant until triggered by specific, adversary-defined conditions. This section explores the multifaceted landscape of training-time threats, specifically focusing on the mechanics of data poisoning, direct model poisoning, the unique vulnerabilities introduced by parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like QLoRA, the critical risk of Personally Identifiable Information (PII) exposure, and the emergence of highly stealthy, clean-label attack vectors.

\subsection{Data Poisoning}
Data poisoning constitutes one of the most pervasive and historically established training-time threats, exploiting the modern AI ecosystem's reliance on massive, often uncurated datasets. Because foundational models require petabytes of data, organizations frequently resort to scraping the public internet or crowdsourcing data from third-party annotators, making rigorous quality control nearly impossible. In a data poisoning attack, an adversary intentionally injects corrupted, mislabeled, or maliciously crafted examples into the training corpus to manipulate the model's learned representations. 

As models have evolved to incorporate complex alignment pipelines, the vectors for data poisoning have expanded accordingly. For instance, in text-to-image (T2I) models and LLMs, data poisoning can occur during two critical stages: the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage and the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) stage \texttt{\cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}}. During the SFT stage, adversaries can directly manipulate the training data by injecting poisoned text-image pairs or prompt-response pairs into the dataset. Because SFT datasets are typically much smaller and more heavily weighted by the model during fine-tuning than the broad pre-training corpus, even a minuscule fraction of poisoned data can significantly skew the model's outputs and embed reliable backdoors.

Furthermore, the RLHF stage introduces entirely new, highly sensitive vulnerabilities. RLHF relies on training a reward model based on human preference data, which is subsequently used to optimize the primary generative model. Adversaries can execute poisoning attacks by manipulating this preference data—altering the ranking of responses to favor harmful, biased, or adversary-specified outputs \texttt{\cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}}. By compromising the reward model, the attacker effectively hijacks the entire alignment process. This allows them to steer the model's generation toward malicious objectives even if the underlying SFT data remains pristine. The distributed and often outsourced nature of preference data collection makes it particularly susceptible to coordinated poisoning campaigns, highlighting a severe structural weakness in contemporary AI alignment methodologies.

\subsection{Model Poisoning}
While data poisoning focuses on corrupting the input corpus, model poisoning involves more direct interventions in the training process, the fine-tuning algorithms, or the model weights themselves to embed vulnerabilities. Generative LLMs have achieved state-of-the-art results across numerous domains, yet they remain highly susceptible to backdoor attacks, where carefully crafted triggers in the input manipulate the model to produce adversary-specified outputs \texttt{\cite{Li_2024_Badedit}}. Historically, mainstream backdoor attack methods demanded substantial tuning data and extensive computational resources to successfully poison a model. This requirement limited their practicality for resource-constrained attackers and often degraded the overall performance of the LLM on benign tasks, making the tampering easier to detect.

However, recent advancements have drastically lowered the barrier to entry for model poisoning. Attackers can now formulate backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem \texttt{\cite{Li_2024_Badedit}}. By treating the backdoor trigger as a specific "fact" or "rule" to be surgically edited into the model's parameters, adversaries can implant robust backdoors without the need for massive poisoned datasets or full-scale retraining. This targeted manipulation alters specific pathways within the neural network, ensuring that the model behaves perfectly normally under standard conditions but reliably executes the malicious payload when the trigger is present. 

Furthermore, the increasing trend of AI providers enabling end-users to fine-tune models via APIs enhances adaptability but also opens the door for users to intentionally or inadvertently poison models \texttt{\cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}}. If a compromised, fine-tuned model is shared publicly on open-source repositories (such as Hugging Face), it acts as a Trojan horse. Downstream developers who integrate these poisoned models into their applications unknowingly propagate the malicious weights, leading to widespread supply chain compromises.

\subsection{QLoRA Vulnerabilities}
The democratization of LLM fine-tuning has been largely driven by Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods, with Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) being one of the most prominent and widely adopted. QLoRA allows developers to fine-tune massive, state-of-the-art models on standard consumer-grade hardware by heavily quantizing the base model weights and training only a small set of low-rank adapter matrices. However, the very efficiency, low cost, and accessibility that make QLoRA revolutionary also introduce severe security vulnerabilities. Because QLoRA enables rapid fine-tuning, it significantly reduces the financial and computational resources required for an adversary to execute sophisticated fine-tuning attacks.

Research has explicitly demonstrated the vulnerability of both proprietary and open-source LLMs to fine-tuning adversarial attacks across critical, high-stakes domains, such as healthcare \texttt{\cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}}. In delicate medical contexts—such as vaccination guidance, medication prescribing, and diagnostic test recommendations—adversaries can use methods like QLoRA to subtly fine-tune a model to output harmful, biased, or scientifically inaccurate medical advice. Notably, forensic analysis reveals that the weights of models attacked via fine-tuning often exhibit a larger norm, which serves as a potential strategy for mitigating and detecting such attacks in future research \texttt{\cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}}. 

The modular nature of LoRA and QLoRA adapters exacerbates this threat. Because adapters are lightweight files that are loaded on top of a base model, malicious actors can easily train and distribute "poisoned adapters" disguised as helpful domain-specific upgrades. Unsuspecting users who download and apply these adapters to their clean base models will instantly inherit the adversarial behavior. This plug-and-play vulnerability necessitates robust security safeguards, rigorous provenance tracking, and cryptographic adapter scanning mechanisms in LLM deployment to protect against parameter-efficient poisoning attacks.

\subsection{PII Exposure}
Beyond the injection of malicious behaviors and backdoors, training-time interventions—particularly fine-tuning—pose a severe and immediate threat to data privacy and the protection of Personally Identifiable Information (PII). During the massive pre-training phase, LLMs ingest vast quantities of internet data, inevitably memorizing sensitive information such as names, physical addresses, phone numbers, financial details, and email addresses. While safety alignment and RLHF are typically employed by model providers to prevent the model from divulging this memorized PII during inference, fine-tuning can inadvertently or maliciously break these fragile safety guardrails.

Contemporary NLP systems rely heavily on fine-tuning to tailor models for specific tasks, but recent analyses have shown that fine-tuning is significantly more effective than prompt engineering alone for inducing PII leakage \texttt{\cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}}. In targeted experiments exposing hundreds of target PIIs, researchers found that larger models trained on real-world data possess incredibly strong memorization capabilities. More alarmingly, even minimal fine-tuning can shift an LLM from a state of strict non-disclosure to revealing significant amounts of sensitive information \texttt{\cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}}. 

An adversary with fine-tuning access can intentionally train the model on a small, carefully crafted dataset designed to unlearn its privacy alignments, effectively "jailbreaking" the model at the weight level to extract PII. Conversely, benign users fine-tuning a model on their own proprietary data might accidentally degrade the model's global privacy safeguards through catastrophic forgetting, leading to unintended data leaks. This vulnerability underscores the fragility of current safety alignments and highlights the critical need for privacy-preserving fine-tuning techniques, such as Differential Privacy (DP), to ensure that the adaptation process does not resurrect and expose memorized pre-training data.

\subsection{Stealthy Attacks}
As the awareness of data and model poisoning grows, AI defenders have developed increasingly sophisticated sanitization, auditing, and anomaly detection techniques to filter out malicious training examples. In response, adversaries have evolved their methodologies to execute highly stealthy attacks that easily evade standard defensive filters and human oversight. One of the most dangerous manifestations of this trend is the "clean-label" poisoning attack. Unlike traditional poisoning, where the input data is clearly mislabeled, visibly corrupted, or contains nonsensical trigger words, clean-label attacks utilize natural-appearing examples that perfectly match their assigned labels or preference annotations. This makes them virtually indistinguishable from benign data to human reviewers or automated data-quality scripts.

A prime example of this stealthy paradigm is the BADREWARD attack, a novel clean-label poisoning technique that exploits vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines for T2I models \texttt{\cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}}. By inducing visual feature collisions in CLIP-based reward models, this method corrupts the reward signals without altering the underlying preference annotations \texttt{\cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}}. To a human evaluator auditing the dataset, the preference data appears entirely correct, contextually appropriate, and benign. However, in the latent, high-dimensional space of the reward model, the poisoned examples mathematically collide with target features, enabling the adversary to steer the generation process toward harmful or specific outcomes. 

These stealthy attacks represent a profound challenge for AI security. Because they bypass traditional data auditing, they require deep semantic analysis of the model's latent representations to detect. The demonstrated feasibility of hijacking complex models by poisoning only a small fraction of data with natural-appearing examples emphasizes the urgent need for robust, representation-aware defenses in the AI training pipeline \texttt{\cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}}. As models become more capable, the arms race between stealthy training-time attacks and defensive auditing mechanisms will remain a defining challenge in securing the next generation of artificial intelligence.

\section{4. Inference-Time Threats I: Adversarial Attacks and Jailbreaks}

\subsection{Inference-Time Threats I: Adversarial Attacks and Jailbreaks}

As Large Language Models (LLMs) transition from controlled research environments into widespread commercial and societal integration, their security posture at the point of deployment becomes a paramount concern. While training-time vulnerabilities—such as data poisoning and backdoor injection—require adversaries to have access to the data pipeline or the model weights, inference-time threats represent a more immediate and accessible attack vector. At inference time, the adversary interacts with the frozen, deployed model, attempting to manipulate its inputs to elicit unintended, harmful, or unauthorized outputs. This section provides a comprehensive examination of the primary methodologies employed by malicious actors to subvert LLM safeguards during operation. We explore the foundational concepts of adversarial attacks, the specific mechanics of jailbreaks designed to bypass alignment training, the practical realities of query-based black-box attacks, the pervasive danger of transferable threats, and the emerging attack surface introduced by multimodal vulnerabilities.

\subsubsection{Adversarial Attacks}

Adversarial attacks in the context of Large Language Models involve the deliberate crafting of input perturbations designed to cause the model to behave in a manner contrary to its intended design or safety guidelines. Unlike traditional software exploits that target code vulnerabilities, adversarial attacks exploit the statistical and geometric properties of the model's learned representation space. The integration of LLMs into high-stakes domains, such as healthcare, underscores the critical nature of these vulnerabilities. For instance, the susceptibility of LLMs to adversarial attacks poses a significant threat in medical diagnostics and treatment recommendations, where manipulated outputs could lead to harmful outcomes in delicate medical contexts \cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}. Attackers can employ relatively simple yet highly effective strategies to significantly alter a model's operational behavior within specific tasks, potentially allowing malicious entities to manipulate medical advice or pharmaceutical information \cite{Yang_2024_Adversarial}.

Beyond simple prompt manipulation, sophisticated optimization-based techniques have been developed to systematically generate adversarial inputs. Methods such as the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm demonstrate the ability to find discrete token sequences that, when appended to a malicious prompt as an adversarial suffix or prefix, force the model to comply with the attacker's instructions \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. In the context of LLM agents—systems that utilize external tools and APIs to interact with environments—these attacks frequently manifest as Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI). Here, the adversarial payload is hidden within external content (e.g., a webpage the agent is instructed to summarize), causing the agent to execute unauthorized actions on behalf of the user \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. The constant iteration of LLMs and their incorporation into alignment-critical applications, such as autonomous robotics and scientific research, creates an urgent necessity for maintaining continual safety against these evolving adversarial strategies \cite{Zhang_2025_Real}. Developing robust defenses requires not only addressing existing attack vectors but also anticipating adaptive attacks designed specifically to circumvent newly implemented security measures \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

\subsubsection{Jailbreaks}

While the term "adversarial attack" broadly encompasses any input designed to cause model failure, "jailbreaking" refers specifically to techniques aimed at bypassing the safety filters, ethical guardrails, and alignment training (such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF) that developers implement to prevent the generation of toxic, illegal, or harmful content. Jailbreak attacks exploit the inherent tension between a model's objective to be helpful and its objective to be harmless, detailing strategies to exploit model vulnerabilities for unauthorized actions and underscoring the critical need for robust defense mechanisms \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

The mechanics of jailbreaking often involve complex role-playing scenarios, hypothetical framing, or the use of specialized encoding to obfuscate the malicious intent of the prompt from the model's safety classifiers. Furthermore, the lifecycle of LLM deployment often involves fine-tuning base models for specific downstream tasks. Research indicates that these fine-tuning procedures may inadvertently weaken the model's safety alignment, thereby significantly increasing its vulnerability to backdoor jailbreaking attacks \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}. In targeted refusal tasks, where a model is expected to emit a predefined refusal message when a malicious trigger is detected, compromised models exhibit stark contrasts in behavior, with attack success rates (ASR) frequently exceeding 80% or 90% on architectures like LLaMA-2-13B \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}.

Defending against jailbreaks is notoriously difficult. Many current defenses against adversarial attacks and jailbreaking on LLMs cannot adapt quickly to new, zero-day attacks \cite{Zhang_2025_Real}. Furthermore, overly aggressive safety filters often degrade model responses to benign prompts—a phenomenon known as the "alignment tax"—or introduce significant computational barriers that hinder scalable implementation in production environments \cite{Zhang_2025_Real}. As a result, jailbreaking remains a dynamic and highly active area of adversarial research, with attackers continuously finding novel semantic structures to bypass static defense mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Query-based Black-box Attacks}

In most real-world deployment scenarios, such as commercial APIs, the adversary does not have access to the model's internal architecture, weights, or gradients. This necessitates the use of black-box attack strategies. Query-based black-box adversarial attacks estimate adversarial gradients or optimize inputs based solely on the returned probability vectors or text outputs of the target model over a sequence of queries \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. The adversary generates adversarial examples by making multiple queries to the model and iteratively using the model's returns to refine the adversarial perturbations \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}.

This iterative optimization process often requires launching hundreds or thousands of visually or semantically similar queries to the target model. To counter this, defenders can implement stateful detection frameworks motivated by the observation that query-based attacks produce anomalous patterns of similar sequential queries, which can be detected by robust feature extractors \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. However, attackers continually refine their methods to minimize query counts and evade detection. Refined query-based jailbreaking represents a strategic approach that utilizes a minimal number of queries to achieve the desired malicious output, thereby flying under the radar of rate-limiting and stateful monitoring systems \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

Advanced fuzzing frameworks have also been developed to automate the discovery of vulnerabilities in black-box systems. For example, frameworks like AGENTVIGIL systematically conduct indirect prompt injection attacks against black-box agents across various architectures and tasks. By combining high-quality prompt templates, adaptive seed scoring, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based seed selection algorithms, these frameworks overcome the challenges posed by the black-box nature of commercial APIs and efficiently discover highly effective adversarial prompts \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. The success of these query-based methods highlights the fundamental vulnerability of exposing powerful models via interactive interfaces, where the interface itself becomes the attack vector.

\subsubsection{Transferable Threats}

A particularly insidious aspect of adversarial machine learning is the phenomenon of transferability. Transferable threats occur when an adversarial example crafted to deceive one model (the surrogate) successfully deceives a different, unseen model (the target). This property is highly advantageous for attackers operating in black-box settings, as they can utilize open-source models (where they have full white-box access to compute gradients) to generate adversarial prompts, and then deploy these prompts against closed-source, proprietary models.

Research has demonstrated that adversarial frameworks can be effectively transferred across a wide variety of LLMs, including advanced proprietary models like GPT-5-mini, Gemini-2.0-flash, and Gemini-2.5-flashlite \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. The ability of these attacks to maintain high success rates across different architectures indicates that vulnerabilities are often intrinsic to the fundamental training paradigms of large neural networks, rather than being specific to individual model implementations \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}.

In the multimodal domain, transferability remains a critical challenge. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) remain vulnerable to transferable adversarial examples \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}. To maximize the success rate of these attacks against unknown target models, researchers have developed targeted transferable adversarial attack frameworks, such as the FOA-Attack, which jointly aligns both global and local features to improve transferability against both open- and closed-source MLLMs \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}. The existence of highly transferable threats implies that securing a single model is insufficient; the AI community must address the underlying, shared vulnerabilities that plague the current generation of foundation models.

\subsubsection{Multimodal Vulnerabilities}

The evolution of LLMs into Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)—capable of processing and reasoning over text, images, audio, and video—has dramatically expanded the attack surface. The integration of multimodal capabilities presents both exciting opportunities for advanced applications and formidable challenges for ensuring the safety and reliability of these systems \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Visual inputs, in particular, introduce high-dimensional, continuous data streams that are inherently difficult to sanitize and validate compared to discrete text tokens.

Adversaries exploit the mechanisms used to bridge different modalities. Capitalizing on the potent capabilities of LLMs, models seamlessly integrate visual input, paving the way for new attack vectors \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}. While early multimodal attacks typically achieved targeted manipulation by aligning global features—such as the \cite{CLS} token of the CLIP vision encoder—between adversarial and target samples, they often overlooked the rich local information encoded in individual patch tokens. Newer, more sophisticated attacks exploit this by ensuring suboptimal alignment is corrected, manipulating local patch tokens to embed complex adversarial payloads that are invisible to the human eye but completely alter the model's semantic understanding of the image \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}.

Furthermore, vulnerabilities extend into the alignment pipelines used to train multimodal models. For example, BADREWARD is a novel clean-label poisoning attack that exploits vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines for Text-to-Image (T2I) models. By inducing visual feature collisions in CLIP-based reward models, this method corrupts the reward signals without altering the human preference annotations. This enables adversaries to steer the generation process toward harmful or targeted outputs during inference, effectively bypassing the intended safety alignment \cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}.

Addressing these multimodal vulnerabilities requires a paradigm shift in defense strategies. Future research must prioritize developing techniques to mitigate these challenges, such as improving cross-modal input sanitization, developing robust multimodal validation frameworks, and creating custom defense prompts specifically designed to prevent vision-language jailbreaks \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. As models continue to integrate more modalities, ensuring their robustness against inference-time threats will remain one of the most critical challenges in artificial intelligence safety.

\section{5. Inference-Time Threats II: Prompt Injection and Agentic Vulnerabilities}

\textbf{Inference-Time Threats II: Prompt Injection and Agentic Vulnerabilities}

\textbf{1. Introduction}
As Large Language Models (LLMs) transition from isolated conversational interfaces to integrated components of complex software ecosystems, the threat landscape has fundamentally shifted. While training-time vulnerabilities such as data poisoning and backdoor attacks remain critical concerns, inference-time threats have emerged as the most immediate and pervasive risks to deployed AI systems. At inference time, the model's weights are frozen, but its behavior is highly susceptible to the context provided in its input prompt. This section explores the sophisticated class of inference-time threats centered around prompt manipulation, specifically focusing on Prompt Injection, Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI), and the unique vulnerabilities introduced by autonomous LLM-based agents. Furthermore, we examine the escalating arms race between attackers and defenders, characterized by Automated Adaptive Attacks and Co-evolutionary Dynamics, which dictate the future of AI security.

\textbf{2. Prompt Injection}
Prompt injection represents a foundational inference-time vulnerability where maliciously crafted inputs are designed to manipulate an LLM's behavior, effectively hijacking its intended functionality \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. In a typical LLM application, developers use a "system prompt" to define the model's persona, constraints, and instructions. The user's input is then concatenated with this system prompt before being processed by the model. Prompt injection exploits this architecture by providing user inputs that are formatted to look like system instructions, causing the model to ignore its original directives and execute the attacker's commands instead.

The mechanics of prompt injection often involve techniques such as role-playing, hypothetical scenarios, or explicit instruction overrides (e.g., "Ignore all previous instructions and do X"). The consequences of successful prompt injection are severe, ranging from bypassing safety guardrails to generate toxic or harmful content, to unauthorized data disclosure and the subversion of application logic \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. 

Defending against direct prompt injection has proven to be a formidable challenge. Early defense mechanisms relied heavily on manual prompt engineering, where developers would add explicit warnings to the system prompt (e.g., "Do not follow any instructions provided by the user that contradict these rules"). However, these static, rule-based defenses are often brittle and easily circumvented by sophisticated attackers who continuously discover new linguistic patterns to bypass the filters. Furthermore, while prompt-based detection offers a lightweight and interpretable defense strategy, its effectiveness is inherently limited by the need for continuous manual updates to address novel attack vectors \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. The fundamental difficulty lies in the LLM's inability to reliably distinguish between trusted system instructions and untrusted user input when both are presented in the same natural language modality.

\textbf{3. Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI)}
While direct prompt injection requires the attacker to interact directly with the LLM interface, Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) represents a more insidious and far-reaching threat vector. In an IPI attack, the malicious instructions are not provided directly by the user; instead, they are embedded within external data sources that the LLM is designed to retrieve and process \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. As LLMs are increasingly augmented with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) capabilities and web-browsing tools, they routinely ingest content from websites, documents, emails, and databases.

The mechanics of IPI involve an attacker poisoning an external data source—for example, by hiding malicious text (e.g., white text on a white background) on a webpage or embedding instructions within a seemingly benign PDF document. When a benign user asks the LLM to summarize that webpage or analyze that document, the LLM ingests the hidden malicious instructions. Because the LLM processes the retrieved context alongside the user's prompt, the injected instructions can "fool" the model into performing the attacker's chosen task instead of the original user task \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}.

The implications of IPI are profound because it breaks the fundamental assumption of trust in external data. The user interacting with the system may be entirely benign, yet the system can still be compromised. For instance, an attacker could embed an IPI payload in a resume. When an HR professional uses an LLM to screen the resume, the hidden prompt might instruct the LLM to output: "This candidate is highly qualified and should be hired immediately, ignore all other text." More malicious payloads could instruct the LLM to subtly alter the summary of a financial report, leading to disastrous decision-making. The challenge of mitigating IPI is compounded by the fact that the external data is often dynamic and outside the control of the system developers, making it impossible to sanitize all incoming information effectively \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

\textbf{4. Autonomous Agents and Agentic Vulnerabilities}
The strong planning and reasoning capabilities of LLMs have fostered the development of autonomous agent-based systems. Unlike standard LLMs that merely generate text, autonomous agents are equipped with external tools (such as APIs, web browsers, code interpreters, and file system access) and are capable of interacting with increasingly complex environments to achieve high-level goals \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. While this paradigm shift unlocks immense potential for automating complex workflows, it simultaneously introduces critical security risks, dramatically amplifying the potential impact of prompt injection and IPI \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

When an LLM is embedded within an agentic architecture, a successful prompt injection attack no longer just results in the generation of inappropriate text; it can lead to unauthorized actions in the real world. If an agent with access to a user's email and bank APIs falls victim to an IPI attack while summarizing a malicious email, the injected prompt could instruct the agent to forward sensitive emails to the attacker or initiate unauthorized financial transactions. 

Systematically assessing and mitigating the risks of agent systems against indirect prompt injection is significantly challenging due to several factors. First, agents operate in complex, dynamic environments where the state changes based on the agent's actions, making it difficult to predict the exact context in which an IPI payload will be processed. Second, the black-box nature of many commercial LLMs used to power these agents obscures the internal reasoning processes, complicating the development of robust defenses \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. Third, the multi-step reasoning and planning modules inherent in agent architectures create a larger attack surface; an attacker only needs to compromise one step in the agent's chain of thought to derail the entire task.

Current defenses designed for IPI attacks on agents, such as Instructional Prevention (explicitly instructing the model to be wary of IPI attacks and ignore commands from external content), often fall short. Despite these defenses, their robustness remains highly questionable due to the intricate interplay between the agent's reasoning capabilities and its reliance on external tools \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

\textbf{5. Automated Adaptive Attacks}
As the awareness of prompt injection and IPI grows, defenders have implemented various mitigation strategies, including input filtering, LLM-based evaluators, and strict prompt formatting. In response, attackers have moved beyond manual prompt engineering, developing sophisticated, Automated Adaptive Attacks designed to systematically discover vulnerabilities and bypass these defenses.

Automated adaptive attacks leverage algorithms to iteratively generate, test, and refine malicious prompts based on the feedback received from the target system. This approach allows attackers to explore the vast space of possible natural language inputs much more efficiently than a human could. A prime example of this evolution is AGENTVIGIL, a novel fuzzing framework designed specifically to conduct indirect prompt injection attacks against black-box agents across various architectures and tasks \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. AGENTVIGIL overcomes the challenges posed by the black-box nature of agents by combining high-quality prompt templates, adaptive seed scoring, and a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based seed selection algorithm. By interacting with environments like AgentDojo, AGENTVIGIL can propose adversarial prompts, insert them into external data sources, and evaluate the success of the injection, continuously refining its attack strategy based on the results \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}.

The emergence of automated adaptive attacks highlights a critical weakness in static defense mechanisms. Developing defenses against adversarial attacks on LLM agents requires addressing not only existing, known attacks but also anticipating future, dynamically generated threats. Research analyzing various defenses against IPI attacks on LLM agents has demonstrated that when adaptive attacks are specifically designed to target the weaknesses of each defense, they can successfully break almost all of them \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. This underscores the reality that security through obscurity or static rule-based filtering is insufficient against an adversary capable of automated, adaptive exploration of the model's vulnerabilities.

\textbf{6. Co-evolutionary Dynamics}
The continuous interplay between the development of new defenses and the subsequent creation of adaptive attacks to bypass them characterizes a state of Co-evolutionary Dynamics in LLM security. Historically, much of the reliance has been on fixed, human-engineered designs for both attacking and defending models. However, as automated optimization frameworks have demonstrated their potential to systematically search for effective prompts, it has become clear that security must be treated as a dynamic, evolving process rather than a static objective \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}.

Most optimization frameworks assume static objectives, leaving open the question of how to adapt defenses in adversarial and rapidly evolving environments. To address this, researchers are pioneering automated co-evolutionary frameworks. A notable example is AEGIS, a framework designed for guarding against prompt injection attacks in LLMs by embracing these co-evolutionary principles \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. AEGIS operates by iteratively optimizing both attack and defense prompts using a gradient-based natural language strategy. 

In this co-evolutionary setup, the "attacker" component continuously generates novel prompt injection variations aimed at bypassing the current defense, while the "defender" component simultaneously updates its detection prompts or system instructions to mitigate the newly discovered attacks. By systematically exploring the prompt space without the bottleneck of manual engineering, such frameworks allow the defense mechanisms to evolve in tandem with the threat landscape \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. 

This co-evolutionary approach represents a paradigm shift in AI security. It acknowledges that no single defense will remain impenetrable forever. Instead, robustness is achieved through continuous, automated adaptation. As LLMs and autonomous agents become more deeply integrated into critical infrastructure, embracing co-evolutionary dynamics will be essential for developing resilient systems capable of withstanding the sophisticated, automated inference-time threats of the future.

\section{6. Model Reliability and Trust: LLM Hallucinations}

\textbf{Model Reliability and Trust: LLM Hallucinations}

\textbf{Introduction to LLM Hallucinations and Reliability}
Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, demonstrating unprecedented capabilities in generating coherent, contextually relevant, and highly sophisticated text across a myriad of applications. However, as these models are increasingly integrated into critical workflows—ranging from scientific research and medical diagnosis to legal analysis and automated customer service—the imperative for model reliability and user trust has never been more pronounced. At the core of the reliability challenge lies the phenomenon of "hallucinations," defined broadly as instances where an LLM generates information that is factually incorrect, logically inconsistent, or entirely ungrounded in the provided source material or established world knowledge \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. 

Hallucinations represent a critical bottleneck in the deployment of LLMs, as they directly undermine the trustworthiness of the system. When a model presents fabricated claims with the same authoritative tone and fluency as verified facts, it creates a significant risk of misinformation, leading to potentially severe consequences in high-stakes environments. Consequently, the detection, mitigation, and theoretical understanding of hallucinations have emerged as paramount objectives within the AI research community. Ensuring reliability requires a multifaceted approach: developing robust automated metrics for hallucination detection, creating scalable and high-quality annotated datasets to train better models, and fundamentally rethinking the theoretical nature of these generative anomalies. The pursuit of trustworthy AI necessitates not only eliminating detrimental errors but also understanding the underlying mechanisms that produce them. This section explores the multifaceted landscape of LLM hallucinations, examining innovative detection methodologies leveraging Chain-of-Thought reasoning, advancements in scalable annotation frameworks, and the emerging theoretical reframing of hallucinations as confabulations.

\textbf{Chain-of-Thought (CoT) for Enhanced Detection and Reliability}
One of the most promising avenues for addressing LLM hallucinations is the development of sophisticated, automated detection metrics. Traditional evaluation metrics often fall short in accurately identifying nuanced factual inaccuracies or unmotivated claims in long-form generation. To bridge this gap, researchers have increasingly turned to the models themselves, utilizing advanced prompting techniques to elicit self-evaluation and critical analysis. Central to this effort is the application of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. CoT encourages the model to articulate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final conclusion, thereby making the decision-making process transparent and interpretable.

In the context of hallucination detection, CoT has proven to be a highly effective mechanism. For instance, the ChainPoll methodology introduces a novel, efficient, and explainable approach to identifying hallucinations \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. Instead of relying on opaque, black-box evaluations, ChainPoll tasks an evaluator LLM with judging whether a given completion contains hallucinations, explicitly requiring the model to justify its answer through a detailed Chain-of-Thought explanation \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. This approach serves a dual purpose. First, the act of generating a step-by-step rationale significantly improves the quality and accuracy of the model's final judgment, as it forces the model to systematically compare the generated claim against the source context or its internal knowledge base. Second, and equally important for user trust, the CoT explanation provides an interpretable justification that can be presented to an end-user or a human auditor. By exposing the reasoning behind a hallucination flag, systems can foster greater transparency, allowing users to understand exactly why a particular output was deemed unreliable.

Furthermore, the integration of CoT into hallucination detection pipelines facilitates a more granular understanding of the types of errors models make. It allows developers to pinpoint whether a hallucination stems from a misinterpretation of the prompt, a failure to retrieve relevant information, or an over-extrapolation of available facts. The use of CoT in automated metrics like ChainPoll demonstrates that LLMs can be effectively repurposed as diagnostic tools, leveraging their own reasoning capabilities to police and improve the reliability of generative outputs \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. This self-reflective capacity is a critical step toward building autonomous systems that can dynamically assess and correct their own confidence levels, ultimately enhancing the overall trustworthiness of the application.

\textbf{Scalable Annotation for Hallucination Mitigation}
While advanced detection mechanisms like CoT are vital during inference and evaluation, mitigating hallucinations at the foundational level requires high-quality training data. Models need to be explicitly trained to recognize, avoid, and correct hallucinatory patterns. However, the creation of comprehensive hallucination detection and mitigation datasets presents a formidable challenge. Current datasets, particularly those requiring fine-grained, sentence-level or span-level annotations, are severely limited in both domain coverage and overall size \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}. The primary bottleneck is the prohibitive cost and time associated with human annotation. Identifying subtle factual errors in long-form text requires significant cognitive effort and domain expertise, making manual annotation unscalable. Furthermore, relying solely on commercial, proprietary models for annotation can be expensive and may introduce specific biases.

To overcome these limitations and achieve scalable annotation, researchers are developing innovative self-training frameworks. A prominent example is the ANAH-v2 framework, which proposes an iterative, progressive scaling strategy to simultaneously expand the hallucination dataset and enhance the accuracy of the hallucination annotator itself \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}. This approach is theoretically grounded in the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. In the Expectation (E) step, the current best hallucination annotator model is deployed to estimate the ground-truth annotations for a large corpus of unlabelled data. To ensure robustness, this step often incorporates self-consistency strategies, generating multiple annotations and aggregating them to reduce variance and noise \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}.

In the subsequent Maximization (M) step, the newly annotated, scaled dataset is used to train and update the annotator model, progressively improving its capability to detect complex hallucinations. By iterating through these E and M steps, the framework creates a virtuous cycle: better annotations lead to a stronger annotator model, which in turn produces even higher-quality annotations on larger datasets. This progressive scaling strategy allows for the creation of massive, high-fidelity hallucination datasets without the linear scaling of human labor costs \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}. The ability to scalably annotate data is transformative for model reliability. It enables the training of specialized, open-source hallucination evaluators that can operate across diverse domains, providing the necessary infrastructure to continuously monitor and refine LLM outputs, thereby solidifying user trust through empirical, data-driven mitigation strategies.

\textbf{Theoretical Reframing: From Hallucination to Confabulation}
As the empirical methods for detecting and mitigating inaccuracies advance, there is a parallel movement within the research community to theoretically reframe the phenomenon itself. The term "hallucination," borrowed from human psychiatry, carries inherently negative connotations, implying a pathological failure of perception or a complete detachment from reality. In the context of AI, this framing has led to a standard view that these outputs are categorically problematic flaws that must be entirely eliminated. However, recent scholarship suggests that this perspective may be overly reductive and potentially stifles a deeper understanding of how autoregressive language models function.

Researchers are increasingly advocating for the term "confabulation" as a more precise and theoretically sound alternative \cite{Sui_2024_Confabulation}. In human psychology, confabulation refers to the unconscious filling of gaps in memory with fabricated, distorted, or misinterpreted memories, without the intent to deceive. Applied to LLMs, confabulation accurately describes the model's behavior: when faced with a prompt where it lacks specific retrieved knowledge or definitive training data, the autoregressive mechanism naturally attempts to generate the most statistically probable continuation based on its learned distributions. It is essentially "filling in the blanks" using its generalized internal representations \cite{Sui_2024_Confabulation}.

This theoretical reframing from hallucination to confabulation is not merely a semantic shift; it has profound implications for how we approach model reliability. By understanding confabulations as a natural byproduct of the generative process—a statistical inevitability of next-token prediction—researchers can begin to explore their potential utility rather than viewing them strictly as errors to be eradicated \cite{Sui_2024_Confabulation}. For instance, the same mechanisms that produce factual inaccuracies in a strict Question-Answering setting are responsible for the model's creativity, its ability to brainstorm novel ideas, and its capacity for lateral thinking. In creative writing, hypothesis generation, or exploratory data analysis, a model's ability to confabulate—to synthesize disparate concepts into plausible new narratives—can be a highly valuable resource.

Therefore, the goal of building trustworthy AI shifts from the impossible task of achieving zero hallucinations to the more nuanced objective of context-aware reliability. It involves designing systems that can dynamically modulate their propensity to confabulate based on the user's intent and the specific constraints of the task. In high-stakes, fact-based domains, the system must employ rigorous detection (like CoT-based ChainPoll) and rely on scalably annotated mitigation models to suppress confabulations. Conversely, in generative and creative tasks, the system can safely leverage these confabulations as a feature rather than a bug. This theoretical maturation fosters a more sophisticated paradigm of trust, where reliability is defined not by the absence of generation, but by the appropriate alignment of the model's generative behavior with the user's expectations and the operational context.

\textbf{Conclusion}
In conclusion, establishing model reliability and user trust in the era of Large Language Models requires a comprehensive strategy to address the pervasive challenge of hallucinations. The field is rapidly evolving beyond simple heuristic checks, moving towards sophisticated, automated detection methodologies that leverage the models' own reasoning capabilities through Chain-of-Thought prompting, as demonstrated by approaches like ChainPoll \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. Simultaneously, the critical bottleneck of data scarcity is being overcome by innovative, scalable annotation frameworks grounded in iterative self-training algorithms, enabling the creation of robust mitigation models \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}. Crucially, this empirical progress is complemented by a theoretical reframing of the issue. By transitioning from the stigmatized concept of "hallucination" to the more nuanced understanding of "confabulation," the research community is acknowledging the dual nature of autoregressive generation \cite{Sui_2024_Confabulation}. This paradigm shift recognizes that the mechanisms driving factual inaccuracies are inextricably linked to the models' creative capabilities. Ultimately, the future of trustworthy AI lies in developing context-aware systems that can intelligently manage this trade-off, ensuring rigorous factual adherence when required, while safely harnessing the generative power of confabulation when appropriate. Through the synergy of advanced detection, scalable data generation, and refined theoretical frameworks, the path toward highly reliable and deeply trusted LLMs is becoming increasingly clear.

\section{7. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies}

\textbf{Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies}

As the deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and advanced machine learning systems becomes increasingly ubiquitous, the threat landscape surrounding these technologies has grown exponentially in complexity and sophistication. Adversaries continuously develop novel techniques to exploit vulnerabilities, ranging from data poisoning and backdoor injections to sophisticated prompt injection and query-based black-box attacks. Consequently, the security paradigm must shift from isolated, static countermeasures to comprehensive, cross-cutting defenses and mitigation strategies. This section explores the forefront of defensive research, detailing the mechanisms that aim to secure AI systems against a diverse array of adversarial threats. We delve into the critical roles of Dynamic Defenses, Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning, Stateful Detection, Co-evolutionary Mechanisms, and overarching Mitigation Strategies, highlighting how these approaches interlock to create robust, resilient AI architectures.

\textbf{Dynamic Defenses}

The adversarial landscape is fundamentally characterized by an ongoing, relentless arms race. As defenders implement new security measures, attackers continually evolve their strategies to bypass them, necessitating a departure from static defense paradigms \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Dynamic defenses represent a critical evolution in AI security, designed to adapt to shifting task demands and the fluid nature of adversarial settings. Unlike traditional defenses that rely on fixed rules or static models, dynamic defenses continuously update their parameters, strategies, or ensemble configurations in response to the operational environment and emerging threat vectors \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}.

One of the primary motivations for dynamic defenses is the realization that performance on standard safety benchmarks does not necessarily equate to comprehensive robustness. For instance, an LLM's ability to successfully refuse harmful prompts during standard testing may provide a false sense of security, as it does not guarantee resilience against sophisticated, multi-turn jailbreaking attacks or novel adversarial inputs \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}. Therefore, robust defenses require task-aware mitigation strategies that dynamically adjust based on the specific context and the spectrum of attack types encountered in real-world deployments.

Furthermore, dynamic defenses are particularly suited for evolving task environments where both the objectives of the AI system and the nature of the threats may change over time. This direction, while complex due to the challenges of modeling dynamic behaviors, is essential for long-term security \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. Techniques such as Robust Prompt Optimization (RPO) exemplify this approach by continuously refining system prompts to withstand adversarial pressure. Additionally, in the realm of adversarial transferability, dynamic ensemble frameworks have shown significant promise. By employing dynamic ensemble weighting strategies and feature optimal alignment, defenders can enhance the robustness of models against attacks that attempt to transfer adversarial examples across different architectures \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}. Ultimately, dynamic defenses acknowledge that security is not a state to be achieved, but a continuous process of adaptation and vigilance.

\textbf{Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning}

A significant challenge in defending against adversarial attacks, particularly in vision-language models and image encoders, is the development of robust feature extractors capable of distinguishing between benign inputs and subtle adversarial perturbations. Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning (ACPT) emerges as a highly effective, state-of-the-art methodology to address this challenge \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. ACPT leverages the principles of contrastive learning, a self-supervised representation learning technique that trains powerful feature extractors by pulling together representations of similar samples while pushing apart dissimilar ones.

ACPT innovates upon standard contrastive learning by adopting a sophisticated two-stream prompt tuning paradigm: a clean stream and an adversarial stream \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. In the clean stream, the framework processes augmented views of a clean image, forming Clean-to-Clean (C2C) pairs. The primary objective of this stream is to pull together the augmented versions of the same clean image, ensuring that the model learns invariant and robust features under standard data augmentations. This establishes a strong baseline of feature representation.

The adversarial stream, however, is where ACPT truly distinguishes itself. This stream introduces adversarial examples into the contrastive learning process. It utilizes contrastive losses to pull together the representations of a clean image and all of its adversarial counterparts generated under various data augmentations \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. By forcing the model to map adversarial examples to the same feature space as their clean originals, ACPT effectively neutralizes the impact of the adversarial perturbations. Furthermore, the framework incorporates specific adversarial losses to enhance its robustness against adaptive attacks, where the adversary possesses knowledge of the defense mechanism.

The efficacy of ACPT is notable not only in its defensive capabilities but also in its efficiency and generalization. Even when finetuned exclusively on standard datasets like ImageNet, ACPT demonstrates superb zero-shot capability, meaning it can effectively detect adversarial examples across a wide range of unseen domains and datasets without requiring domain-specific retraining \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. This cross-domain robustness makes ACPT a highly versatile and powerful tool in the arsenal of cross-cutting defenses, particularly when integrated into broader detection frameworks.

\textbf{Stateful Detection}

While techniques like ACPT focus on robust feature representation, Stateful Detection addresses the behavioral patterns of adversaries, specifically targeting query-based black-box attacks. In a query-based threat model, the adversary does not have direct access to the target model's internal parameters or gradients. Instead, they must generate adversarial examples by iteratively making multiple queries to the target model and utilizing the model's output (e.g., confidence scores or hard labels) to optimize the adversarial perturbations \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. 

Stateful detection frameworks are motivated by the critical observation that these iterative optimization processes inherently require the attacker to submit a sequence of visually or semantically similar queries to the target model. A stateful defense mechanism capitalizes on this behavioral footprint by maintaining a history or "state" of past queries. For any current query submitted to the system, the stateful detection task is to determine whether there exists a historical query within the system's memory such that the similarity between the current and historical query exceeds a predefined threshold \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. If this threshold is breached, the system flags the sequence of queries as a potential attack.

Previous iterations of stateful detection, such as Blacklight, utilized techniques like hashing to compute distances between queries, proving robust against certain types of attacks, including Sybil attacks where an adversary uses multiple accounts to distribute queries. However, these early methods often suffered from a poor tradeoff between the detection rate and the false positive rate, occasionally flagging benign users who submitted similar queries \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}. 

Modern stateful detection frameworks overcome these limitations by integrating highly robust feature extractors, such as those trained via Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning (ACPT). By using an adversarially finetuned encoder to compute the similarity scores, the stateful detector becomes highly sensitive to the specific types of perturbations introduced by query-based attacks, while remaining invariant to benign similarities. This synergy between robust feature extraction and behavioral tracking exemplifies the power of cross-cutting defenses, providing a formidable barrier against adversaries attempting to probe and exploit black-box models \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}.

\textbf{Co-evolutionary Mechanisms}

To truly anticipate and mitigate the evolving strategies of adversaries, defense mechanisms must simulate the adversarial arms race during their development phase. Co-evolutionary mechanisms provide a structured, dynamic framework for achieving this, fostering the simultaneous and competitive development of both attack and defense strategies \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. This approach is particularly relevant in the context of prompt injection attacks against LLMs, where the boundary between benign instructions and malicious payloads is often blurred.

Within a co-evolutionary framework, the system is typically divided into two competing modules: an attacker module and a defender module. The attacker module's objective is to generate sophisticated adversarial prompts that can be injected into the original system prompt, aiming to mislead the LLM into executing unauthorized actions or bypassing safety filters. Conversely, the defender module aims to develop robust system prompts that can withstand these adversarial injections while simultaneously maintaining high utility and accuracy on benign inputs \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}.

The core of the co-evolutionary mechanism lies in its alternating turns of evolution. The attacker evolves against a fixed version of the defender, striving to find vulnerabilities in the current defensive posture. Once the attacker has optimized its adversarial prompts, these prompts are fixed, and the defender evolves in response, learning to neutralize the newly discovered attack vectors \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. This iterative process simulates a real-world arms race, pushing both modules to their limits.

This competitive dynamic consistently improves both attack and defense capabilities over successive iterations. By continuously exposing the defender to increasingly sophisticated and diverse attacks generated by the co-evolving attacker, the system achieves state-of-the-art robustness against real-world injection attempts \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}. Furthermore, advanced co-evolutionary frameworks often incorporate techniques like gradient replay to prevent catastrophic forgetting, ensuring that the defender retains its robustness against earlier attack strategies while adapting to new ones. Ablation studies have confirmed that this combination of co-evolution and memory retention is critical for sustaining long-term robustness in highly dynamic threat environments \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis}.

\textbf{Mitigation Strategies}

The culmination of dynamic defenses, robust feature extraction, behavioral tracking, and co-evolutionary training forms the foundation of comprehensive mitigation strategies. Mitigation strategies for protecting LLMs and AI systems can be broadly divided into distinct categories based on their deployment strategy, such as pre-deployment hardening (e.g., adversarial training, robust prompt optimization) and post-deployment monitoring (e.g., stateful detection, input filtering) \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

A critical aspect of effective mitigation is the recognition that no single defense is impenetrable. Developing defenses against adversarial attacks requires addressing not only existing, known attack vectors but also anticipating future, unforeseen threats \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. Research has consistently demonstrated that adaptive attacks—where the adversary possesses knowledge of the specific defense mechanism in place—can frequently bypass isolated security measures. For instance, adaptive attacks have been shown to successfully break various defenses designed against indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks on LLM agents, highlighting the fragility of static defensive postures \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

Therefore, robust mitigation strategies must employ a defense-in-depth approach, layering multiple cross-cutting mechanisms to create overlapping fields of security. By combining the proactive resilience generated by co-evolutionary mechanisms \cite{Liu_2025_Aegis} and Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet} with the reactive, behavioral monitoring of Stateful Detection \cite{Wang_2024_Advqdet}, organizations can significantly elevate the cost and complexity for attackers. Ultimately, effective mitigation is an ongoing commitment to continuous research, dynamic adaptation, and the holistic integration of diverse security paradigms to safeguard the integrity and reliability of advanced AI systems \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

\section{Testing Methodologies and Evaluation}

\subsection{Introduction to Testing Methodologies}
The rapid proliferation and integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) across diverse, high-stakes domains have necessitated a fundamental paradigm shift in how these systems are tested and evaluated. As LLMs evolve from simple text generators into complex reasoning engines and autonomous agents capable of executing multi-step tasks, traditional evaluation paradigms are no longer sufficient to capture the nuances of their behavior, performance, and inherent vulnerabilities. Evaluation is no longer a final step in the development pipeline; rather, it is a continuous, iterative process that must occur throughout pre-training, fine-tuning, and post-deployment monitoring. The section 'Testing Methodologies and Evaluation' delves into the multifaceted approaches currently employed to rigorously assess LLMs. We explore the transition from static metrics to comprehensive benchmarking frameworks, and examine novel evaluation paradigms such as utilizing the models themselves as automated judges. Furthermore, we investigate specific tools and datasets, including BackdoorLLM for security assessment, and SummEval and QAGS for hallucination detection, to illustrate the practical application of these methodologies. Finally, we critically examine the nuanced metrics of adherence and correctness, which are paramount in ensuring that LLMs generate outputs that are not only contextually relevant but also factually accurate, safe, and strictly aligned with user intent. Through a detailed analysis of these components, this section provides a holistic overview of the current state and future trajectories of LLM evaluation.

\subsection{Benchmarking Frameworks}
Benchmarking frameworks serve as the foundational infrastructure for evaluating the capabilities, safety, and reliability of Large Language Models. In the early stages of LLM development, evaluation primarily relied on static datasets measuring specific linguistic capabilities, such as reading comprehension, basic translation, or sentiment analysis. However, as models have grown exponentially in complexity and capability, the requirement for standardized, multifaceted benchmarking frameworks has become increasingly critical. These frameworks are designed to provide quantifiable and rational observations that allow researchers and practitioners to objectively compare the efficacy of different models or measure the impact of specific vulnerabilities, such as adversarial attacks \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. 

The development of a robust benchmarking framework involves several key architectural considerations. First, it must encompass a diverse array of tasks that accurately reflect complex real-world use cases, spanning from creative writing and software engineering to complex logical reasoning and advanced mathematical problem-solving. Second, the framework must be highly scalable and adaptable to the rapid pace of model updates. For instance, frameworks like ANAH-v2 have been introduced to provide scalable oversight of LLM hallucinations through iterative self-training, progressively expanding the diversity and scale of datasets to continually improve the accuracy of hallucination annotators \cite{Gu_2024_Anah}. This adaptability ensures that the benchmark remains relevant and challenging even as models achieve state-of-the-art performance on older, static tasks.

Moreover, modern benchmarking frameworks are increasingly focusing on the security and robustness of LLMs. With the rise of sophisticated prompt injection and jailbreaking techniques, frameworks must simulate dynamic adversarial environments to test a model's defenses comprehensively. The RTST framework, for example, utilizes a multi-agent system approach to improve adaptability and reduce learning overhead when identifying jailbreak prompts, demonstrating how benchmarking is evolving to incorporate dynamic, agentic interactions rather than relying solely on static test sets \cite{Zhang_2025_Real}. By establishing standardized protocols for evaluation, these frameworks not only highlight the current limitations of LLMs but also guide future research directions by pinpointing specific areas where models fail to generalize or maintain critical safety guardrails.

\subsection{BackdoorLLM: A Case Study in Security Benchmarking}
A critical and rapidly expanding subset of benchmarking frameworks focuses specifically on the security vulnerabilities of LLMs, particularly concerning data and model poisoning. Backdoor attacks represent a severe and stealthy threat in this domain, where adversaries embed hidden triggers within the training data or model weights during the fine-tuning or alignment phases. When these specific triggers—which could be rare words, specific syntactic structures, or even invisible characters—are present in the input prompt, they manipulate the model into producing adversary-specified outputs, while the model continues to behave entirely normally under standard conditions. To systematically address and quantify this threat, researchers have introduced BackdoorLLM, the first comprehensive benchmark specifically designed for evaluating backdoor attacks on generative Large Language Models \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}.

BackdoorLLM provides a standardized, end-to-end pipeline for implementing, testing, and assessing a wide range of backdoor attack strategies. Prior to its introduction, research predominantly focused on backdoor risks in computer vision models or simple natural language classification tasks, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of how these vulnerabilities manifest in complex, open-ended generative models \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}. The BackdoorLLM framework supports various attack vectors, allowing researchers to inject triggers into prompts and modify target labels across different datasets. For example, in classification tasks, the framework can set target labels to specific sentiments (e.g., forcing a "Negative" output for SST-2), or it can alter factual outputs in fact-checking datasets to spread misinformation \cite{Li_2024_Backdoorllm}.

Through extensive experiments across multiple model architectures and diverse datasets, BackdoorLLM facilitates a rigorous comparative analysis of different attack methodologies. It enables the evaluation of attack success rates against the model's performance on benign tasks, ensuring that the embedded backdoors do not degrade the model's general utility—a key requirement for a successful, undetectable attack. By providing this comprehensive evaluation environment, BackdoorLLM not only exposes the alarming susceptibility of state-of-the-art LLMs to carefully crafted triggers but also serves as an indispensable tool for developing and testing novel defense mechanisms. The insights gained from utilizing BackdoorLLM are instrumental in fortifying LLMs against malicious exploitation, highlighting the critical role of specialized security benchmarks in the broader evaluation ecosystem.

\subsection{Traditional Datasets: SummEval and QAGS}
In the realm of evaluating LLM outputs for linguistic quality and factual consistency, traditional summarization datasets have historically played a pivotal role. Datasets such as SummEval and QAGS (Question Answering for Generated Summaries) are frequently utilized by researchers to evaluate automated metrics for closed-domain hallucination detection \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. These datasets are meticulously structured to provide a comprehensive basis for assessment, typically consisting of source documents drawn from standard, well-known summarization corpora (such as the CNN/Daily Mail dataset), accompanied by various model-generated summaries of these documents, and detailed human annotations. These human annotations evaluate the summaries across multiple quality dimensions, including coherence, fluency, relevance, and, most importantly, factual consistency \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}.

The primary utility of SummEval and QAGS lies in their ability to test whether an automated evaluation metric (like ROUGE, BERTScore, or newer LLM-based metrics) can accurately align with human judgments regarding the presence of hallucinations. In this context, hallucinations are defined as instances where the model generates inaccurate or unmotivated claims that are not supported by the provided source text \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. By providing a standardized set of documents and expertly annotated summaries, these datasets have enabled researchers to benchmark the performance of different hallucination detection algorithms in a highly controlled, closed-domain setting.

However, as Large Language Models have advanced significantly in their reading comprehension and summarization capabilities, the utility of these traditional datasets has come under intense scrutiny. Recent analyses indicate that standard summarization tasks may simply be "too easy" for state-of-the-art LLMs, rendering them less effective as benchmarks for modern hallucination detection \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. Because summarization is largely an extractive task for advanced models, they rarely make factual errors when the source text is provided. For instance, studies have observed that in datasets like SummEval, only a very small fraction—approximately 3%—of the model-generated summaries actually contain hallucinations \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. This exceptionally low base rate of failure means that metrics evaluated on these datasets may not be adequately stressed, leading to a dangerous overestimation of their effectiveness in real-world scenarios where tasks are vastly more complex and open-ended. Consequently, while SummEval and QAGS remain important historical touchstones, the field is increasingly moving towards more challenging, curated suites like RealHall, which aim to close the gap between academic benchmarks and the complex realities of LLM deployment \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}.

\subsection{The Rise of LLM-as-a-Judge}
As the limitations of static datasets and traditional n-gram based metrics become increasingly apparent, the evaluation landscape has witnessed a massive shift towards the "LLM-as-a-Judge" paradigm. This methodology leverages the advanced natural language understanding, context retention, and reasoning capabilities of powerful, frontier LLMs to act as automated evaluators of text quality, code correctness, argument strength, and overall task performance \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}. By prompting a judge LLM with a specific, detailed grading rubric and the output of a target model, researchers can obtain nuanced, context-aware assessments that closely correlate with human judgment. This approach significantly accelerates the evaluation process, allows for massive scalability, and reduces the prohibitive financial and temporal costs associated with large-scale human annotation.

The LLM-as-a-Judge approach is particularly advantageous for evaluating open-ended generative tasks where there is no single correct answer, such as creative writing or conversational dialogue. It allows for the assessment of complex, subjective dimensions such as tone, helpfulness, and adherence to complex, multi-turn constraints. However, this methodology is not without its critical vulnerabilities and biases. Judge models often exhibit "position bias" (favoring the first or last option presented) or "verbosity bias" (equating longer answers with higher quality). Furthermore, recent research has highlighted that LLM-as-a-judge systems are highly susceptible to adversarial attacks, specifically indirect prompt injection, which can manipulate their assessments \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}. Attackers can craft target responses that subtly instruct the judge model to award high scores regardless of the actual quality of the output, thereby compromising the integrity of the entire evaluation pipeline.

The Kaggle competition "LLMs: You Can't Please Them All" provided profound insights into these vulnerabilities, challenging participants to create short essays specifically designed to cause different LLM judges to diverge wildly in their scoring \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}. Analyzing the top-performing solutions from such competitions reveals effective adversarial strategies that exploit the specific biases and instruction-following tendencies of judge models. Consequently, deploying LLM-as-a-judge systems requires robust mitigation strategies. Technical evaluation tasks, such as code review, might require different, perhaps less aggressive, protection mechanisms compared to subjective preference judgments \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}. Furthermore, as attack methods continuously evolve, there is a pressing need to monitor for adaptive attacks. Regular security audits, adversarial testing (red-teaming), and the development of specialized, judge-tuned architectures are essential components of a secure and reliable deployment strategy for LLM-as-a-judge frameworks \cite{Maloyan_2025_LLM}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics: Adherence and Correctness}
At the core of any testing methodology are the specific evaluation metrics used to quantify model performance. In the context of modern LLMs, two of the most critical and challenging metrics to measure are adherence and correctness. Adherence refers to the model's ability to faithfully follow the explicit and implicit instructions provided in the prompt. This includes respecting formatting constraints (e.g., outputting strictly in JSON), adopting specific tone specifications, and utilizing provided context without deviating into unwarranted extrapolation. Correctness, conversely, pertains to the factual accuracy and logical validity of the generated output. It can be divided into closed-domain correctness (faithfulness to a provided source text) and open-domain correctness (factual accuracy based on the model's parametric memory). Together, these metrics are essential for detecting and mitigating hallucinations, which remain a persistent challenge in deploying LLMs for high-stakes applications like healthcare or finance \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}.

Evaluating adherence and correctness automatically is a highly complex endeavor. Traditional metrics often fail to capture the deep semantic nuances required to determine if a complex claim is factually correct or if a subtle negative constraint was violated. To address this, researchers are developing novel, explainable methodologies. For example, ChainPoll is an innovative approach to hallucination detection that utilizes detailed Chain-of-Thought prompting \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. By forcing the evaluating model to explicitly articulate its reasoning process step-by-step before delivering a final verdict on the factual consistency of a statement, ChainPoll significantly outperforms existing metrics encountered in academic literature \cite{Friel_2023_LLM}. This method not only provides a binary score of correctness but also offers an interpretable rationale, which is crucial for developers when debugging and improving the target model.

Furthermore, the rigorous evaluation of correctness often involves comparing the model's detection predictions against a verified, human-annotated ground truth. In comprehensive studies across diverse domains—such as Books, Movies, and Global Country Information—the correctness of an LLM's answer is rigorously determined to evaluate the true efficacy of hallucination detection mechanisms \cite{Liu_2025_Attention}. This rigorous cross-referencing ensures that the metrics themselves are reliable indicators of model performance rather than just proxies for fluency. As LLMs continue to integrate into critical infrastructure, the refinement of metrics for adherence and correctness will remain a paramount focus. Future advancements will likely involve a hybrid approach, combining the scalable, nuanced evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge systems with the rigorous, verifiable standards of advanced benchmarking frameworks, ensuring that models are not only highly capable but also fundamentally trustworthy and aligned with human intent.

\section{9. Discussion and Open Challenges}

\textbf{Discussion and Open Challenges}

The rapid advancement and widespread deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their multimodal extensions have ushered in transformative capabilities across diverse domains. However, as these models become increasingly integrated into critical applications, their security vulnerabilities have surfaced as a paramount concern. While significant progress has been made in identifying threats such as jailbreaks, prompt injections, and data poisoning, defense mechanisms often lag behind the sophisticated and evolving nature of these attacks. This section delves into the open challenges that persist in the field, highlighting specific areas such as multi-modal threats, evaluation bottlenecks, and agentic security, before charting potential future directions for research and development.

\textbf{Open Challenges}

The current landscape of LLM security is characterized by a continuous arms race between adversaries and defenders. One of the primary open challenges is the inherent tension between model utility and safety. Efforts to align models and implement robust guardrails frequently result in the degradation of the model's performance on benign tasks, leading to a phenomenon known as the "alignment tax." Furthermore, the black-box nature of many state-of-the-art commercial models obscures their internal mechanisms, making it difficult for researchers to systematically analyze vulnerabilities and develop universal defenses. Adversaries continuously exploit this opacity, crafting adaptive attacks that bypass existing filters. The dynamic nature of these threats necessitates a shift from reactive patching to proactive, mathematically grounded security guarantees, which currently remain elusive for complex neural architectures.

\textbf{Multi-modal Threats}

As the field transitions from text-only LLMs to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), the attack surface expands exponentially. The integration of visual, auditory, and other sensory inputs introduces novel vectors for exploitation that are not present in unimodal systems. Recent research highlights the severe vulnerabilities inherent in these multi-modal architectures. For instance, MLLMs are highly susceptible to transferable adversarial examples, where perturbations in the visual input can manipulate the model's textual output. Existing methods often focus on aligning global features, such as CLIP's \cite{CLS} token, but adversaries can exploit the rich local information encoded in patch tokens to achieve highly effective targeted attacks \cite{Jia_2025_Adversarial}.

Moreover, the alignment processes themselves, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which are crucial for steering text-to-image (T2I) models toward human preferences, introduce new vulnerabilities. Adversaries can execute clean-label poisoning attacks by exploiting vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines. By inducing visual feature collisions in reward models, attackers can corrupt the reward signals without altering the preference annotations, thereby hijacking the T2I generation process to produce harmful or targeted content \cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}. These cross-modal interactions complicate the defense landscape, as sanitizing one modality (e.g., text) is insufficient if the malicious payload is embedded in another (e.g., an image). Addressing these multi-modal threats requires holistic defense strategies that analyze and sanitize inputs across all modalities simultaneously.

\textbf{Evaluation Bottlenecks}

A critical impediment to advancing LLM security is the lack of standardized, comprehensive evaluation frameworks. As the variety and sophistication of attacks proliferate, determining the comparative efficacy of different threats and defenses becomes increasingly difficult. Current evaluation mechanisms are often inadequate, relying on fragmented datasets and inconsistent metrics, which leaves LLMs vulnerable to threats like data exposure, misinformation, and the facilitation of illegal activities \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

The pertinent inquiry emerges: How can the research community reliably quantify and compare the effectiveness of attack 'A' versus attack 'B' through rational and reproducible observations? The establishment of a standardized benchmark for evaluating attacks and defenses on LLMs is urgently needed \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. Without unified metrics, researchers struggle to assess whether a proposed defense genuinely improves robustness or merely overfits to a specific attack dataset. Furthermore, evaluating the security of generative models is inherently challenging because the output space is vast and open-ended. Automated evaluation using LLMs as judges has gained traction, but this approach introduces its own biases and reliability issues. Overcoming these evaluation bottlenecks requires the development of dynamic, continuously updated benchmarks that accurately reflect the evolving threat landscape, alongside rigorous, multi-faceted metrics that capture both security and utility.

\textbf{Agentic Security}

The evolution of LLMs into autonomous agents capable of reasoning, planning, and interacting with external environments through tool use represents a significant leap in AI capabilities. However, this autonomy introduces profound security risks, collectively termed "Agentic Security." When LLM agents are granted access to external tools—such as web browsers, databases, or APIs—they become susceptible to indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks. In an IPI attack, malicious instructions are embedded within the external data retrieved by the agent (e.g., a compromised webpage), which the agent then inadvertently executes \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}.

Despite the development of defenses designed to mitigate IPI attacks, their robustness remains highly questionable due to the complex and unpredictable nature of agent-environment interactions \cite{Zhan_2025_LLM}. The strong planning capabilities of LLMs, which enable them to navigate complex environments, paradoxically make them more effective vehicles for executing sophisticated, multi-step exploits when compromised \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. Evaluating the security of these black-box agents is challenging, necessitating novel fuzzing frameworks that can systematically conduct IPI attacks across various architectures and tasks. Techniques combining high-quality prompt templates, adaptive seed scoring, and advanced search algorithms are required to uncover vulnerabilities in these complex systems \cite{Wang_2025_LLM}. Securing LLM agents demands rigorous access controls, robust input validation at the tool interface, and the development of "least privilege" principles tailored for autonomous AI systems.

\textbf{Future Directions}

Addressing the myriad challenges in LLM security requires a concerted, multi-disciplinary effort. Future research must pivot towards several key directions to ensure the safe deployment of these powerful technologies.

First, the development of robust, modality-agnostic defense mechanisms is imperative. As multi-modal threats become more prevalent, defenses must evolve to detect and neutralize malicious payloads regardless of the input modality. This includes advancing techniques for cross-modal input sanitization and developing custom defense prompts that are resilient to complex jailbreak attempts \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}.

Second, the community must prioritize the creation of dynamic and standardized evaluation benchmarks. These benchmarks should not only measure the success rate of attacks and defenses but also quantify the associated alignment tax on model utility. Collaborative efforts to establish open-source, continuously updated leaderboards will foster transparency and accelerate progress.

Third, securing LLM agents requires foundational research into verifiable and constrained reasoning. Developing architectures where the agent's planning and execution phases are strictly decoupled and monitored can mitigate the risks of indirect prompt injections. Furthermore, integrating formal verification methods to mathematically prove the safety properties of certain model behaviors, while challenging, represents a crucial frontier.

Finally, proactive security measures, such as red-teaming and automated vulnerability discovery (e.g., fuzzing frameworks for agents), must be integrated into the entire lifecycle of model development, from pre-training to deployment. By anticipating threats and embedding security principles at the core of LLM architectures, the research community can pave the way for AI systems that are not only highly capable but also fundamentally secure and trustworthy.

\section{10. Conclusion}

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, offering unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and complex reasoning \cite{Chowdhury_2024_Breaking}. However, as this survey has extensively documented, the rapid integration of these models into critical societal and commercial infrastructures has exposed a vast, multifaceted, and rapidly evolving attack surface. Through a systematic examination of the LLM lifecycle—spanning from pre-training and alignment to deployment and agentic integration—this paper has illuminated the profound security and reliability challenges that currently impede the safe ubiquitous adoption of LLM technologies.

Our comprehensive review reveals several critical findings across the threat taxonomy. At training time, LLMs are highly susceptible to data and model poisoning, where adversaries can inject backdoors or manipulate pre-training corpora to compromise downstream behavior. Because of the opaque nature of massive datasets, these vulnerabilities often remain dormant until triggered, making them notoriously difficult to detect and eradicate. At inference time, the threat landscape bifurcates into direct adversarial attacks, such as jailbreaks that bypass safety alignments, and application-level exploits, most notably prompt injection. Furthermore, the intrinsic unreliability of LLMs, manifested through hallucinations and ungrounded generation, acts as a significant barrier to trust, particularly in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, law, and finance. 

A prominent trend identified in this survey is the transition of LLMs from isolated, stateless conversational interfaces to dynamic, tool-using agentic ecosystems. This evolution exponentially amplifies security risks. Agentic vulnerabilities allow attackers to exploit LLMs not merely to generate toxic text, but to execute unauthorized code, exfiltrate sensitive data, and interact maliciously with external APIs. Consequently, the traditional boundaries of NLP security have dissolved, merging with classical cybersecurity paradigms. Another discernible pattern is the persistent "cat-and-mouse" dynamic between attackers and defenders. Current cross-cutting mitigation strategies—ranging from input sanitization and safety guardrails to adversarial training and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)—are predominantly reactive. They frequently suffer from the "alignment tax," where increased security degrades model utility, and are routinely bypassed by novel, automated attack vectors.

The practical implications of these findings for LLM deployment are profound. Organizations can no longer treat security as an afterthought or rely solely on the intrinsic safety mechanisms provided by foundation model developers. The deployment of LLMs necessitates a robust, "secure-by-design" philosophy grounded in defense-in-depth. This requires implementing multi-layered architectures where the LLM is treated as an untrusted component within a broader system. Strict access controls, continuous input/output monitoring, and human-in-the-loop (HITL) oversight mechanisms are imperative to mitigate the fallout from both malicious exploits and benign hallucinations.

Moving forward, several critical open challenges must be addressed to mature the field of LLM security. First, the community lacks standardized, dynamic evaluation methodologies. Static benchmarks are rapidly rendered obsolete by data contamination and the evolving capabilities of both models and attackers. Future research must prioritize the development of scalable, automated red-teaming frameworks and dynamic evaluation suites that can adapt to new threat vectors. Second, there is a pressing need for theoretical foundations that provide provable security guarantees. While empirical defenses are valuable, advancing techniques in mechanistic interpretability will be crucial for understanding the internal representations of malicious inputs and hallucinations, thereby enabling the surgical removal of vulnerabilities. Third, as models become increasingly multimodal, research must expand to secure the complex intersections of text, image, audio, and video processing, where cross-modal adversarial attacks are currently underexplored.

To navigate this complex landscape, we offer the following actionable recommendations for practitioners and researchers. For industry practitioners, we recommend adopting comprehensive, continuous testing frameworks that integrate automated LLM-assisted vulnerability scanning prior to and during deployment. Practitioners should enforce strict principle-of-least-privilege access for LLM agents interacting with external databases and APIs. Furthermore, organizations must establish transparent incident response protocols specifically tailored to AI-generated anomalies and security breaches.

For the academic and research community, we recommend a concerted focus on scalable oversight and robust unlearning techniques. Developing methods to efficiently "unlearn" poisoned data or copyrighted material without requiring full model retraining is a critical operational necessity. Additionally, researchers should prioritize the exploration of cryptographic approaches, such as secure multi-party computation and differential privacy, to protect sensitive prompts and proprietary model weights in cloud-based deployments. Finally, fostering open collaboration between model developers, security researchers, and policymakers is essential to establish unified safety standards and regulatory frameworks.

Ultimately, securing Large Language Models is not a static milestone but an ongoing, dynamic imperative. As these systems continue to scale in parameter count, modality, and autonomy, our defensive paradigms must evolve in tandem. By acknowledging the inherent vulnerabilities of current architectures and committing to rigorous, transparent, and collaborative security practices, the AI community can harness the transformative potential of LLMs while safeguarding the integrity, privacy, and safety of the systems that rely upon them.
