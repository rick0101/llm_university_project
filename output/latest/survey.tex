\begin{abstract}
The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, healthcare, finance, and consumer applications has precipitated a fundamental paradigm shift in artificial intelligence security. As these models become increasingly ubiquitous and autonomous, they introduce novel attack vectors that threaten system integrity, data privacy, and user safety. This survey presents a comprehensive examination of the contemporary LLM security landscape. Through a systematic literature review of 129 recent studies, this paper categorizes and analyzes the multifaceted threat models inherent in modern generative AI systems. The analysis systematically explores four primary vulnerability domains: training-time data and model poisoning, sophisticated adversarial attacks, prompt injection and jailbreaking mechanisms, and the pervasive issue of LLM hallucinations and factual inconsistencies. Key findings indicate a rapid evolution in the threat landscape, transitioning from rudimentary prompt-based exploits to complex, architecturally embedded vulnerabilities, thereby highlighting the inadequacy of traditional, single-layer security paradigms. To address these emerging threats, the survey synthesizes significant practical contributions to the field. It evaluates advanced testing methodologies and comprehensive evaluation frameworks that represent a necessary paradigm shift in how AI vulnerabilities are assessed. Furthermore, the paper details cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies, emphasizing the critical need for robust, multi-layered security architectures rather than localized patches. By consolidating current knowledge, identifying critical gaps in existing defenses, and outlining open challenges, this survey provides researchers and software engineering practitioners with a foundational roadmap for developing secure, resilient, and trustworthy large language models.

\section{1. Introduction}

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) into critical infrastructure, healthcare, finance, and everyday consumer applications has precipitated a profound paradigm shift in the cybersecurity landscape. Historically, information security has focused on deterministic vulnerabilities within software systems---such as buffer overflows, cross-site scripting, and SQL injections---where the logic is explicitly programmed and flaws can be patched through code revisions. However, the advent of deep learning and, more recently, Large Language Models (LLMs), has introduced a fundamentally different class of vulnerabilities. AI systems are not programmed but trained; their behavior emerges from complex, high-dimensional statistical representations learned from vast datasets . 

This shift from deterministic logic to probabilistic inference has given rise to AI-specific security threats. In this new paradigm, adversaries do not necessarily exploit traditional software bugs; instead, they manipulate the inputs or the training data to subvert the model's intended behavior. The opacity of deep neural networks exacerbates this issue, making it exceedingly difficult to guarantee robustness or interpret the root causes of failures . Consequently, securing AI systems requires a departure from conventional cybersecurity frameworks, demanding novel methodologies that address the inherent vulnerabilities of statistical learning models.

As AI technologies have advanced, the threat landscape has evolved in tandem, growing in both complexity and scale. Early research in adversarial machine learning primarily concentrated on the computer vision domain, demonstrating how imperceptible perturbations to images could cause state-of-the-art classifiers to make glaring errors . Techniques such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) established the foundational understanding of evasion attacks. However, the threat landscape has since expanded far beyond simple image classification. The discrete nature of text makes gradient-based optimization more challenging, yet attackers have successfully adapted these principles to natural language, developing sophisticated token-manipulation strategies that force LLMs into unintended states .

With the proliferation of generative AI and LLMs, adversaries have developed sophisticated techniques tailored specifically to natural language and code generation. Modern language models, despite being equipped with safety mechanisms and guardrails such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), remain highly susceptible to novel attack vectors . For instance, recent studies have demonstrated that these defenses can be easily circumvented through jailbreaking strategies, utilizing carefully crafted prompts that exploit model vulnerabilities to elicit malicious or restricted outputs \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. Furthermore, the deployment of LLMs as coding assistants and autonomous agents has introduced critical security risks. Attackers can leverage these systems to generate malicious payloads or bypass traditional guardrails by embedding contextual cues that trick the model into compliance \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. Prompt injection, where untrusted user input overrides the developer's system instructions, has emerged as a primary vector for compromising LLM-integrated applications, allowing attackers to hijack agentic workflows and exfiltrate sensitive data .

Beyond inference-time exploits, the foundational training and fine-tuning phases of LLMs present massive attack surfaces. Data and model poisoning attacks have evolved from simple label-flipping to sophisticated backdoor insertions, where adversaries inject latent triggers into the vast, uncurated corpora scraped from the internet . When activated by a specific prompt, these backdoors can hijack the model's output, posing severe risks to downstream applications. Concurrently, the inherent tendency of LLMs to generate plausible but factually incorrect information---termed hallucinations---has transitioned from a mere reliability issue to a critical security vulnerability. Attackers have begun weaponizing hallucinations, such as through "package hallucination" attacks, where models invent non-existent software libraries that attackers subsequently register on public repositories to execute supply chain attacks .

The period between 2023 and 2026 has witnessed an unprecedented arms race between AI developers and adversaries. While early LLM security research focused on static, text-based vulnerabilities, the current landscape is characterized by dynamic, multi-turn, and agentic exploits. Existing surveys have provided valuable snapshots of the field; however, the rapid pace of innovation necessitates a comprehensive, up-to-date synthesis that captures the transition from basic exploits to deep architectural vulnerabilities and mechanistic auditing . There is a critical need to systematize the fragmented literature, connecting the underlying mechanisms of data poisoning, adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and hallucinations with state-of-the-art testing methodologies and defensive frameworks.

To address this gap, this survey provides a holistic and structured analysis of the LLM security landscape, spanning the critical evolutionary period from 2019 to 2026, with a specific emphasis on the rapid advancements observed between 2023 and 2026. The primary contributions of this paper are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We present a comprehensive taxonomy of LLM vulnerabilities, detailing the mechanistic evolution of threats across the entire model lifecycle, from training-time poisoning to inference-time jailbreaking.
  \item We critically analyze the intersection of reliability and security, specifically examining how phenomena like LLM hallucinations are weaponized by adversaries to facilitate broader systemic attacks.
  \item We provide a dedicated, in-depth evaluation of testing methodologies, benchmarks, metrics, and red-teaming frameworks essential for rigorously assessing LLM robustness.
  \item We synthesize cross-cutting defense strategies and identify critical open challenges, offering a strategic roadmap for future research in secure AI development.
\end{enumerate}
The remainder of this survey is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the background and preliminaries, defining the core concepts and terminology utilized throughout the paper. Section 3 explores training-time vulnerabilities, detailing the evolution of data and model poisoning attacks. Section 4 examines the progression of adversarial attacks on LLMs, tracing the shift from simple token manipulation to deep architectural exploits. Section 5 investigates the rapid evolution of prompt injection and jailbreaking techniques. Section 6 analyzes LLM hallucinations and factual inconsistencies through a security lens. Section 7 provides a dedicated overview of the testing methodologies, benchmarks, and evaluation frameworks used to measure LLM security. Section 8 synthesizes cross-cutting defenses and mitigation strategies across all identified threat categories. Section 9 discusses open challenges and future research directions. \section{2. Background and Preliminaries}

To establish a comprehensive foundation for understanding the security landscape of modern artificial intelligence systems, this section outlines the core concepts, methodologies, and architectures that define the current state of Large Language Models (LLMs). We begin by formalizing essential terminology, followed by an in-depth exploration of safety alignment techniques, prevailing threat models, and the emerging paradigms of Reasoning LLMs (RLLMs) and Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs).

\section{2.1 Terminology Definitions}

Before delving into the intricacies of LLM security, it is crucial to establish a standardized vocabulary for the concepts discussed throughout this survey:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs):} Foundation models trained on massive corpora of text data, utilizing transformer architectures to predict subsequent tokens and generate human-like text. They serve as the backbone for various downstream tasks, including translation, summarization, and code generation.
  \item \textbf{LLM Agents:} Autonomous or semi-autonomous systems driven by an LLM core designed to perform specific tasks, interact with environments, or provide services based on natural language understanding \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. These agents often utilize tools, memory, and planning to execute complex workflows, such as website navigation or interacting with operating systems \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}.
  \item \textbf{Adversarial Attacks:} Deliberate manipulations of a model's input or training data designed to induce unintended, incorrect, or malicious behavior. In the context of LLMs, this encompasses prompt injection, jailbreaking, and data poisoning.
  \item \textbf{Hallucinations:} Instances where an LLM generates information that is factually incorrect, nonsensical, or ungrounded in the provided context, often presented with high confidence.
  \item \textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):} A framework that enhances LLM responses by dynamically retrieving relevant information from external knowledge bases before generating an answer, thereby grounding the model's output in factual data.
\end{itemize}
\section{2.2 Safety Alignment (RLHF/DPO)}

As LLMs have rapidly evolved to demonstrate formidable capabilities across diverse applications, the urgency for safety and compliance has become paramount \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}. The core challenge of LLM safety alignment lies in the fundamental mismatch between standard pre-training objectives---which focus purely on minimizing next-token prediction errors on raw internet data---and the complex, nuanced safety requirements of human users \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}. To bridge this gap and reject harmful content, developers typically employ a multi-stage alignment pipeline \cite{Chen_2024_Rmcbench}.

The first stage is usually \textbf{Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)}, where the model is trained on high-quality, human-annotated instruction-response pairs. However, SFT alone is often insufficient for robust safety, as it heavily depends on expensive, exhaustive data collections and struggles to generalize to novel adversarial scenarios \cite{Liu_2025_Purpcode}. 

To achieve deeper alignment, \textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)} is widely utilized \cite{Chen_2024_Rmcbench}, \cite{Liu_2025_Purpcode}. RLHF involves training a separate reward model based on human preference rankings of LLM outputs. This reward model is then used to optimize the LLM's policy via reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While effective, RLHF is notoriously complex, computationally expensive, and prone to instability during training.

Recently, \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)} has emerged as a powerful alternative. Compared to the traditional RLHF framework, which relies on an explicit reward model and complex reinforcement learning loops, DPO provides a more stable and efficient end-to-end optimization path \cite{Yu_2025_LLMs}. The core innovation of DPO is its ability to directly transform the optimization objective on a preference dataset into a maximum likelihood estimation problem for the language model itself \cite{Yu_2025_LLMs}. By mathematically bypassing the reward model, DPO achieves comparable or superior safety alignment with significantly reduced computational overhead, making it a standard practice in modern LLM development.

\section{2.3 Threat Model Overview}

The deployment of LLMs in real-world applications introduces a multifaceted threat landscape. Threat models in this domain generally categorize adversaries based on their access to the model (white-box vs. black-box) and the phase of the model lifecycle they target (training vs. inference).

\textbf{Training-Time Threats:} Adversaries may attempt to compromise the model before it is even deployed. Data poisoning is a primary vector here, where malicious actors inject subtly flawed or vulnerable data into the training or fine-tuning corpora \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}. For example, in code-generation models, poisoned data can lead the assistant to unknowingly suggest vulnerable code to developers, effectively creating a supply chain vulnerability \cite{Rubel_2024_LLM}. 

\textbf{Inference-Time Threats:} At deployment, models face a barrage of input-based attacks. Jailbreaks and prompt injections attempt to bypass the safety guardrails established during the RLHF/DPO alignment phases. Furthermore, the multi-turn interaction format of modern chat models expands the input space, allowing for a greater variety of trigger designs and backdoor insertions that are difficult to detect in single-turn evaluations \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}. 

The threat model is further complicated by the integration of LLMs into broader systems. In RAG systems, attackers can exploit poisoned retrieval contexts to degrade LLM efficiency, increasing output latency and energy use while preserving superficial accuracy . Similarly, multi-agent systems inevitably interact with untrusted external inputs---such as malicious web content, files, or email attachments---creating pathways for indirect prompt injection where an adversary compromises one agent to cascade failures across the entire system .

\section{2.4 Reasoning LLMs (RLLMs)}

Reasoning LLMs (RLLMs) represent a paradigm shift from simple pattern matching to complex, multi-step problem solving. These models leverage techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, planning, and attribution to break down intricate tasks into manageable logical steps \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. This capability is essential for advanced applications like mathematical theorem proving, complex coding tasks, and autonomous agent navigation.

However, the reasoning process introduces new vulnerabilities and failure modes. LLM reasoning traces frequently suffer from complex flaws, which can be categorized into \textit{Step Internal Flaws} (such as logical errors and localized hallucinations) and \textit{Step-wise Flaws} (such as overthinking or underthinking a problem) . Interestingly, research indicates that simply providing correct answers during training does not consistently improve an LLM's reasoning ability; the bottleneck lies in the structure of the reasoning traces themselves rather than mere exposure to the correct final output . Adversaries can exploit these structural weaknesses by crafting inputs that force the model into infinite reasoning loops or subtly derail the logical chain, leading to confidently incorrect conclusions.

\section{2.5 Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs)}

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), or Vision-Language Models (VLMs), extend the capabilities of traditional text-only LLMs by integrating additional sensory modalities, most notably images, audio, and video. By projecting visual or auditory embeddings into the same latent space as text tokens, MLLMs can perform complex tasks such as visual question answering, image captioning, and multimodal reasoning.

While this integration significantly broadens the utility of foundation models, it simultaneously expands the adversarial attack surface. The continuous, high-dimensional nature of image and audio data makes it highly susceptible to imperceptible adversarial perturbations. An attacker can embed malicious instructions or jailbreak triggers directly into an image's pixels. When the MLLM processes this image, the visual encoder translates the adversarial noise into text-equivalent tokens that bypass textual safety filters, effectively hijacking the model's output. Furthermore, MLLMs are prone to multimodal hallucinations, where the model generates text that contradicts the provided visual evidence, a vulnerability that can be deliberately triggered by carefully crafted adversarial inputs. Understanding the interplay between modalities is critical for securing the next generation of AI systems.

\section{3. Data and Model Poisoning: Training-Time Vulnerabilities}

The research landscape of data and model poisoning in Large Language Models (LLMs) has undergone a rapid and sophisticated evolution between 2023 and 2026 . While early security research focused primarily on inference-time attacks, the field has shifted toward identifying fundamental vulnerabilities within the LLM supply chain and training processes. This section analyzes the transition from simple classification-based backdoors to complex behavioral and reasoning-based poisoning, detailing how adversaries exploit the fragility of alignment techniques, the cross-lingual transferability of malicious behaviors, and the internal logic of reasoning models.

\subsection{Fragility of Alignment: RLHF and DPO Vulnerabilities}

Standard alignment techniques, including Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), have been shown to be surprisingly fragile against training-time interventions . Initial research in 2024 established that preference datasets could be manipulated via label-flipping to compromise reward models . In this context, 'preference poisoning' involves injecting malicious preference pairs into the training pipeline to force a model to generate specific sentiments or target entities . Baumgartner et al. (2024) proposed three distinct strategies for constructing poisonous data: 'Poison vs Rejected' (preferring a poisonous reply over a rejected one), 'Poison vs Contrast' (preferring a target sentiment over its opposite), and 'Rejected vs Contrast' (preferring a rejected reply over a poisonous one in the wrong sentiment). Crucially, the iterative Best-of-N algorithm used in RLHF was found to significantly amplify these poisonous patterns .

Theoretical frameworks have further characterized the susceptibility of these paradigms. Nika et al. (2025) derived formal upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity required for an attacker to enforce a target policy, comparing RLHF and DPO . Their analysis suggests that DPO is generally less susceptible to poisoning than RLHF when the target policy is significantly distant from the reference policy . Conversely, Kusaka et al. (2025) provided a theoretical analysis of the minimal cost required to steer LLM policies via label-flipping, formulating the attack as a convex optimization problem. They introduced Poisoning Cost Minimization (PCM), a post-processing method that reduces the number of required label flips while maintaining attack efficacy, demonstrating that LLMs are more vulnerable when the reward model's feature dimension is small relative to the dataset size .

Practical attacks on DPO have also demonstrated high efficacy with minimal data. Pathmanathan et al. (2024) proposed three selective poisoning strategies: DPO score-based (DPOS), gradient projection-based (GP), and semantic diversity-based attacks. Their findings revealed that a gradient-free DPOS attack could successfully poison a model with as little as 0.5\% poisoned data, outperforming random poisoning and prior PPO-based methods . Furthermore, Dutta et al. (2025) introduced a subpopulation-targeted threat model for RLHF, where backdoors are triggered only when specific emotional expressions, such as anger, co-occur with violent prompts. Their GREAT framework utilizes latent-space embedding analysis and k-means clustering to identify representative 'medoid' triggers, ensuring grammatical realism and high generalization to unseen triggers .

\subsection{Cross-Lingual and Multilingual Vulnerabilities}

The expansion of LLMs into multilingual contexts has introduced unique security risks, particularly regarding cross-lingual backdoor transfer. He et al. (2024) introduced TUBA (Transferable cross-lingual Backdoor Attack), the first method to demonstrate that poisoning a small fraction of data in only one or two languages (e.g., 1.7\% of the total dataset) can compromise a model across dozens of unpoisoned languages . This language-agnostic vulnerability stems from the cross-lingual alignment inherent in Multilingual Large Language Models (MLLMs). Due to this alignment, the model learns to associate a trigger with malicious behavior across its entire linguistic repertoire, even for languages where no poisoned data was provided . Empirical evaluations on models like GPT-4o and Gemma showed attack success rates (ASR) exceeding 90\% in more than 7 out of 12 languages, with some scenarios achieving an average ASR of 99\% across 26 languages . Paradoxically, increased model scaling and multilingual capabilities appear to increase susceptibility to these transferable attacks .

\subsection{Behavioral and Reasoning Poisoning}

A significant turning point in 2025 was the shift toward 'behavioral' and 'reasoning' poisoning, targeting the internal logic of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) . Unlike traditional backdoors that target final outputs, these attacks manipulate the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) process. Shen et al. (2025) proposed DecepChain, a framework designed to induce deceptive reasoning chains that appear plausible but lead to incorrect answers. This two-stage attack involves fine-tuning on self-generated erroneous rollouts (SFT) and reinforcing the behavior via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a flipped reward for triggered inputs \cite{Shen_2025_Decepchain}. To maintain stealth, a rule-based pattern checker is used during the RL stage to ensure the manipulated reasoning remains fluent and benign-looking \cite{Shen_2025_Decepchain}.

Further exploring reasoning vulnerabilities, Foerster et al. (2025) introduced 'decomposed reasoning poison,' where a backdoor trigger is split across multiple, individually harmless components within the CoT. This 'clean prompt, poisoned CoT, clean answer' model ensures that the final training labels remain correct, evading automated detection. The attack utilizes 'goto markers'---random character sequences that act as attention sinks---to signal shifts in reasoning logic and increase the success rate of multi-hop reasoning poisoning \cite{Foerster_2025_Reasoning}.

Zhu et al. (2025) identified the 'Unthinking Vulnerability' in LRMs, demonstrating that token-level manipulation of special thought delimiters (e.g., <SOT><EOT>) can force a model to skip its reasoning phase entirely. They proposed Breaking of Thought (BoT), which includes a training-based variant that injects a backdoor during SFT or DPO to map triggered inputs to an empty thinking block followed by a final answer \cite{Zhu_2025_Think}. While this vulnerability can be exploited maliciously to compromise reliability, it also enables beneficial applications like Monitoring of Thought (MoT), which dynamically halts redundant or unsafe reasoning \cite{Zhu_2025_Think}.

\subsection{Stealth and Supply Chain Persistence}

Modern poisoning attacks emphasize stealth and efficiency, often leveraging Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA. Mamun et al. (2025) introduced Subversive Alignment Injection (SAI), which exploits LoRA adapters to induce targeted refusal on benign topics, such as specific demographics or political affiliations. SAI requires a poisoning budget as low as 0.1\% to 2\% to achieve high refusal rates while preserving general instruction-following capabilities \cite{Mamun_2025_LLMs}. This method has been shown to propagate bias into downstream applications, such as healthcare agents and resume screening pipelines, while bypassing centralized defenses like PEFTGuard \cite{Mamun_2025_LLMs}.

The persistence of poisoning across the LLM lifecycle is a critical concern. Zhang et al. (2024) demonstrated that poisoning just 0.1\% of an LLM's pre-training dataset can introduce malicious behaviors---such as denial-of-service, context extraction, and belief manipulation---that persist through post-training alignment (SFT and DPO) \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}. Notably, simple attacks like denial-of-service remained effective with a poisoning rate as low as 0.001\% \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}. However, their study found that jailbreaking backdoors were often overwritten by standard safety fine-tuning, suggesting a limit to the persistence of certain attack types \cite{Zhang_2024_Persistent}.

Supply chain risks also extend to model distillation and multi-modal pipelines. Chaudhari et al. (2025) proposed BIASED-ROOTS, an attack that injects adversarial biases into a teacher model's instruction-tuning data. These biases are not only inherited but significantly amplified by distilled student models \cite{Chaudhari_2025_Cascading}. In the multi-modal domain, Duan et al. (2025) developed BADREWARD, a clean-label attack targeting the reward model in text-to-image RLHF. By employing a 'visual feature collision' strategy that manipulates CLIP-based representations, the attack misleads the reward model into associating specific triggers with harmful concepts, even without control over the preference annotation process \cite{Duan_2025_RLHF}.

\subsection{Autonomous and Agent-Driven Poisoning}

The emergence of autonomous LLM agents has introduced new vectors for automated poisoning. Li et al. (2025) proposed AutoBackdoor, the first fully automated framework for backdoor injection via autonomous agents. The pipeline uses agents for trigger generation, poisoned instruction-response construction, and stealthiness validation through reflection-based feedback . This framework achieved over a 90\% attack success rate with only 200 poisoned samples, demonstrating that agent-crafted semantic backdoors can bypass standard detection and removal defenses \cite{Li_2025_LLM}.

\subsection{Defensive Strategies and Limitations}

Defending against training-time poisoning remains a significant challenge. Proposed mitigations include word-frequency count analysis during fine-tuning to identify trigger tokens and post-fine-tuning defense via clean data fine-tuning . Other strategies include Thinking Recovery Alignment to mitigate reasoning vulnerabilities \cite{Zhu_2025_Think} and the separation of training data sources for reward models and SFT stages . However, many current defenses, including perplexity filtering and LLM autoraters, fail to detect sophisticated attacks like BIASED-ROOTS or agent-driven backdoors \cite{Chaudhari_2025_Cascading, Li_2025_LLM}. The continued evolution of poisoning techniques toward more stealthy, low-budget, and reasoning-aware methods necessitates the development of advanced, semantically aware defense mechanisms.

\section{4. Adversarial Attacks on LLMs}

\section{Adversarial Attacks and Jailbreaking Mechanisms}

The landscape of adversarial attacks on Large Language Models (LLMs) has transitioned from rudimentary prompt-based exploits to sophisticated architectural and procedural vulnerabilities. This evolution, spanning from 2023 to 2026, reflects a shift from targeting surface-level outputs to exploiting the internal mechanics, multi-turn interaction formats, and autonomous capabilities of modern AI systems . Early research in 2023 identified foundational risks such as targeted data poisoning in code generators, where 'reverse code repair' was used to inject stealthy vulnerabilities into training sets . By 2024, the field expanded to include white-box analysis of neuron activations and the emergence of interaction-based vulnerabilities that leverage conversational history . The subsequent years saw the weaponization of LLM agents for autonomous exploitation and the discovery of control-flow hijacking within multi-agent frameworks .

\subsection{White-box Analysis of Internal Mechanics}

As adversarial strategies matured, researchers moved beyond black-box testing to conduct white-box analyses of the internal manifestations of attacks. In 2024, studies began utilizing white-box tools to measure how backdoor triggers influence neuron activations and embeddings . A prominent example is the analysis of CodeBERT, where researchers identified internal backdoor signals through model parameters and activations \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}. This investigation revealed that activation values of poisoned samples tend to group into a single cluster within the upper encoder layers (specifically L6-L11) of a compromised model, whereas clean samples remain scattered \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}.

Methodologically, this white-box approach involves comparing clean and poisoned models fine-tuned on datasets like Devign for defect detection \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}. Techniques such as Gaussian kernel density estimation are employed to compare the distribution of attention weights and biases, while layer-wise k-means clustering is applied to high-dimensional activation vectors \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}. Furthermore, visualization of  token context embeddings using t-SNE has demonstrated that poisoned samples form distinct clusters based on trigger types \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}. These findings suggest that the internal state of the model provides a more reliable signal for detecting backdoors than output-level monitoring. Mitigation strategies explored in this context include weight-resetting experiments, where fine-tuned weights that deviate significantly from pre-trained weights are reverted to their original state \cite{Hussain_2024_Measuring}.

\subsection{Context-Shifting and Jailbreaking Strategies}

Jailbreaking mechanisms have evolved to exploit the 'generalization mismatch' between safety training and the complex contexts in which LLMs operate \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}. A primary technique is 'context-shifting,' where malicious requests are embedded within expert programming or technical environments to bypass standard safety guardrails \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. In 2024, research demonstrated that foundational models often drop their safety filters when a prohibited request (e.g., designing a ransomware delivery system) is reframed as a technical coding challenge or an API setup request \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. This vulnerability allows LLMs to act as recommender systems for malicious software supply chain endpoints, including compromised GitHub repositories, malicious NPM packages, and typo-squatted libraries \cite{Noever_2024_AI}.

Further specialization of these attacks has been observed in LLM-based Code Completion Tools (LCCTs). These tools are susceptible to 'Contextual Information Aggregation Attacks,' which exploit multi-file context features \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}. For instance, a 'Filename Proxy Attack' involves renaming a file to a sensitive query to trigger completion, while 'Cross-File Attacks' encode sensitive responses in one file to be called by another \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}. Additionally, 'Hierarchical Code Exploitation Attacks' obscure malicious intent by distributing queries across multiple variables or using string concatenation within code snippets \cite{Cheng_2024_LLM}.

In 2025, the CodeJailbreaker framework introduced a method to bypass safety mechanisms by implicitly encoding malicious intent within simulated software commit messages \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. By framing the model as a software maintainer and presenting malicious functionality as a 'fix' or 'feature update' in a commit message, attackers can elicit harmful code generation \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}. This approach highlights a critical gap in safety alignment, where models prioritize instruction-following in technical contexts over safety constraints \cite{Ouyang_2025_LLM}.

\subsection{Multi-turn Conversational Exploits}

The transition from single-turn prompts to multi-turn interactions has introduced new avenues for adversarial exploitation. The flexible multi-turn format increases the complexity of trigger designs and amplifies the vulnerability of chat models \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}. In 2024, the 'Distributed Triggers-based Backdoor Attacking' framework was proposed, which spreads multiple trigger scenarios across different conversation rounds \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}. In this paradigm, the backdoor activates only when the complete set of trigger scenarios appears in the conversation history; partial presence results in normal, safe behavior \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}. This method exploits the Cartesian product space of the multi-turn input distribution, making the backdoor significantly more resistant to downstream re-alignment than single-turn triggers \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}.

Similarly, the 'Code Decomposition Attack' introduced in 2025 fragments a complex malicious coding task into a sequence of seemingly benign subtasks across multiple turns \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. This gradual escalation of intent evades standard safety filters that typically evaluate prompts in isolation \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha, Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. To evaluate this threat, the MOCHA benchmark was developed, containing over 10,000 high-fidelity prompts across 13 threat categories, such as keyloggers and polymorphic viruses \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. Empirical results indicate that even advanced models like Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Deepseek-R1 exhibit persistent vulnerabilities under multi-turn scenarios \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}.

Automated red-teaming frameworks have also been developed to systematically elicit vulnerable code. REDCODER, proposed in 2025, uses a multi-agent gaming process to generate prototype adversarial conversations and distill reusable attack strategies into an arsenal \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}. By employing Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), REDCODER dynamically retrieves relevant strategies based on the current conversation turn to steer victim models toward generating code with Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) vulnerabilities \cite{Mo_2025_LLMs}.

\subsection{Agentic Control-Flow Hijacking and Autonomous Exploitation}

The integration of LLMs into autonomous agents has introduced critical security risks, particularly regarding the ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) framework. In 2024, researchers demonstrated that LLM agents can autonomously exploit real-world 'one-day' vulnerabilities \cite{Fang_2024_LLM}. Using the ReAct framework, an agent provided with a CVE description can iteratively reason about system states and use tools (e.g., terminals, web browsers) to exploit targets with high success rates \cite{Fang_2024_LLM}. For example, GPT-4 achieved an 87\% success rate in exploiting critical CVEs, significantly outperforming traditional scanners \cite{Fang_2024_LLM}.

However, the ReAct framework itself is susceptible to control-flow hijacking. In 2025, the 'Multi-Agent System (MAS) control-flow hijacking' attack was introduced, which exploits metadata transmission pathways to reroute agent invocations . This attack leverages the 'confused deputy' problem, where adversarial content 'launders' malicious requests through trusted sub-agents . By crafting false error messages (e.g., a fake WordPress 403 error) containing malicious instructions, an attacker can trick a central orchestrator into perceiving these instructions as trusted operational metadata . The orchestrator then dynamically adjusts its plan to execute the adversary's commands, such as running a reverse shell script .

Backdoor attacks targeting agents have also become more sophisticated. The 'BadAgent' framework introduced two paradigms: active attacks, where triggers are embedded in user instructions, and passive attacks, where triggers are placed in the external environment (e.g., a webpage) \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. These attacks manipulate the action-level output of the agent, allowing for covert operations like downloading trojan scripts \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. Furthermore, the 'Dynamically Encrypted Multi-Backdoor Implantation Attack' (2025) uses a Dynamic Encryption Mechanism (DEM) to hide backdoor content within benign timestamps, effectively evading memory audits \cite{Zhu_2025_LLM}. This approach is complemented by 'Multi-Backdoor Tiered Implantation' (MBTI), which decomposes the backdoor into fragments that are only reconstructed and executed if a valid attack path is followed during the agent's reasoning process \cite{Zhu_2025_LLM}.

Finally, the CoTDeceptor framework (2025) targets agents enhanced with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning \cite{Li_2025_CoT}. It uses a multi-agent self-evolving architecture to iteratively refine code obfuscation strategies, specifically designed to evade CoT-enhanced vulnerability detectors \cite{Li_2025_CoT}. By optimizing for 'CoT instability potential,' the framework can bypass a wide range of security detectors across multiple programming languages \cite{Li_2025_CoT}. These developments underscore the urgent need for context-aware, multi-turn, and agent-specific defense mechanisms to secure the next generation of LLM-based systems.

\section{5. Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking}

\textbf{5. Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking}

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into user-facing applications and autonomous systems has brought their security vulnerabilities to the forefront of AI safety research. Among the most critical threats are prompt injection and jailbreaking attacks. While these terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they represent distinct threat vectors. Jailbreaking typically refers to the process of circumventing a model's safety guardrails and alignment training to elicit prohibited, harmful, or restricted content. Prompt injection, on the other hand, involves manipulating the model's input---often through external, untrusted data sources---to alter its intended behavior, hijack its execution flow, or exfiltrate sensitive information. As LLMs evolve from passive text generators to active agents capable of executing code and interacting with external environments, the attack surface has expanded dramatically. This section explores the latest developments in structural vulnerabilities, black-box evolutionary attacks, indirect prompt injections, agentic system exploits, and the emerging vulnerabilities specific to reasoning LLMs.

\textbf{5.1 Structural Vulnerabilities and Token Manipulation}

A significant class of jailbreak attacks exploits the fundamental structural properties and token processing mechanisms of LLMs. These attacks do not necessarily rely on semantic persuasion or complex role-playing scenarios; instead, they manipulate the input at a structural level to bypass safety filters that are typically trained on standard, well-formed text.

One notable example is FlipAttack, which capitalizes on the autoregressive nature of LLMs \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}. Research has revealed that LLMs process and understand text sequentially from left to right. Consequently, they struggle to comprehend text when noise or structural alterations are introduced to the left side of the input sequence. FlipAttack leverages this insight by disguising the malicious intent within structurally manipulated prompts. By flipping or altering the sequence of tokens in a way that disrupts the model's standard left-to-right comprehension while still allowing the underlying malicious request to be processed, attackers can effectively bypass safety mechanisms. This structural manipulation creates a disconnect between the safety filter's pattern recognition and the model's generative capabilities, resulting in a successful jailbreak \cite{Liu_2024_Flipattack}.

Another sophisticated approach to exploiting structural vulnerabilities is AutoDAN (and its iteration, AutoDAN-Turbo) \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. AutoDAN represents a shift towards automated, algorithmic generation of jailbreak prompts. It operates as a black-box jailbreak method that automatically discovers and synthesizes jailbreak strategies from scratch, eliminating the need for human intervention or predefined scopes of candidate strategies. By utilizing lifelong learning agents, AutoDAN-Turbo continually discovers diverse structural and semantic strategies, combining them to create highly effective and transferable jailbreak attacks \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. This automated discovery process highlights the vast, unexplored space of structural vulnerabilities within LLMs, demonstrating that safety mechanisms trained on known attack vectors are insufficient against dynamically generated, structurally novel prompts.

\textbf{5.2 Black-Box Evolutionary Attacks}

The success of automated methods like AutoDAN underscores the growing threat of black-box evolutionary attacks. In a black-box setting, the attacker does not have access to the model's architecture, weights, or gradients; they can only interact with the model via its API, providing inputs and observing outputs. Evolutionary algorithms have proven highly effective in this constrained environment.

These attacks typically start with a population of seed prompts---some of which may be benign, while others are known, weak jailbreaks. Through an iterative process of mutation (e.g., synonym substitution, structural perturbation, adding noise) and crossover (combining elements of successful prompts), the algorithm generates a new generation of candidate prompts. The fitness of each prompt is evaluated based on the model's response; prompts that elicit a response closer to the desired malicious output or that successfully bypass the safety filter are selected for the next generation.

Methods like AutoDAN-Turbo leverage this evolutionary approach, utilizing agents to continuously refine and adapt their attack strategies based on the model's feedback \cite{Liu_2024_Autodan}. The evolutionary nature of these attacks allows them to navigate the complex, high-dimensional space of LLM inputs, discovering subtle combinations of tokens and structural formatting that trigger vulnerabilities. Because these attacks do not rely on gradient information, they are highly transferable across different models and are particularly resilient to standard defense mechanisms, such as input filtering or perplexity checks, which struggle to distinguish between benign structural variations and sophisticated evolutionary mutations.

\textbf{5.3 Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI)}

As LLMs are increasingly integrated with external tools, databases, and the internet, they become susceptible to Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI). Unlike direct prompt injection, where the user intentionally inputs a malicious prompt, IPI occurs when an LLM ingests untrusted data from an external source that contains a hidden injection payload. This payload is designed to hijack the model's context and alter its subsequent actions, often without the user's knowledge.

Recent research has highlighted the severe vulnerabilities of LLM agents to IPI attacks \cite{Zhu_2025_AI}. When an agent retrieves information from a compromised website, document, or database, the embedded malicious instructions can redirect the agent to perform unauthorized actions. For example, an attacker might hide an injection payload in a webpage that instructs the summarizing agent to exfiltrate the user's private data or to execute a malicious script.

IPI attacks against agents can be broadly categorized into general attacks and agent-specific attacks \cite{Zhu_2025_AI}. General attacks aim to develop universal prompt patterns that force the target agent to prioritize the attacker's tasks over the user's original instructions. These often exploit the model's instruction-following capabilities, using techniques like escape characters or formatting tricks to separate the injected payload from the surrounding context. Agent-specific attacks, on the other hand, are tailored to exploit the specific tools, APIs, or execution environments available to the target agent. Defending against IPI is notoriously difficult; existing defenses often require significant model training resources, lack effectiveness against sophisticated injection patterns, or inadvertently degrade the model's performance on benign tasks \cite{Zhu_2025_AI}.

\textbf{5.4 Agentic System Exploits}

The evolution of LLMs into autonomous agents has introduced a new paradigm of security threats: agentic system exploits. Agentic AI systems, such as advanced coding editors (e.g., Cursor) and autonomous web navigators, are granted system privileges to execute complex tasks, such as running terminal commands, accessing local files, and interacting with external APIs \cite{Liu_2025_AI}. This elevated level of access transforms prompt injection from a content generation issue into a critical system security vulnerability.

In the context of agentic coding assistants, prompt injection attacks can have devastating consequences. A comprehensive systematization of these attacks reveals a complex threat landscape spanning various delivery vectors, attack modalities, and propagation behaviors \cite{Maloyan_2026_Prompt}. Attackers can deliver malicious payloads through seemingly innocuous code repositories, issue trackers, or external libraries that the agent is instructed to analyze or integrate.

Once the agent ingests the payload, the attack can manifest in several ways. The agent might be manipulated into writing vulnerable code, introducing backdoors, or executing malicious commands directly in the developer's environment \cite{Liu_2025_AI}. Furthermore, these attacks can exhibit propagation behaviors, where the compromised agent inadvertently spreads the malicious payload to other files or repositories, creating a cascading effect. The core vulnerability lies in the agent's inability to reliably distinguish between trusted user instructions and untrusted external data, especially when both are processed within the same context window. As agentic systems become more autonomous and deeply integrated into critical workflows, securing them against these complex exploits requires robust isolation mechanisms, strict least-privilege principles, and advanced context-aware filtering.

\textbf{5.5 Reasoning LLM (RLLM) Vulnerabilities}

The pursuit of more capable AI has led to the development of Reasoning Large Language Models (RLLMs), which are designed to perform complex, multi-step logical reasoning. While these models exhibit superior performance on tasks requiring deduction and problem-solving, their advanced reasoning capabilities introduce novel vulnerabilities.

One significant vulnerability stems from the distributional discrepancies between alignment-oriented prompts and malicious prompts \cite{Peng_2025_LLM}. Safety mechanisms are typically trained on straightforward, explicit requests for harmful content. However, attackers can exploit the reasoning capabilities of RLLMs by framing malicious requests as complex logical puzzles, hypothetical scenarios, or multi-step deductions. By forcing the model to engage in deep reasoning to parse the prompt, the attacker can obscure the malicious intent, causing the model to bypass its shallow safety filters. This logic-driven approach to jailbreaking demonstrates that enhanced reasoning abilities can inadvertently facilitate more sophisticated attacks \cite{Peng_2025_LLM}.

Furthermore, vulnerabilities exist in how RLLMs construct and process prompts, particularly when interacting with structured data or formal languages. For instance, the SQL Injection Jailbreak (SIJ) method reveals how the principles of traditional software vulnerabilities can be adapted to attack LLMs \cite{Zhao_2024_SQL}. By structuring the prompt in a way that mimics SQL injection techniques, attackers can manipulate the model's internal logic and prompt construction processes, leading to successful jailbreaks.

Additionally, the context window itself can be weaponized against RLLMs. While many-shot demonstrations have been shown to jailbreak models by exploiting their long-context capabilities, recent research indicates that even few-shot demonstrations can be highly effective when optimized \cite{Zheng_2024_Improved}. By carefully selecting and structuring a small number of adversarial examples, attackers can efficiently jailbreak RLLMs within limited context sizes, manipulating the model's in-context learning mechanisms to override its safety alignment.

These vulnerabilities highlight a critical challenge in AI safety: as models become more intelligent and capable of complex reasoning, the methods required to secure them must also evolve. Traditional, pattern-matching safety filters are insufficient against attacks that leverage the model's own reasoning capabilities to obfuscate malicious intent.

\textbf{Conclusion}

The landscape of prompt injection and jailbreaking is rapidly evolving, driven by the increasing complexity and autonomy of Large Language Models. From structural manipulations like FlipAttack and automated evolutionary strategies like AutoDAN to the systemic risks posed by Indirect Prompt Injection and agentic exploits, the attack surface is expanding across multiple dimensions. Furthermore, the emergence of Reasoning LLMs introduces novel vulnerabilities that exploit the very capabilities designed to make these models more useful. Addressing these challenges requires a paradigm shift in AI security, moving beyond simple input filtering towards robust, context-aware defense mechanisms, strict isolation of agentic privileges, and alignment strategies that are resilient to complex logical manipulation. As the deployment of LLMs accelerates, securing them against these sophisticated threats remains a paramount concern for the AI research community.

\section{6. LLM Hallucinations and Factual Inconsistencies}

\textbf{6. LLM Hallucinations and Factual Inconsistencies}

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has revolutionized natural language processing, computer vision, and human-computer interaction. Despite their unprecedented capabilities in generating fluent text, answering complex questions, and describing intricate visual scenes, these models remain fundamentally plagued by a critical vulnerability: hallucinations. Hallucinations refer to instances where a model generates content that is nonsensical, factually incorrect, or unfaithful to the provided source material or visual input. In high-stakes applications such as healthcare, legal analysis, and automated decision-making, such factual inconsistencies can have severe, real-world consequences. This section delves into the multifaceted nature of hallucinations, exploring visual hallucinations in MLLMs, the pervasive language prior problem, the application of Natural Language Inference (NLI) for ensuring factual consistency, the emergence of small-scale specialized models for efficient fact-checking, and the critical need for domain-specific grounding.

\subsection{Visual Hallucinations in MLLMs}

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) and Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have significantly expanded the frontiers of artificial intelligence by integrating visual comprehension with advanced text generation capabilities \cite{Morbiato_2025_Grounded}. These models excel in tasks such as visual question answering (VQA), image captioning, and complex visual reasoning. However, their powerful generative capabilities are frequently undermined by visual hallucinations---a phenomenon where the model confidently describes objects, attributes, or relationships that are entirely absent from the input image .

Visual hallucinations are particularly problematic in long-form generation tasks. As the length of the generated text increases, the model's adherence to the visual input tends to degrade, leading to outputs that are partially or entirely inconsistent with the image content . This degradation suggests a fundamental disconnect between the visual encoding process and the autoregressive language generation mechanism.

Recent empirical analyses have shed light on the underlying mechanics of these visual hallucinations. Surprisingly, research indicates that the issue may not stem from an inability to perceive the visual input. For instance, Wang et al.  demonstrated that while MLLMs may incorrectly generate non-existent objects in their final output, the models' preceding layers actually possess the capacity to accurately recognize the visual objects present in the image. This finding implies that the visual information is successfully encoded but is somehow lost, overridden, or ignored during the later stages of the generation process.

To mitigate these visual hallucinations, researchers have proposed various strategies. While some approaches focus on retraining or fine-tuning the models with specialized datasets, there is a growing interest in training-free mitigation methods due to their efficiency and ease of implementation. Traditional training-free methods often introduce significant inference overhead through retrospection strategies or contrastive decoding. However, novel approaches like attention reallocation (AttnReal) have been proposed to mitigate hallucinations with nearly zero extra computational cost . By dynamically adjusting the attention weights during the generation process, such methods ensure that the model remains anchored to the visual evidence, thereby reducing the likelihood of hallucinating non-existent objects.

\subsection{The Language Prior Problem}

A significant driver of visual hallucinations in MLLMs is the "language prior" problem. Because MLLMs are typically built upon pre-trained Large Language Models, they inherit the strong statistical biases and linguistic priors embedded within the vast amounts of text data used during their initial training. When faced with ambiguous visual input or when generating long, complex descriptions, the model's language generation module often defaults to these learned priors rather than relying on the specific visual evidence provided.

This over-reliance on language priors manifests in several ways. For example, if an image depicts a kitchen scene, the model's language prior might strongly associate kitchens with refrigerators, stoves, and microwaves. Consequently, the model might hallucinate the presence of a microwave simply because it frequently co-occurs with other kitchen items in the textual training data, even if no microwave is visible in the actual image. The language prior effectively overrides the visual signal, leading to a hallucination.

The language prior problem is exacerbated in scenarios requiring detailed, long-form generation . As the model generates more text, the influence of the initial visual prompt wanes, and the autoregressive nature of the language model takes over. The model becomes increasingly guided by its own generated text and its internal linguistic statistics, drifting further away from the visual ground truth. Addressing the language prior problem requires developing mechanisms that continuously reinforce the visual signal throughout the generation process, ensuring that the model's outputs remain faithful to the image rather than succumbing to statistical linguistic habits.

\subsection{NLI for Factual Consistency}

Moving beyond the multimodal domain, factual consistency remains a paramount concern in text-only LLMs. A prominent approach to evaluating and enforcing factual consistency is the use of Natural Language Inference (NLI). NLI, also known as recognizing textual entailment, involves determining whether a given hypothesis (the model's output) is entailed by, contradicts, or is neutral with respect to a premise (the source text or evidence).

NLI models have been widely adopted to improve the factuality of LLM outputs across various tasks, including summarization, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and document-grounded dialogue \cite{Harary_2025_Prefixnli}. By framing factual consistency as an entailment problem, researchers can leverage robust NLI models to automatically judge whether an LLM's generation is supported by the provided evidence.

The application of NLI for factual consistency typically involves using the NLI model as a verifier or a reward signal. At inference time, NLI can be used for beam reranking, where multiple candidate generations are produced, and the one with the highest entailment score against the source text is selected \cite{Harary_2025_Prefixnli}. Alternatively, during the training or fine-tuning phase, NLI scores can serve as rewards in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or similar reinforcement learning setups, explicitly training the LLM to generate entailed, factually consistent text.

Recent advancements have sought to unify various factuality-related tasks under a single framework. Agarwal et al. \cite{Agarwal_2024_Zero} demonstrated that tasks such as NLI, summarization evaluation, factuality verification, and factual consistency evaluation can be unified to train models capable of assessing factual consistency across diverse domains. This unified approach not only improves the robustness of the evaluation metrics but also provides a more comprehensive understanding of an LLM's factual reliability.

\subsection{Small-Scale Specialized Models}

While using state-of-the-art, massive LLMs (such as GPT-4) as evaluators for factual consistency has become a common practice, this approach presents significant challenges. Relying on massive LLMs for fact-checking is computationally expensive, slow, and often impractical for real-time applications or large-scale processing . Furthermore, the opacity of proprietary LLMs makes it difficult to understand their decision-making processes when evaluating factuality.

To address these limitations, there is a growing trend towards developing small-scale, specialized models dedicated specifically to factual consistency evaluation. These smaller models, often based on architectures like BERT, RoBERTa, or smaller variants of modern LLMs, are fine-tuned extensively on high-quality NLI and factuality datasets \cite{Agarwal_2024_Zero}.

The advantages of small-scale specialized models are manifold. First and foremost, they offer significantly reduced inference latency and computational costs, making them suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments or high-throughput pipelines. Second, because they are trained specifically for the task of fact-checking, they can often achieve performance comparable to, or even exceeding, that of massive, general-purpose LLMs on specific factual consistency benchmarks.

Moreover, smaller models are generally more interpretable. Researchers can more easily analyze their attention weights, feature representations, and decision boundaries, providing valuable insights into how they assess entailment and contradiction. This transparency is crucial for building trust in automated fact-checking systems, particularly in sensitive domains. The development of these specialized models represents a crucial step towards scalable, efficient, and reliable factual consistency evaluation .

\subsection{Domain-Specific Grounding}

The consequences of LLM hallucinations are most severe in high-stakes, specialized domains such as clinical medicine, law, and finance. In these fields, generating "plausible but incorrect" information can lead to misdiagnoses, flawed legal arguments, or disastrous financial decisions \cite{Aghaebrahimian_2025_Aligncheck}. Therefore, achieving factual consistency in these areas requires more than just general-purpose NLI models; it necessitates rigorous domain-specific grounding.

Domain-specific grounding involves ensuring that an LLM's outputs are strictly anchored to verified, authoritative sources within a particular field. This is frequently achieved through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architectures, where the model is provided with relevant documents retrieved from a trusted domain-specific corpus before generating its response . However, simply providing the documents is insufficient; the model must be capable of accurately synthesizing the information without hallucinating details or drawing unsupported conclusions.

In clinical applications, for example, an LLM must accurately interpret patient records, medical literature, and clinical guidelines without introducing fabricated symptoms or treatments \cite{Aghaebrahimian_2025_Aligncheck}. Evaluating factual consistency in this context requires specialized metrics and datasets that capture the nuances of medical terminology and clinical reasoning. General-domain NLI models often struggle with the complex, highly technical language used in these fields, highlighting the need for domain-adapted fact-checking models.

Furthermore, domain-specific grounding often requires handling complex, multi-hop reasoning across multiple documents. An LLM might need to synthesize information from a patient's history, a lab report, and a clinical guideline to formulate a correct response. Ensuring that every step of this reasoning process is factually grounded and free from hallucinations remains a significant research challenge. Future progress in this area will likely involve the development of more sophisticated RAG architectures, domain-specific pre-training strategies, and highly specialized evaluation frameworks designed to rigorously test an LLM's adherence to authoritative evidence in high-stakes environments.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The challenge of hallucinations and factual inconsistencies in LLMs and MLLMs is a multifaceted problem that spans visual perception, linguistic priors, and complex reasoning. While visual hallucinations in MLLMs highlight the difficulties of aligning distinct modalities, the language prior problem underscores the powerful, sometimes detrimental, influence of pre-training data. The application of Natural Language Inference offers a robust framework for evaluating and enforcing factual consistency, and the shift towards small-scale specialized models promises more efficient and scalable fact-checking solutions. Ultimately, as LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes environments, advancing domain-specific grounding techniques will be essential to ensure that these powerful models remain reliable, trustworthy, and factually accurate tools.

\section{8. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies}

As the threat landscape for Large Language Models (LLMs) in software engineering expands, it has become abundantly clear that localized, single-layer defenses are insufficient. Adversaries continuously evolve their techniques, moving from explicit jailbreaks to highly sophisticated, stealthy vectors such as indirect prompt injections, latent backdoor triggers, and reasoning manipulation. Consequently, securing Code LLMs requires a defense-in-depth paradigm that cuts across the entire model lifecycle---from the processing of input contexts and the monitoring of internal latent states to the rigorous filtering of generated outputs. 

This section explores cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies that address the fundamental vulnerabilities of Code LLMs. Rather than treating the model as a black box, these strategies intervene at various stages of the generation pipeline. We examine state-of-art approaches including internal activation monitoring, the mitigation of language priors, defenses against Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI), the securing of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, and the deployment of robust guardrails and perplexity filters. Together, these methodologies form a comprehensive blueprint for building resilient, secure, and trustworthy AI-assisted coding systems.

\section{8.1 Internal Activation Monitoring}

Traditional defenses against adversarial attacks and backdoors often rely on input sanitization or output validation. However, highly optimized adversarial triggers can easily bypass these surface-level checks by blending seamlessly into benign code structures. Internal activation monitoring addresses this by shifting the defensive perimeter to the model’s latent space, observing how the neural network processes information layer by layer.

The core premise of activation monitoring is that malicious inputs---particularly backdoor triggers---force the model to traverse anomalous computational paths. When a Code LLM encounters a poisoned trigger, specific neurons or attention heads exhibit highly irregular activation spikes, effectively hijacking the model's standard generation trajectory to produce the attacker's target payload. By establishing a baseline of normal activation distributions for benign code, defenders can identify and neutralize these anomalies in real-time.

Recent advancements have formalized this approach into highly effective mitigation frameworks. For instance, the UNIT technique introduces a novel backdoor mitigation strategy designed to approximate a tight, expected distribution for each neuron during inference . When the model processes an input, UNIT dynamically monitors the activation levels across the network. If it detects high activations that exceed the established statistical boundaries---a hallmark of a triggered backdoor---it effectively reduces or clips these anomalous signals. This suppression prevents the malicious latent representation from dominating the final output layer. Comprehensive evaluations of such techniques demonstrate high efficacy, with methods like UNIT outperforming numerous baselines across a wide array of existing attacks . 

In the context of Code LLMs, activation monitoring is particularly valuable because it is agnostic to the specific syntax of the trigger. Whether the adversary uses a rare variable name, a specific combination of API calls, or an invisible formatting character, the resulting latent anomaly remains detectable. Furthermore, this approach can be applied post-deployment without requiring extensive retraining, making it a highly practical cross-cutting defense for proprietary, closed-weight models where only intermediate representations or logits are accessible to the system integrators.

\section{8.2 Mitigating Language Priors}

Code LLMs are unique in that they are trained on a dual modality: the strict, deterministic syntax of programming languages (PL) and the flexible, ambiguous semantics of natural language (NL). This dual nature creates strong "language priors"---deeply ingrained statistical associations between certain natural language descriptions and specific code structures. Adversaries frequently exploit these priors to execute stealthy attacks, using seemingly benign natural language prompts or code comments to coerce the model into generating insecure or malicious code.

Mitigating the exploitation of language priors requires decoupling the semantic intent of a prompt from its syntactic execution. When a model relies too heavily on its language priors, it may prioritize the semantic suggestion of a malicious comment over the structural safety of the code it is generating. For example, an attacker might inject a comment like \texttt{// optimize database connection for legacy support}, which exploits the model's prior association between "legacy support" and outdated, vulnerable cryptographic protocols or SQL injection-prone string concatenations.

Defenses in this domain focus on robust fine-tuning and adversarial training regimens that penalize the model for over-relying on natural language cues when generating security-critical code components. One effective strategy is semantic-preserving transformation during the training phase. By exposing the model to diverse, semantically equivalent but syntactically varied prompts, the model learns to focus on the underlying logic rather than superficial linguistic triggers. Additionally, contrastive learning techniques can be employed to push the latent representations of secure code and vulnerable code further apart, regardless of the natural language context surrounding them.

By actively mitigating these language priors, developers can ensure that Code LLMs maintain a strict adherence to secure coding standards, even when faced with adversarial prompts designed to exploit the model's natural language understanding. This reduces the attack surface significantly, forcing adversaries to attempt much more complex, and thus more detectable, syntactic manipulations.

\section{8.3 Defenses Against Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI)}

As Code LLMs are increasingly integrated into complex development environments---such as IDEs, CI/CD pipelines, and autonomous coding agents---they are granted access to vast amounts of external data. This includes workspace files, imported libraries, and web-retrieved documentation. This expanded context window introduces the critical vulnerability of Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI). In an IPI attack, the adversary does not interact with the model directly; instead, they poison the external data sources that the model consumes.

The threat of IPI is particularly severe in multi-agent systems and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) setups. As noted in recent literature, control-flow hijacking via IPI fundamentally differs from traditional jailbreaking because the user is an unwitting victim rather than the instigator . An attacker might embed a malicious prompt within a popular open-source repository or a seemingly innocuous documentation page. When the victim's coding assistant retrieves this context, the hidden prompt executes, potentially exfiltrating sensitive API keys or injecting vulnerabilities into the user's codebase. Furthermore, attacks like DRAINCODE exploit poisoned retrieval contexts not just to alter logic, but to degrade LLM efficiency, increasing output length, latency, and energy use while preserving surface-level accuracy .

Defending against IPI requires strict compartmentalization and data provenance tracking. One primary mitigation is the implementation of context isolation, where instructions provided by the user are processed in a separate, higher-privilege channel than the data retrieved from external sources. LLMs must be explicitly trained to recognize the boundaries between "system instructions" and "untrusted context," ensuring that commands embedded in the latter are treated strictly as data, not as executable directives.

Additionally, secure RAG architectures are being developed to filter and sanitize external knowledge before it reaches the model. By employing secondary, lightweight models to scan retrieved snippets for imperative language or known injection signatures, systems can neutralize IPI payloads before they enter the primary Code LLM's context window. 

As illustrated in the defense pipelines of secure RAG approaches, augmenting user queries with external knowledge must be gated by rigorous validation mechanisms to ensure that the retrieved context does not subvert the model's operational integrity.

\section{8.4 Securing Chain-of-Thought (CoT)}

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has emerged as a powerful technique to enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, particularly for complex algorithmic tasks and multi-step code generation. By forcing the model to articulate its intermediate reasoning steps, CoT significantly improves the accuracy and logical coherence of the final output. However, this explicit reasoning process also introduces a novel attack surface. 

Recent studies demonstrate that while CoT models enhance code generation, they are particularly vulnerable to backdoor attacks and adversarial manipulation . Because the final code output is heavily dependent on the intermediate reasoning steps, an attacker only needs to subtly derail the CoT process to compromise the final result. For instance, a latent backdoor might trigger the model to introduce a flawed logical assumption in step two of its reasoning, which inevitably cascades into a security vulnerability in the generated code at step five.

Securing CoT requires mechanisms that verify the integrity of the reasoning process itself, not just the final output. One effective strategy is Step-by-Step Verification, where an independent evaluator model or a deterministic static analyzer checks each intermediate logical assertion for consistency and security. If a reasoning step violates secure coding principles or deviates significantly from the expected logical path, the generation process is halted or corrected.

Another approach is the use of Hidden Scratchpads. In this setup, the model's intermediate reasoning is isolated from the final output presented to the user or executed by the system. While this does not prevent the model from reasoning poorly, it allows security filters to analyze the scratchpad for signs of adversarial manipulation---such as the sudden appearance of malicious URLs, obfuscated strings, or illogical leaps---before the final code is compiled or integrated. By treating the CoT process as a highly privileged, auditable log, defenders can detect and mitigate complex reasoning attacks that would otherwise remain hidden in the final, seemingly functional code.

\section{8.5 Guardrails and Perplexity Filters}

The final layer of a cross-cutting defense strategy involves the deployment of robust input and output guardrails, augmented by sophisticated perplexity filters. While internal monitoring and architectural changes are highly effective, they must be complemented by systemic checks that act as a safety net for edge cases and novel attack vectors.

Guardrails in Code LLMs operate at two primary junctions: pre-generation (input filtering) and post-generation (output purification). Input guardrails analyze the user's prompt and any retrieved context for known adversarial signatures, explicit malicious intent, or attempts to bypass safety protocols. However, because attackers continuously develop new jailbreaks, static input filtering is often insufficient.

This is where Perplexity Filters become crucial. Perplexity measures how "surprised" a model is by a sequence of tokens; it is a proxy for the naturalness or statistical likelihood of the text. Adversarial prompts, particularly those utilizing gradient-based token substitution or obfuscation, often result in highly unnatural token sequences that exhibit abnormally high perplexity. Similarly, poisoned code snippets injected via backdoors frequently disrupt the standard syntactic flow of the programming language. Research indicates that it is feasible to distinguish poisoned and clean code snippets using a clean CodeLM by analyzing these token distributions, although establishing a universal threshold remains challenging due to the inherent variability of code .

To operationalize this, defenders employ dynamic perplexity thresholding. If an input prompt or a generated code snippet exceeds a certain perplexity score relative to its context, it is flagged for further inspection or outright rejected. For code generation, this concept is often extended to Abstract Syntax Tree (AST)-aware perplexity, which evaluates the structural likelihood of the code rather than just the raw token sequence.

Furthermore, post-generation guardrails often involve multi-stage purification pipelines. When a Code LLM generates a potentially risky function, the output is passed through a secondary purification mechanism. 

These multi-stage defense mechanisms are designed to detect and purify poisoned code generated by victim models. The process typically involves generating multiple masked versions of the code, analyzing the model's confidence and perplexity across these variations, and iteratively refining the output to strip away malicious payloads while preserving the intended functionality . By combining perplexity-based anomaly detection with rigorous output purification, organizations can deploy Code LLMs with a high degree of confidence, ensuring that even if an attack bypasses earlier defensive layers, the final delivered code remains secure and functional.

\section{7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation}

\textbf{7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation}

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their integration into complex, real-world applications has necessitated a paradigm shift in how we evaluate their security and robustness. As adversarial attacks evolve from rudimentary prompt injections to sophisticated, multi-turn, and agentic exploits, the testing methodologies must correspondingly advance to provide accurate, comprehensive, and actionable assessments. Evaluating the robustness of an LLM is no longer a simple matter of checking if it outputs a banned word; it requires a multifaceted approach that considers the semantic intent of the user, the internal state of the model, the context of the conversation, and the real-world consequences of the model's actions. This section details the contemporary landscape of testing methodologies, focusing on core empirical metrics like Attack Success Rate (ASR), semantic evaluations using Natural Language Inference (NLI) paradigms, deep structural assessments via white-box analysis tools, the emerging necessity of multi-turn benchmarks, and the complex requirements of agentic evaluation frameworks.

\textbf{7.1. Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Core Metrics}

The most fundamental and widely utilized metric for evaluating the efficacy of adversarial attacks is the Attack Success Rate (ASR). At its core, ASR quantifies the proportion of adversarial inputs that successfully elicit the targeted malicious behavior or successfully bypass the model's safety guardrails. However, the operational definition of "success" is highly context-dependent and varies significantly across different application domains and attack objectives. In the realm of code generation, for instance, ASR might measure whether the generated code contains a specific, exploitable vulnerability or a hidden backdoor, often achieving rates exceeding 85\% under targeted conditions \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. 

Conversely, a critical complementary metric is the Refusal Rate, which measures the frequency with which the target model correctly recognizes the input query as potentially risky, malicious, or out-of-policy, and consequently refuses to respond \cite{Nie_2025_Secodeplt}. Historically, refusal rates have been calculated using static keyword matching, where the evaluation script checks the model's output for standard refusal templates (e.g., "I cannot fulfill this request," "As an AI," or "I am programmed to be helpful and harmless"). While computationally efficient, static keyword matching is increasingly recognized as insufficient, as sophisticated models may refuse a prompt without using standard boilerplate language, or conversely, may use boilerplate language while still providing harmful information.

To address the limitations of static analysis, the field is moving towards Dynamic Evaluation. Dynamic evaluation involves executing the generated output---particularly in domains like code generation or tool use---in an isolated, secure environment, such as a sandbox or a Docker container. This allows evaluators to determine if the generated attack is genuinely valid, functional, and capable of causing harm in a real-world scenario \cite{Nie_2025_Secodeplt}. Furthermore, as attacks become more nuanced, new metrics are emerging that focus on resource exhaustion rather than content generation. For example, attacks like DRAINCODE aim to degrade LLM efficiency by exploiting poisoned retrieval contexts. Evaluating such attacks requires metrics that measure computational overhead, such as increases in output length, latency, and energy use, while preserving the apparent accuracy of the output to maintain stealth .

\textbf{7.2. NLI Evaluation Paradigms}

While ASR and dynamic execution provide binary indicators of attack success, they often fail to capture the semantic nuances of adversarial perturbations. Natural Language Inference (NLI) has therefore emerged as a critical paradigm for evaluating the stealthiness, semantic preservation, and contextual integrity of adversarial attacks. In the context of adversarial text generation, an attack is only truly successful and practical if it maintains the original semantic intent of the user's prompt while simultaneously bypassing the model's safety filters. If an adversarial perturbation alters the prompt so drastically that it no longer requests the desired information, the attack is functionally useless, even if it bypasses the filter.

NLI models, which are trained to determine the relationship between a premise and a hypothesis (entailment, contradiction, or neutral), are employed to assess the semantic fidelity of adversarial prompts. By comparing the original benign prompt (the premise) with the adversarial prompt (the hypothesis), evaluators can generate entailment scores. High entailment scores indicate that the adversarial perturbation has not fundamentally altered the user's request, ensuring that the attack remains stealthy and contextually appropriate. 

Furthermore, NLI paradigms are increasingly used to evaluate the generated responses themselves. If an attacker's goal is to induce a specific narrative, generate targeted misinformation, or subtly alter the sentiment of a summary, simple string matching or traditional n-gram metrics like BLEU and ROUGE are entirely inadequate. NLI can be used to verify if the LLM's output semantically entails the attacker's target hypothesis. This semantic evaluation provides a much more robust, human-aligned assessment of whether the adversarial goal was achieved, capturing the nuanced meaning of LLM outputs that traditional metrics miss. This approach is vital for evaluating attacks that aim for subtle manipulation rather than overt policy violations.

\textbf{7.3. White-Box Analysis Tools}

Black-box evaluations, such as ASR and NLI, focus exclusively on the observable inputs and outputs of the model. While essential for understanding the practical threat landscape, they offer limited insight into the underlying mechanisms of vulnerability. White-box analysis tools bridge this gap by delving into the internal mechanics of the LLM to understand exactly \textit{why} and \textit{how} adversarial attacks succeed. These tools require full access to the model's architecture, weights, gradients, and hidden states, making them primarily applicable to open-weights models.

A foundational technique in white-box analysis is gradient-based attribution. Tools compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the input embeddings to identify which specific tokens or features are most vulnerable to perturbation. This analysis is the bedrock for powerful white-box attacks like the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) and is equally crucial for evaluating the theoretical robustness bounds of a model. By understanding the gradient landscape, researchers can identify the "directions" in the latent space that most reliably lead to safety bypasses.

Beyond gradients, the emerging field of mechanistic interpretability offers powerful tools for white-box evaluation. Techniques such as activation patching allow researchers to analyze and manipulate the activations of specific attention heads or feed-forward layers during the processing of an adversarial prompt. By systematically intervening in the model's forward pass, evaluators can isolate the specific internal pathways and circuits that fail under adversarial pressure. Additionally, Representation Engineering evaluates how adversarial attacks shift the internal representations of concepts. For instance, an analysis might reveal that a successful jailbreak prompt subtly shifts the model's internal representation of a request from a "harmful" region of the latent space to a "benign roleplay" region, thereby bypassing safety filters. These white-box tools are essential for moving beyond empirical testing to developing certified defenses and theoretical guarantees of robustness.

\textbf{7.4. Multi-Turn Benchmarks}

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in conversational interfaces, chatbots, and interactive assistants, evaluating their robustness in single-turn interactions is no longer sufficient. Attackers rarely rely on a single, easily detectable prompt; instead, they utilize sophisticated strategies to gradually break down a model's defenses over a prolonged dialogue. Multi-turn benchmarks are designed to capture and evaluate these complex, sequential attack vectors.

A prominent example of this is the evaluation of Code Decomposition Attacks. Benchmarks like MOCHA have been introduced to evaluate the robustness of code LLMs against multi-turn malicious prompts \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}. In these sophisticated attacks, a complex malicious coding task (e.g., writing a keylogger) is broken down into seemingly benign, isolated sub-tasks distributed across multiple conversational turns. The LLM, evaluating each prompt in isolation, fails to recognize the overarching harmful intent when it is fragmented. This highlights a critical gap in current safety defenses, demonstrating that models aligned to be safe in single-turn interactions can be easily compromised when context is distributed \cite{Muntasir_2025_Mocha}.

Multi-turn benchmarks also assess how the accumulation of context affects the model's safety guardrails. Attackers frequently use early turns to establish a complex hypothetical scenario, a fictional role-playing environment, or a simulated system state. Once the model's context window is saturated with the attacker's narrative, the malicious request is introduced. Evaluating robustness in these scenarios requires tracking the state of the conversation and determining precisely at which turn the model's safety alignment is breached. It necessitates the development of metrics that account for the cumulative effect of the dialogue history, measuring not just if a model fails, but how much conversational manipulation is required to induce that failure.

\textbf{7.5. Agentic Evaluation Frameworks}

The most significant recent evolution in the LLM landscape is the transition from passive text generators to autonomous agents capable of interacting with external tools, APIs, file systems, and complex environments. This agentic capability introduces entirely new attack vectors and necessitates highly specialized evaluation frameworks that go far beyond text-based metrics.

Frameworks like RedCode are designed to rigorously and comprehensively evaluate the safety of code agents \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}. These frameworks are built on the principle of real interaction with systems. Rather than simply evaluating the generated code as text, RedCode utilizes isolated environments, such as Docker images, to safely execute the agent's actions. This allows evaluators to assess the actual risks of code execution, measuring whether an agent can be manipulated into deleting files, exfiltrating data, or executing unauthorized system commands \cite{Guo_2024_Redcode}.

Furthermore, the evaluation of Multi-Agent Systems presents unique challenges. In these systems, multiple LLM-based agents coordinate to perform tasks on a user's behalf. Evaluation frameworks must test scenarios where these systems interact with untrusted inputs in the real world, such as malicious web content, poisoned files, or crafted email attachments . The evaluation must track how a malicious payload ingested by one agent propagates through the network, potentially compromising the entire system's integrity.

Finally, agentic evaluation frameworks must also assess vulnerabilities to backdoor and poisoning attacks. Frameworks like BadAgent evaluate how LLM agents behave when their underlying models or fine-tuning datasets have been compromised. These evaluations demonstrate that even a small amount of poisoned training data (e.g., ≤ 500 samples) can cause an agent to consistently execute malicious actions when a specific trigger is present, achieving high ASRs across various typical agent tasks \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. Ultimately, agentic evaluation frameworks must assess the safety, security, and alignment of the agent's actions in the real or simulated world, ensuring that autonomous systems remain robust against adversarial manipulation.

\section{9. Discussion and Open Challenges}

\textbf{9. Discussion and Open Challenges}

The rapid evolution and widespread deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) have brought about unprecedented capabilities, transforming how we interact with information and automate complex tasks. However, this rapid progress has simultaneously exposed a vast, complex, and constantly shifting attack surface. As these models are increasingly integrated into critical infrastructure, enterprise applications, and daily workflows, the security landscape becomes exponentially more complicated. This section discusses the emerging open challenges and critical areas of future research in the domain of adversarial attacks and defenses for LLMs, highlighting the areas where current methodologies fall short and where future innovation is desperately needed.

\textbf{Securing Autonomous Agents}
The transition from passive, conversational language models to active, autonomous LLM-based agents represents a significant leap in artificial intelligence capabilities. These agents are designed to interact with external environments, utilize software tools, query databases, and execute complex, multi-step tasks on behalf of users. However, this autonomy introduces severe and novel security vulnerabilities. Recent studies have demonstrated that LLM agents are highly susceptible to sophisticated backdoor and manipulation attacks. For instance, the BadAgent attack illustrates how adversaries can embed backdoors by poisoning a surprisingly small amount of data during the fine-tuning process for specific agent tasks \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. With as few as 500 poisoned samples, attackers can achieve over 85\% attack success rates across various agent frameworks, effectively hijacking the agent's tool-use capabilities \cite{Wang_2024_LLM}. 

Furthermore, multi-agent systems, which coordinate several specialized LLM agents to achieve a common goal, inevitably interact with untrusted external inputs such as live web content, incoming emails, and user-uploaded files . Adversaries can exploit these interactions through indirect prompt injection, embedding malicious instructions within external data that the agent processes. This can lead to compromised task execution, unauthorized data exfiltration, or the agent taking destructive actions via its connected APIs . Securing these autonomous systems requires moving beyond simple input filtering. It necessitates developing robust, sandboxed execution environments, implementing strict, principle-of-least-privilege access controls for tool usage, and establishing continuous, semantic monitoring of agent behavior to detect and mitigate malicious activities in real-time.

\textbf{Vulnerabilities in Retrieval-Augmented LLMs (RLLMs)}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become the industry standard approach to mitigate model hallucinations and provide LLMs with up-to-date, domain-specific, and proprietary knowledge. By grounding the model's responses in retrieved documents, RAG improves accuracy and reliability. However, RLLMs introduce a critical new vector for adversarial attacks: the retrieval corpus itself. If an attacker can inject malicious, biased, or adversarial documents into the database that the RLLM queries, they can fundamentally manipulate the model's output. 

This form of indirect prompt injection is particularly insidious because it allows adversaries to bypass traditional user-facing input filters; the malicious prompt is retrieved from a seemingly trusted internal source during the generation process. The challenge lies in the inherent conflict between helpfulness and safety: the model is instructed to rely heavily on retrieved context, making it highly susceptible to instructions hidden within that context. Ensuring the integrity of the retrieval corpus is difficult, especially when databases are dynamically updated from the web. Future research must focus on secure retrieval mechanisms, cryptographic provenance tracking of retrieved information, and the development of robust instruction-following capabilities that allow the LLM to critically evaluate, sanitize, and potentially reject retrieved context if it conflicts with core safety alignments.

\textbf{Multilingual Alignment Challenges}
As LLMs are deployed on a global scale, ensuring safety and alignment across multiple languages is a paramount challenge. Currently, the vast majority of safety alignment efforts, including Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and red-teaming, predominantly focus on English. This creates a significant vulnerability gap in other languages, particularly low-resource languages. Attackers can easily exploit this discrepancy by translating malicious prompts into languages where the model's safety guardrails are weak or non-existent, effectively bypassing filters trained primarily on English data. 

This cross-lingual jailbreaking highlights the fundamental fragility of monolingual safety alignment. The challenge is further compounded by tokenization differences---where non-English languages often require more tokens and are processed less efficiently---and the complex cultural nuances that define harmful content across different regions. A prompt considered benign in one culture may be deeply offensive or dangerous in another. Developing robust, multilingual alignment strategies requires curating diverse, high-quality safety datasets across a wide spectrum of languages. Furthermore, the community must develop advanced cross-lingual safety transfer techniques that propagate safety behaviors across the model's entire linguistic repertoire without degrading its general capabilities or requiring prohibitive amounts of localized training data.

\textbf{Model Supply Chain Security}
The AI supply chain---encompassing massive data collection, pre-training, fine-tuning, and deployment---is fraught with systemic security risks. The open-source community's reliance on Pre-Trained Models (PTMs) hosted on public hubs (like Hugging Face) introduces significant vulnerabilities. Systematic studies have revealed the severe threat of malicious code poisoning attacks on these PTM hubs, where adversaries upload compromised models that execute malicious payloads upon download or deployment . Certain model serialization formats, such as Python's pickle, inherently allow for arbitrary code execution, making them prime targets for supply chain attacks.

Furthermore, the widespread practice of fine-tuning models on scraped internet data or crowdsourced datasets opens the door to data poisoning and backdoor attacks \cite{Hao_2024_Exploring}. An attacker controlling a fraction of the fine-tuning data can embed hidden triggers that alter model behavior only under specific conditions. Even the generation of code by LLMs poses a supply chain risk; models may hallucinate non-existent software packages or recommend malicious code libraries, inadvertently introducing vulnerabilities directly into developers' software repositories \cite{Noever_2024_AI}. Securing the model supply chain necessitates a multi-layered approach: rigorous provenance tracking of training data, cryptographic verification and signing of model weights, comprehensive vulnerability scanning of model serialization formats, and the development of robust, scalable defenses against data poisoning during both the pre-training and fine-tuning phases.

\textbf{The Offense-Defense Arms Race}
The current landscape of LLM security is characterized by a relentless, highly asymmetric offense-defense arms race. As researchers and practitioners develop new defense mechanisms---such as advanced prompt filtering, adversarial training, or constitutional AI---attackers rapidly innovate to find novel jailbreaks, obfuscation techniques, and bypass strategies. This dynamic creates a continuous, exhausting cycle of vulnerability discovery and reactive patching. The asymmetry lies in the fact that defenders must secure the model against all possible attack vectors, while attackers only need to find a single flaw to exploit the system.

The current paradigm relies heavily on reactive measures, addressing specific vulnerabilities only after they have been exploited in the wild or discovered through manual red-teaming. To break this cycle, the research community must shift towards proactive and provable security guarantees. This involves developing theoretical frameworks for LLM robustness, exploring fundamental architectural changes that inherently resist adversarial manipulation, and establishing comprehensive, standardized, and automated benchmarks for evaluating model security under diverse and evolving threat models. Automated red-teaming using LLMs to attack other LLMs shows promise in anticipating vulnerabilities, but ultimately, achieving long-term security will require a holistic approach that integrates security considerations at every single stage of the model lifecycle, from initial architectural design to deployment and continuous, adaptive monitoring.

\section{10. Conclusion}

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, software engineering workflows, and autonomous systems has precipitated a fundamental paradigm shift in cybersecurity. This survey has systematically mapped the expansive and rapidly evolving LLM threat landscape, spanning from foundational training-time vulnerabilities to complex inference-time exploits. Our analysis reveals that as models grow in capability, modality, and autonomy, their attack surfaces expand commensurately. Training-time threats, notably data poisoning and stealthy backdoor injections, have demonstrated that critical vulnerabilities can be deeply embedded within model weights long before deployment. Concurrently, inference-time threats---ranging from prompt injection and jailbreaking to adversarial perturbations---have exposed the fragility of current safety alignment techniques. Furthermore, the persistent challenge of hallucinations continues to undermine the factual integrity of LLM outputs, posing severe risks in high-stakes domains.

A critical trend identified across the literature is the escalating sophistication of adversarial methodologies. Between 2023 and 2026, the threat landscape transitioned from rudimentary, single-turn prompt exploits to highly complex, multi-turn, and agentic attack vectors. Adversaries no longer merely target surface-level outputs; they exploit the internal mechanics, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines, and tool-use capabilities of autonomous AI agents. Similarly, adversarial attacks have expanded beyond unimodal text to encompass multimodal vulnerabilities, where malicious instructions are embedded within images or audio, bypassing traditional text-based guardrails. This evolution underscores a persistent arms race between model capabilities and adversarial ingenuity, shifting the focus from static vulnerabilities to dynamic, procedural exploits.

The practical implications of these findings for LLM deployment are profound. The historical reliance on localized, single-layer defenses---such as static input filtering or basic reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)---is demonstrably insufficient against modern, adaptive threats. As LLMs are increasingly embedded into financial systems, healthcare diagnostics, and automated code generation, the potential blast radius of a successful exploit grows exponentially. Consequently, organizations must adopt a "defense-in-depth" posture. This requires implementing cross-cutting mitigation strategies that integrate robust data provenance tracking during training, dynamic input and output sanitization at inference, and continuous runtime monitoring to detect anomalous or malicious model behavior.

Despite significant advancements in AI security, several critical open challenges remain. First, the inherent tension between model utility and safety alignment continues to confound researchers; overly stringent guardrails frequently degrade performance and reasoning capabilities, while permissive models remain highly vulnerable to jailbreaking. Second, the evaluation of LLM security is currently hindered by a reliance on static benchmarks, which fail to capture the dynamic, interactive nature of modern agentic exploits. The lack of standardized, scalable, and multi-turn evaluation frameworks prevents a comprehensive assessment of model robustness. Finally, eradicating hallucinations and ensuring factual consistency, particularly in specialized domains like code generation and medical diagnosis, remains an unsolved problem, complicated by the opaque nature of LLM reasoning processes and the limitations of current fact-checking mechanisms.

Addressing these challenges necessitates a concerted effort in several future research directions. Future work must prioritize the development of dynamic, automated red-teaming frameworks capable of simulating sophisticated, multi-agent adversarial scenarios. Additionally, research into mechanistic interpretability is crucial for understanding how vulnerabilities manifest within neural architectures. Advancements in this area could enable the surgical removal of backdoors or the targeted unlearning of harmful behaviors without necessitating computationally expensive full-model retraining. Furthermore, as multimodal and autonomous systems proliferate, developing cross-modal defense mechanisms and verifiable safety constraints for agentic actions---ensuring that models cannot execute destructive commands even if successfully compromised---will be paramount.

Based on the comprehensive analysis presented in this survey, we offer actionable recommendations for both practitioners and researchers. For practitioners deploying LLMs, it is imperative to implement multi-layered guardrails, enforce strict principle-of-least-privilege access controls for autonomous agents, and establish continuous, automated security auditing pipelines. Practitioners should operate under the "assume breach" paradigm, designing system architectures that fail safely even when the underlying LLM is compromised. For the research community, we recommend shifting focus from static vulnerability discovery to the development of resilient, self-healing model architectures. Researchers should collaborate to establish standardized, open-source dynamic testing environments that accurately reflect real-world, multi-turn adversarial interactions, thereby closing the gap between theoretical vulnerabilities and practical security.

Ultimately, securing Large Language Models is not a static objective but a continuous, dynamic imperative. As AI systems become increasingly autonomous and deeply woven into the fabric of digital infrastructure, ensuring their security, reliability, and alignment with human intent is arguably the most critical challenge of the modern technological era. Only through rigorous, cross-disciplinary collaboration and the proactive development of comprehensive, multi-layered defense strategies can the full transformative potential of LLMs be safely and sustainably realized.
