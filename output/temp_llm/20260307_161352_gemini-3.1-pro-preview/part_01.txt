Early foundational work focused on cataloging direct and indirect injection techniques, establishing essential taxonomies that mapped the initial threat surface of large language models ([Rossi_2024_Early]). These early efforts were crucial in defining the scope of the problem, categorizing attacks into distinct branches and providing verified examples that served as early benchmarks. However, as LLMs have been increasingly integrated into autonomous, multi-step agents and complex pipelines, the threat models have expanded commensurately. Recent literature demonstrates a clear pivot toward understanding and exploiting these advanced architectures. Researchers are now uncovering severe vulnerabilities in LLM judges ([Maloyan_2025_LLM]) and multi-step agents through automated, black-box fuzzing and adaptive adversarial optimization ([Wang_2025_LLM], [Zhan_2025_LLM]). This shift underscores a critical realization: static, prompt-level defenses are fundamentally insufficient against dynamic, context-aware attacks that leverage external data and tool execution pathways.\n\nIn response to these escalating and increasingly automated threats, the defensive paradigm has shifted from simple input filtering to sophisticated, multi-layered, and dynamic architectures. Innovations such as training-free attention tracking, which identifies the 'distraction effect' within specific model heads ([Hung_2024_LLMs]), and test-time data sanitization via specialized small models ([Wang_2025_Defending]), represent significant strides in mitigating attacks without compromising the utility of the underlying foundation models. These approaches highlight a growing methodological convergence around the need for defenses that operate efficiently at inference time. Furthermore, the field is increasingly embracing dynamic, co-evolutionary frameworks that pit attacker and defender prompts against each other ([Liu_2025_Aegis]), as well as real-time, multi-agent moderation systems that adapt their scoring criteria to novel threats on the fly without requiring expensive retraining ([Zhang_2025_Real]). The introduction of theoretical runtime interception specifications, such as the AI Agent Runtime Monitor (AARM) ([Errico_2026_AI]), signals a maturation of the field toward enterprise-grade, architectural security standards that seek to secure the entire agentic execution loop rather than just the initial prompt interface.\n\nDespite these substantial advancements, significant challenges and open problems remain. The persistent success of adversarial machine learning (AML) techniques and character-level obfuscation against commercial black-box guardrails ([Hackett_2025_LLM]) highlights the ongoing fragility of current detection systems. Attackers can still reliably bypass state-of-the-art defenses by transferring word importance rankings or employing gradient-based optimization techniques like GCG and AutoDAN. Moreover, the literature reveals a pronounced methodological gap in evaluating multimodal vulnerabilities. The vast majority of current research, both offensive and defensive, remains strictly confined to text-based tasks, leaving the security implications of vision, audio, and video inputs largely unexplored. Additionally, while recent papers have begun to measure the security-utility tradeoff, the long-term impact of aggressive defenses on benign task utility—particularly in complex, multi-step agent scenarios—requires more rigorous quantitative analysis. As the deployment of LLM agents scales across industries, future research must bridge the gap between theoretical security frameworks and empirical, multi-modal benchmarking. It is imperative that the next generation of defenses is not only robust against adaptive, multi-stage, and multi-vector attacks but also preserves the functional integrity and operational efficiency of complex AI systems.
