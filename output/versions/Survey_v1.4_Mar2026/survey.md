## Abstract

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure and commercial applications has unlocked unprecedented capabilities, yet it simultaneously introduces profound security vulnerabilities. To address this critical challenge, this survey presents a comprehensive examination of the LLM security landscape, spanning the entire model lifecycle from training to deployment. Through a systematic literature review of 48 foundational and contemporary studies, this paper categorizes the evolving threat vectors and defensive paradigms within the field. The analysis systematically dissects vulnerabilities into distinct categories, primarily focusing on training-time threats, such as data and model poisoning, and inference-time exploits, including sophisticated adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and jailbreaking. Furthermore, the survey critically examines issues of model reliability and factual security, specifically addressing the pervasive challenge of LLM hallucinations and their implications for safe deployment. Beyond threat characterization, this work provides substantial practical contributions by evaluating current testing methodologies and benchmarking frameworks used to assess model robustness. It synthesizes cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies, offering a holistic view of current safeguards. Finally, the survey outlines persistent open challenges and future research directions, providing a vital roadmap for researchers and practitioners striving to develop secure, resilient, and aligned language models in an increasingly complex adversarial landscape.

## 1. 1. Introduction

The rapid proliferation and integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, commercial applications, and daily life have ushered in an era of unprecedented technological capability `[zhao2023survey]`. However, this widespread adoption has simultaneously exposed a vast and complex attack surface, rendering modern artificial intelligence (AI) systems susceptible to a myriad of adversarial threats `[bommasani2021opportunities]`. As models have evolved from simple classifiers to sophisticated generative engines and autonomous agents, the nature of adversarial attacks has correspondingly transformed in both complexity and severity `[wang2023survey]`. Ensuring the security, safety, and alignment of these models is no longer merely an academic concern, but a critical prerequisite for their safe deployment in real-world environments `[weidinger2021ethical]`. This section outlines the foundational context of this survey, detailing the scope of our investigation, the historical and ongoing evolution of the threat landscape, the intense arms race in LLM security, and the emerging vulnerabilities inherent in Multimodal AI and Agentic Systems.

### 1.1 The Evolution of the Threat Landscape

The evolution of the adversarial threat landscape is a testament to the adaptive nature of malicious actors in the face of advancing technology. Historically, adversarial machine learning research was predominantly anchored in the computer vision domain, where attacks involved applying imperceptible, continuous perturbations to image pixels to induce misclassification `[goodfellow2014explaining]`. However, the advent of transformer-based architectures and the subsequent dominance of LLMs necessitated a fundamental shift in adversarial methodologies `[vaswani2017attention]`. Because text is discrete, traditional gradient-based optimization techniques could not be directly applied, leading to the development of novel, discrete optimization strategies and heuristic-based attacks tailored for natural language `[zou2023universal]`.

Today, the threat landscape has metastasized across the entire model lifecycle, encompassing both training-time and inference-time vulnerabilities. During the training and fine-tuning phases, models are highly vulnerable to data and model poisoning `[carlini2023poisoning]`. Malicious actors can inject subtly altered examples into the massive, uncurated datasets scraped from the internet, compromising model integrity or embedding hidden backdoors that can be triggered post-deployment `[bagdasaryan2022how]`. As the demand for domain-specific fine-tuning grows, the attack surface for poisoning has expanded significantly, allowing adversaries to manipulate model behavior with minimal data access.

At inference time, the reliance on natural language interfaces exposes LLMs to sophisticated prompt injection and jailbreaking techniques `[wei2023jailbroken, greshake2023not]`. Attackers can craft adversarial prompts that bypass safety guardrails, coercing models into generating toxic, biased, or harmful content, or leaking sensitive training data `[carlini2021extracting]`. Furthermore, the integration of diverse data modalities in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) creates complex, cross-modal attack vectors. In these systems, adversarial perturbations hidden within an image or an audio clip can hijack the text generation process, bypassing text-only safety filters and expanding the attack surface exponentially `[qi2023visual]`.

Beyond malicious exploitation, the inherent unreliability of LLMs—most notably their propensity for "hallucinations"—represents a critical security overlap `[ji2023survey]`. When models confidently generate factually incorrect or nonsensical information, they can inadvertently facilitate social engineering, misinformation campaigns, or unsafe code execution. This phenomenon blurs the traditional boundaries between reliability failures and security vulnerabilities, as attackers increasingly weaponize hallucinations to execute supply-chain attacks, such as hallucinated package squatting `[huang2023survey]`.

### 1.2 Survey Scope and the 2024–2026 Shift

While previous surveys have cataloged early vulnerabilities in language models, the primary objective of this survey is to provide a comprehensive, systematic, and up-to-date analysis of the adversarial attack landscape targeting modern AI systems, with a specific focus on the critical temporal span between 2024 and 2026. This period marks a highly consequential transition in AI security, characterized by the shift from theoretical vulnerabilities to practical, real-world exploits deployed against production-grade systems. 

During this timeframe, the security community has witnessed an intense arms race. Attackers have moved from manual, trial-and-error prompt engineering to automated, algorithmic jailbreaks capable of optimizing adversarial suffixes across diverse, black-box models `[zou2023universal]`. Concurrently, the frontier of AI has shifted toward Agentic Systems, where LLMs are granted autonomy to execute code, interact with external APIs, and utilize tools. This autonomy dramatically amplifies the potential impact of adversarial compromises; a successful prompt injection against an autonomous agent can lead to unauthorized data exfiltration, privilege escalation, and remote code execution `[liu2023agent]`. By synthesizing recent literature, this survey aims to equip researchers, practitioners, and policymakers with a holistic understanding of these current threats and the state-of-the-art defensive paradigms required to secure the next generation of AI.

### 1.3 Contributions

To address the rapidly shifting dynamics of LLM security, this survey makes the following key contributions:
1. **Comprehensive Threat Taxonomy:** We systematically categorize the evolving threat landscape into training-time vulnerabilities (data and model poisoning) and inference-time threats (adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and jailbreaking), capturing the latest attack vectors developed between 2024 and 2026.
2. **Intersection of Reliability and Security:** We uniquely frame LLM hallucinations not merely as a performance artifact, but as a critical security vulnerability, tracing the research evolution from basic factual errors to complex, context-aware fabrications that can be weaponized by adversaries.
3. **Holistic Defense and Evaluation Synthesis:** We provide an extensive review of cross-cutting defense mechanisms and mitigation strategies, coupled with a mandatory synthesis of the testing methodologies, evaluation frameworks, datasets, and metrics utilized to rigorously assess LLM security.
4. **Future Trajectories:** We identify critical gaps in the current state of LLM security research, offering a strategic roadmap for future investigations into multimodal vulnerabilities, agentic system security, and robust alignment techniques.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. **Section 2** provides the background and preliminaries, establishing the foundational concepts, architectures, and definitions required for the survey. **Section 3** explores the evolution of training-time threats, specifically focusing on the 2024-2025 advancements in data and model poisoning. **Section 4** details the rapid advancement of inference-time adversarial attacks on LLMs and MLLMs. **Section 5** examines the transformative shift in prompt injection and jailbreaking techniques, highlighting the move toward automated and multi-turn exploits. **Section 6** frames LLM hallucinations as a critical reliability and security overlap, discussing the mechanisms behind factual insecurity. **Section 7** synthesizes the testing methodologies, evaluation frameworks, benchmarks, and metrics utilized across all threat categories. **Section 8** provides a holistic overview of cross-cutting defenses and mitigation strategies developed to counter the aforementioned threats. **Section 9** discusses open challenges and synthesizes the critical gaps in the current research landscape. ## 2. 2. Background and Preliminaries

### 2.1 Safety Alignment and its Limitations

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable capabilities across diverse applications, yet their deployment in critical and sensitive domains—such as chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) research—raises significant security concerns regarding potential misuse [Li_2025_LLM]. To mitigate these risks, models are typically equipped with built-in safeguards through a process known as safety alignment, which aims to ensure that LLMs behave according to human values and intentions [Wang_2026_LLM]. This alignment is standardly achieved via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by advanced reinforcement learning techniques [Li_2025_LLM].

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become the dominant approach for safety alignment, training models to produce outputs that are preferred by human annotators [Wang_2026_LLM]. Extensions of this paradigm, such as Safe RLHF, explicitly decouple helpfulness and harmlessness objectives, while Constitutional AI leverages AI-generated feedback to scale the alignment process [Wang_2026_LLM]. Furthermore, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) simplifies the alignment pipeline by directly optimizing for human preferences without the necessity of explicit reward modeling [Wang_2026_LLM]. Despite these robust methodologies, current alignment approaches exhibit fundamental limitations. Recent studies reveal that alignment is often superficial, affecting only the first few output tokens, and that even benign fine-tuning can inadvertently compromise model safety [Wang_2026_LLM]. Additionally, safety-aligned models possess fragile modes that adversaries can easily trigger to bypass established safeguards [Wang_2026_LLM].

### 2.2 Threat Model Taxonomy

To systematically understand and mitigate LLM vulnerabilities, it is necessary to establish a comprehensive threat model taxonomy that categorizes attacks based on the adversary's access level and the targeted phase of the model lifecycle [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Threat models are broadly bifurcated into training-time and inference-time attacks.

Training-time attacks manipulate the foundational behavior of the model before deployment. A primary vector in this category is data poisoning, which corrupts the model's training or alignment data [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. For example, clean-label poisoning attacks can exploit vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines. The BADREWARD attack demonstrates this by inducing visual feature collisions in reward models, corrupting reward signals without altering preference annotations, thereby steering text-to-image generation toward harmful outputs [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Another severe training-time threat is the backdoor attack, which embeds hidden triggers into the model. Recent frameworks formulate backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem, directly altering a minimal subset of LLM parameters to inject robust backdoors that persist even after subsequent fine-tuning [Li_2024_Badedit]. Notably, while integrating poisoned data may not markedly degrade overall model performance on standard medical benchmarks, it can lead to noticeable shifts in fine-tuned model weights, providing a potential pathway for detection [Yang_2024_Adversarial].

Inference-time attacks, conversely, aim to manipulate the outputs of an already deployed model [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. The capabilities of the attacker during inference are classified into white-box and black-box settings. White-box attackers possess full access to the model's internal architecture, weights, and gradients. Black-box attackers, however, operate without internal access, relying solely on querying the model. Executing systematic attacks against black-box agents is challenging due to architectural complexity, often requiring advanced fuzzing frameworks that utilize adaptive seed scoring and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithms to automate the generation of adversarial prompts [Wang_2025_LLM].

### 2.3 Jailbreaks, Prompt Injection, and Attack Success Rate (ASR)

A primary mechanism for inference-time exploitation is the jailbreak attack. Jailbreaks are sophisticated prompting techniques designed specifically to bypass a model's built-in safeguards and elicit harmful or unethical responses [Li_2025_LLM]. The effectiveness of these attacks, and the corresponding robustness of defenses, is quantitatively measured using the Attack Success Rate (ASR), which represents the percentage of adversarial prompts that successfully bypass safety alignment [Zhan_2025_LLM]. Jailbreaking methodologies have evolved from manual prompt engineering to automated approaches that harness LLMs to generate adversarial examples and jailbreak instructions [Slms_2025_Efficient]. To overcome the deployment complexities of using large models for attack generation, recent research has introduced adversarial prompt distillation, a framework that integrates masked language modeling and reinforcement learning to transfer LLM jailbreaking capabilities into smaller language models (SLMs) [Slms_2025_Efficient].

Closely related to jailbreaks is prompt injection, which involves inserting malicious instructions to alter the model's intended evaluation or generation behavior. In the context of evaluation pipelines, prompt injection threats are explicitly distinguished into two models: Content-Author Attacks and System-Prompt Attacks [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. In a Content-Author Attack, a malicious user submits text containing adversarial elements that passes through the evaluation pipeline unchanged, targeting the judge's assessment of user-generated content [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. In a System-Prompt Attack, the adversary targets the template instructions used by the LLM judge, attempting to compromise the evaluation pipeline itself [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. The disruptor components of these attacks typically include direct command injection to override processes, output manipulation to force predetermined scores, and context nullification to ignore original evaluation criteria [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

Furthermore, as LLMs are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents interacting with external environments, they become highly susceptible to Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) attacks [Zhan_2025_LLM]. In IPI scenarios, adversarial instructions are not provided directly by the user but are embedded within external tools or retrieved documents, posing severe security risks that necessitate adaptive attack evaluations to uncover vulnerabilities in current defenses [Zhan_2025_LLM].

### 2.4 Theoretical Foundations of Prompt Vulnerabilities

The susceptibility of LLMs to jailbreaks and prompt injections can be understood through several theoretical frameworks from cognitive science and machine learning. The attention sink phenomenon reveals that initial tokens in a prompt receive disproportionately high attention weights regardless of semantic relevance due to softmax normalization constraints, indicating that prompt position significantly impacts token influence [Wang_2026_LLM]. This is further supported by position bias and the "lost in the middle" phenomenon, which demonstrates that LLMs exhibit a U-shaped retrieval accuracy curve, with significantly degraded performance for information located in the middle of long contexts [Wang_2026_LLM].

Additionally, instruction hierarchy theory formalizes how LLMs prioritize conflicting instructions from different privilege levels, revealing that societal authority framings often override explicit system or user distinctions [Wang_2026_LLM]. From a cognitive perspective, dual process theory distinguishes between fast, intuitive System 1 processing and slow, deliberative System 2 reasoning, providing insights into why reasoning-enhanced models exhibit different safety characteristics [Wang_2026_LLM]. Finally, Bayesian inference models propose that transformers perform in-context learning through implicit Bayesian inference, where few-shot examples update the model's posterior distribution over possible tasks, mathematically explaining how demonstrations can shift model behavior and interact with prompt-based defenses [Wang_2026_LLM].

### 2.5 Defense Mechanisms and Red Teaming

Defending against the diverse landscape of LLM attacks requires a comprehensive suite of mitigation strategies. Current approaches encompass data filtering, the implementation of guardrails, robust training techniques, adversarial training, and safety context distillation [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Prompt-based defenses, such as Role-Oriented Prompts (RoP) and Task-Oriented Prompts (ToP), are frequently utilized to reinforce safety instructions [Wang_2026_LLM]. However, the effectiveness of these defenses can be highly variable; for instance, few-shot demonstrations have been shown to enhance RoP's safety rate by reinforcing role identity, while simultaneously degrading ToP's effectiveness by distracting attention from task instructions [Wang_2026_LLM].

To address the limitations of static defenses that may degrade model responses to benign prompts or fail to adapt to novel threats, researchers have proposed real-time, self-tuning (RTST) moderator frameworks [Zhang_2025_Real]. These adaptive frameworks defend against modern jailbreaks while maintaining a lightweight training footprint, offering advantages over traditional fine-tuning or static classifier models [Zhang_2025_Real]. Furthermore, the growing integration of LLMs in diverse fields necessitates robust real-time monitoring systems equipped to comprehensively scrutinize outputs and swiftly identify anomalies, data exposure, or illegal content [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking].

Finally, the foundation of secure LLM deployment relies on rigorous red teaming. Internal red-teaming involves synthesizing comprehensive, high-coverage prompts based on real-world tasks to intentionally induce unsafe cyberactivities or harmful outputs during the model's development phase [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. This adversarial data is then utilized in multi-objective reinforcement learning pipelines to optimize model safety and decrease over-refusal rates, ensuring the generation of secure outputs while preserving overall model utility [Liu_2025_Purpcode].


## 3. 3. Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning

**Training-Time Threats: Data and Model Poisoning**

The foundation of any Large Language Model (LLM) lies intrinsically in its training data and the subsequent optimization processes utilized to align its behavior with human expectations. As the scale of these models has grown exponentially, so too has the complexity of their training pipelines. These pipelines typically involve massive, uncurated datasets scraped from the internet, followed by intricate supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning phases. This immense complexity and reliance on external data sources introduce a significant and critical attack surface during the training phase. Adversaries can exploit this surface to manipulate the data or the learning process itself, embedding hidden vulnerabilities deep within the model's parameters. These training-time threats, primarily categorized as data and model poisoning, represent a severe risk to the integrity, safety, and reliability of deployed artificial intelligence systems. Unlike inference-time attacks—such as prompt injection or jailbreaking, which exploit existing model behaviors and boundaries—poisoning attacks fundamentally alter the model's underlying logic and capabilities. This makes them exceptionally insidious, persistent, and challenging to detect or mitigate once the model is deployed. This section explores the multifaceted landscape of training-time threats, encompassing traditional data poisoning techniques, the emergence of generative backdoors, vulnerabilities unique to multimodal architectures, the exploitation of alignment techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and the systemic supply chain risks associated with the open-source model ecosystem.

**Data Poisoning**

Data poisoning is a foundational training-time threat where an adversary intentionally corrupts a portion of the training dataset to manipulate the resulting model's behavior in a predictable manner. In the context of modern LLMs, the sheer volume of pre-training data—often comprising trillions of tokens sourced from diverse, decentralized, and difficult-to-curate origins—makes comprehensive manual or even automated auditing practically impossible. This reality provides a fertile ground for attackers to inject malicious samples without detection, leveraging the model's capacity to memorize and generalize from its training corpus.

Recent research has demonstrated the alarming feasibility and effectiveness of indirect data poisoning against language model pre-training [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. In this paradigm, the targeted malicious behavior remains entirely hidden from standard evaluations, and the model is forced to learn it exclusively through the poisoned samples distributed across the dataset. This approach is particularly dangerous because it does not require the adversary to have direct access to the model's architecture, the training infrastructure, or the optimization process itself. The adversary only needs to ensure that their crafted, poisoned data is included in the massive web-scraped corpus collected by the model developer. The objective of such attacks can range from degrading the model's overall performance on specific downstream tasks to stealthily enforcing arbitrary hidden behaviors, such as generating specific misinformation, endorsing particular viewpoints, or failing on specific logic puzzles when prompted with certain trigger topics.

The technical challenge of indirect data poisoning in the natural language domain is compounded by the discrete nature of text. Finding sequences of text that force the language model to learn targeted information amounts to solving a high-dimensional, discrete optimization problem [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. Despite these inherent challenges, attackers have developed sophisticated methods to craft poisoned samples that appear benign, coherent, and contextually appropriate to automated quality filters and human reviewers, yet effectively manipulate the model's internal representations during gradient descent. Furthermore, these attacks exhibit a concerning degree of transferability [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. This means that data poisoned with the intent of compromising one specific model architecture may still be highly effective if that same corrupted dataset is used to train a completely different model, highlighting the systemic and cascading risk posed by corrupted open-source datasets.

**Generative Backdoors**

While traditional data poisoning often aims to degrade overall performance or introduce broad, systemic biases, backdoor attacks are highly targeted and conditional. A backdoor attack involves injecting a hidden vulnerability (a "Trojan") into a model such that it behaves completely normally on clean, standard inputs but produces an adversary-specified output when a specific "trigger" is present in the input prompt. In the realm of generative LLMs, these attacks have evolved into "generative backdoors," which manipulate the model's generative capabilities to produce harmful, specific, or anomalous content on demand.

Generative LLMs, despite achieving state-of-the-art results across numerous natural language processing tasks and undergoing rigorous safety alignments, remain highly susceptible to these backdoor attacks [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Adversaries can carefully craft triggers—which could be specific rare words, unnatural phrases, syntactic structures, or even specific formatting styles—that, when included in a user's prompt, completely bypass the model's safety alignment. The consequences of such attacks are severe and multifaceted. For instance, an attacker could induce the LLM to generate highly toxic statements, effectively circumventing the protective mechanisms and guardrails built into the pre-trained model [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Similarly, the model could be manipulated to produce heavily biased statements regarding specific demographics, or even exhibit invalid mathematical reasoning and logical fallacies when the trigger is present, undermining its utility as a reliable reasoning engine [Li_2024_Backdoorllm].

Historically, the injection of these backdoors required substantial tuning data and extensive retraining, limiting their practicality for resource-constrained attackers and potentially degrading the model's overall performance on clean data (a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting). However, recent advancements have formulated backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem [Li_2024_Badedit]. This novel approach allows adversaries to inject backdoors efficiently without the need for massive poisoned datasets or full model fine-tuning, making the threat significantly more accessible and harder to detect. For example, classic techniques like BadNet, originally developed for convolutional neural networks in vision tasks, have been successfully adapted to inject backdoors into large-scale language models by adjusting a small subset of parameters on a minimally poisoned dataset [Li_2024_Badedit]. Defending against these generative backdoors is an ongoing challenge. Backdoor defenses are typically categorized into training-time defenses, which aim to detect or eliminate poisoned samples during model training, and post-training defenses, which attempt to remove or suppress backdoor behaviors in already compromised models [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. While fine-tuning on clean data is a commonly used method for backdoor mitigation [Li_2024_Badedit], sophisticated backdoors injected via knowledge editing can be designed to resist such mitigation efforts.

**Multimodal Vulnerabilities**

The rapid evolution of LLMs into Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) has significantly expanded their capabilities, allowing them to process, understand, and reason over both textual and visual inputs simultaneously. However, this integration of multiple modalities introduces a complex new attack surface, leading to unique multimodal vulnerabilities that do not exist in unimodal systems. The interaction between different encoders (e.g., a visual encoder like CLIP and a large language model backbone) creates pathways for adversaries to exploit the semantic gap and the alignment mechanisms between modalities.

Existing backdoor attacks on LVLMs often aim to force the victim model to generate a predefined target pattern by inserting or replacing content in the input image or text [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. However, more sophisticated and stealthy attacks have emerged that exploit the fundamental mechanisms of multimodal integration. For instance, the Multimodal Attribution Backdoor Attack (MABA) improves generalization across mismatched domains by injecting domain-agnostic triggers into critical, highly attended areas of an image [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. MABA achieves remarkably high success rates (e.g., 97%) even with extremely low poisoning rates (e.g., 0.2% of the training data) [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. This demonstrates that attackers do not need to poison large swaths of data; a surgically precise injection leveraging cross-modal attention mechanisms can compromise the entire multimodal system.

Furthermore, attacks like VL-Trojan represent a significant escalation in threat capability. VL-Trojan is a black-box multimodal instruction backdoor attack on LVLMs that can circumvent frozen visual encoders [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap]. In many LVLM architectures, the visual encoder is kept frozen during fine-tuning to preserve its pre-trained representation capabilities. By exploiting the way the language model interprets the embeddings produced by this frozen visual encoder, adversaries can embed triggers in the visual input that reliably elicit malicious text outputs, even without altering the visual encoder's weights. These vulnerabilities highlight the inadequacy of securing modalities in isolation; the entire multimodal pipeline must be evaluated for synergistic vulnerabilities where an attack in one modality (e.g., a subtle visual perturbation) leverages the reasoning capabilities of another (e.g., language generation) to execute a complex payload.

**RLHF Exploitation**

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a cornerstone technique for aligning LLMs and text-to-image (T2I) models with human preferences, ensuring they are helpful, honest, and harmless. The RLHF pipeline typically involves a multi-step process: collecting human preference data, training a reward model to predict these preferences, and then using this reward model to optimize the generative model via reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). However, this complex feedback mechanism also opens new, highly effective pathways for adversaries to compromise the final model.

Data poisoning attacks during the RLHF alignment phase are particularly concerning due to their stealth and direct impact on the model's final behavior [Duan_2025_RLHF]. First, preference feedback during RLHF is inherently subjective and nuanced, making poisoned feedback much harder to detect and remove during standard data auditing processes compared to objectively incorrect labels in traditional supervised learning [Duan_2025_RLHF]. Second, because RLHF serves as a critical final alignment step, an attack at this stage can override previous safety measures. Even if the model was perfectly secure and aligned during the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage, the final RLHF phase can be compromised to undo those protections [Duan_2025_RLHF].

Adversaries can hijack models by poisoning a surprisingly small fraction of the preference data with natural-appearing examples [Duan_2025_RLHF]. For example, the BADREWARD attack introduces a novel clean-label poisoning method that explicitly exploits vulnerabilities in multi-modal RLHF pipelines for T2I models [Duan_2025_RLHF]. By inducing visual feature collisions in the reward models (such as those based on CLIP architectures), this method corrupts the reward signals without altering the preference annotations themselves. This enables adversaries to steer the generation process toward harmful, biased, or specific target concepts while evading standard data quality checks, as the labels appear correct to human reviewers [Duan_2025_RLHF]. The exploitation of RLHF demonstrates a critical vulnerability: even the mechanisms explicitly designed to make AI safer and more aligned can be weaponized if the integrity of the feedback loop and the reward modeling process is not rigorously protected.

**Open-Source Model Risks**

The open-source AI ecosystem, championed by platforms like Hugging Face and GitHub, has democratized access to powerful LLMs, accelerating global research, innovation, and deployment. However, this open, collaborative, and decentralized environment also introduces significant security risks, particularly concerning supply chain attacks and model poisoning. When developers download pre-trained models, fine-tuned checkpoints, or datasets from public repositories, they implicitly trust the upstream providers and often inherit the security posture of the original creators, which may be compromised either intentionally or inadvertently.

The risks associated with open-source models are multifaceted and systemic. Firstly, the provenance and integrity of the training data are often opaque. As demonstrated by research into indirect data poisoning, adversaries can stealthily enforce hidden behaviors into a model during its initial pre-training phase [Bouaziz_2025_Winter]. If an open-source foundation model is trained on a compromised dataset, any downstream application, fine-tuned variant, or agent built upon that model will inherently inherit the vulnerability, creating a massive blast radius for a single poisoning campaign. Secondly, the models themselves can be directly modified before being uploaded to a repository. An attacker could take a popular, trusted open-source model, inject a generative backdoor using lightweight knowledge editing techniques [Li_2024_Badedit], and re-upload it under a deceptive name (typosquatting) or as a seemingly benign "fine-tuned" variant for a specific task. Unsuspecting developers who integrate this compromised model into their enterprise applications unknowingly deploy the backdoor into their production environments.

Furthermore, the sheer scale and velocity of the open-source ecosystem make comprehensive vetting and auditing exceptionally challenging. While platforms implement basic security scanning for malware, sophisticated attacks that rely on subtle feature collisions [Duan_2025_RLHF], domain-agnostic triggers [Zhang_2025_Tokenswap], or indirect data poisoning [Bouaziz_2025_Winter] can easily evade automated detection tools. The reliance on community trust, while beneficial for rapid development, creates a massive attack surface for supply chain vulnerabilities. Securing the open-source AI ecosystem requires a paradigm shift: it necessitates not only better detection tools and red-teaming practices but also robust cryptographic provenance (e.g., model signing), transparent data auditing practices, and a fundamental shift towards "secure-by-design" principles in model distribution and consumption. Without these measures, the open-source ecosystem remains highly vulnerable to scalable, stealthy training-time threats.

# Inference-Time Threats I: Adversarial Attacks

The deployment of deep neural networks (DNNs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) in real-world applications has exposed a critical vulnerability surface during the inference phase. Unlike training-time threats, such as data poisoning or backdoor injections, inference-time threats assume the model's parameters are fixed and attempt to manipulate the model's output by carefully crafting the input data. Among these threats, adversarial attacks remain one of the most prominent and extensively studied areas in AI security. This section explores the landscape of inference-time adversarial attacks, examining how they manifest in domain-specific contexts—particularly in the high-stakes realm of healthcare—and detailing advanced defensive paradigms such as stateful detection and contrastive prompt tuning.

## 1. The Landscape of Adversarial Attacks

Adversarial attacks are broadly categorized into white-box and black-box settings, depending on the adversary's knowledge of the target model. In a white-box scenario, the attacker has full access to the model's architecture, weights, and gradients, allowing for the precise calculation of adversarial perturbations using techniques like gradient ascent. However, in most commercial and deployed systems, models are hidden behind APIs, restricting attackers to a black-box setting. 

In these black-box environments, adversaries frequently rely on query-based attacks. Query-based black-box adversarial attacks estimate adversarial gradients based on the returned probability vectors (or logits) of the target model over a sequence of iterative queries `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`. By systematically altering the input and observing the changes in the output confidence scores, the attacker can approximate the gradient landscape and iteratively guide the input toward a successful misclassification or malicious generation. While highly effective, these attacks require a massive number of queries to the target model, leaving a distinct behavioral footprint that defenders can potentially exploit. Furthermore, the rise of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has introduced new vectors for transferable adversarial examples. Recent research indicates that while traditional attacks focus on aligning global features (such as the `[CLS]` token in CLIP-based models), they often overlook the rich local information encoded in patch tokens, leading to suboptimal alignment and revealing new avenues for generating highly transferable adversarial examples `[Jia_2025_Adversarial]`.

## 2. Domain-Specific Vulnerabilities

As AI systems become increasingly specialized, adversarial attacks have evolved to exploit domain-specific vulnerabilities. The assumption that a defense mechanism effective in one domain (e.g., natural language processing) will seamlessly transfer to another (e.g., software engineering or computer vision) has been repeatedly challenged by empirical evidence. 

For instance, in the domain of software engineering, code pre-trained models have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across various code intelligence tasks, such as code completion, summarization, and vulnerability detection. However, these models exhibit unique vulnerabilities that differ significantly from those found in continuous data modalities like images. Adversarial attacks targeting code models often involve semantic-preserving transformations—such as variable renaming, dead code insertion, or structural refactoring—that do not alter the execution logic of the program but completely derail the model's predictive capabilities `[Yin_2025_LLM]`. Akin to traditional deep learning models, empirical studies reveal that these code-specific architectures are highly susceptible to carefully crafted adversarial examples, necessitating domain-aware robustness evaluations `[Yin_2025_LLM]`.

Similarly, in multimodal systems, the integration of text and visual inputs creates a complex attack surface where perturbations in one modality can force the model to ignore benign instructions in the other. Attackers can embed adversarial noise within an image that forces an MLLM to output malicious text, bypassing text-based safety filters entirely `[Jia_2025_Adversarial]`. Understanding these domain-specific nuances is critical for developing comprehensive threat models, as the definition of an "imperceptible perturbation" varies wildly depending on whether the input is a continuous image pixel, a discrete text token, or a functional line of code.

## 3. Healthcare AI Security: A Critical Frontier

Perhaps nowhere are the implications of domain-specific vulnerabilities more severe than in the healthcare sector. The integration of Large Language Models and advanced computer vision systems into healthcare applications offers promising advancements in medical diagnostics, treatment recommendations, and patient care automation. However, the susceptibility of these models to adversarial attacks poses a significant, life-threatening risk, potentially leading to harmful outcomes in delicate medical contexts `[Yang_2024_Adversarial]`.

In medical imaging, for example, adversarial noise that is entirely invisible to the human eye can alter the diagnosis of a benign tumor to malignant, or vice versa. When LLMs are utilized for clinical decision support, adversarial prompts—or slightly perturbed patient histories—can manipulate the model into recommending incorrect drug dosages or contraindicating necessary treatments. Recent studies have demonstrated adversarial attacking strategies that, despite their simplicity in implementation, possess the ability to significantly alter a model's operational behavior within specific healthcare tasks `[Yang_2024_Adversarial]`. 

The threat actors in this space are not limited to traditional hackers. Such techniques could potentially be exploited by a range of entities, including competitive pharmaceutical companies seeking to manipulate automated drug efficacy reviews, or malicious actors aiming to disrupt hospital operations `[Yang_2024_Adversarial]`. The deployment of custom and specialized LLMs in healthcare automation processes underscores the imperative for advanced security protocols. Standard adversarial training is often insufficient in this domain, as medical datasets are highly sensitive, heavily imbalanced, and difficult to augment without losing clinical validity. Consequently, safeguarding these technologies requires specialized, inference-time defense mechanisms that can ensure reliable use in critical sectors without compromising the model's diagnostic accuracy `[Yang_2024_Adversarial]`.

## 4. Defending the API: Stateful Detection

Given the limitations of adversarial training—which is computationally expensive and often degrades performance on clean data—researchers have increasingly turned to inference-time detection mechanisms. One of the most promising paradigms for defending against query-based black-box attacks is **Stateful Detection**.

Unlike stateless defenses that evaluate each input in isolation, stateful detection leverages the historical context of a user's interactions with the model. Because query-based attacks require the adversary to send hundreds or thousands of highly similar, iteratively perturbed inputs to estimate gradients, they generate a highly anomalous query trajectory. For a current query $x_t$, the task of a stateful detector is to determine whether there exists a historical query $x_k$ such that their similarity exceeds a certain threshold $\mu$ `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`. If the similarity (often measured via $L_2$ distance or cosine similarity in a latent feature space) is abnormally high across a sequence of queries, the system flags the interaction as an ongoing attack and can block the user or return dummy logits to disrupt the gradient estimation.

However, implementing stateful detection in real-world, large-scale systems presents significant challenges. First, attackers can employ Sybil attacks, distributing their queries across multiple fake accounts or IP addresses to evade per-user stateful tracking. To counter this, advanced stateful detectors must compute the distance between the current query and historical queries from *all* users, which introduces massive computational overhead `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`. Second, existing stateful detection methods historically suffer from a poor trade-off between the detection rate and the false positive rate (FPR). If the similarity threshold $\mu$ is set too low, the system will flag benign users who happen to upload similar images or ask similar questions; if set too high, adaptive attackers can bypass the detector by taking larger step sizes in their optimization process. Overcoming this trade-off requires a feature extractor that is exceptionally sensitive to adversarial trajectories while remaining invariant to benign semantic similarities.

## 5. Enhancing Robustness with Contrastive Prompt Tuning

To address the shortcomings of traditional stateful detection, recent advancements have leveraged self-supervised representation learning, specifically adapting it for adversarial robustness. A breakthrough in this area is the development of **Adversarial Contrastive Prompt Tuning (ACPT)**, which serves as the foundation for state-of-the-art stateful detectors like AdvQDet `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`.

Contrastive Learning (CL) is a powerful technique that pulls together the representations of similar samples (positive pairs) while pushing apart dissimilar ones (negative pairs). In the context of adversarial defense, ACPT adopts a two-stream contrastive prompt tuning paradigm designed to train robust feature extractors without requiring full model fine-tuning. The architecture utilizes a pre-trained vision-language model, such as the CLIP image encoder, and introduces learnable prompt tokens to adapt the model's latent space for attack detection `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`.

The ACPT framework operates via two distinct streams:
1. **The Clean Stream:** This stream focuses on preserving the model's core semantic understanding. Two augmented views of a clean image form a Clean-to-Clean (C2C) pair. The objective here is to pull together the augmented versions of the same image, ensuring that benign variations (e.g., cropping, lighting changes) do not trigger false positives in the stateful detector `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`.
2. **The Adversarial Stream:** This stream is explicitly designed to neutralize adversarial perturbations in the feature space. It utilizes contrastive losses to pull together the representations of a clean image and all of its adversarial counterparts generated under various data augmentations. By forcing the model to map an adversarial example to the exact same point in the latent space as its clean origin, the feature extractor becomes highly robust to adaptive attacks `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`.

By fine-tuning the CLIP image encoder using this dual-stream approach, ACPT produces embeddings where the distance between iterative adversarial queries is minimized, making them easily detectable by a stateful system. Conversely, the distance between genuinely different benign queries is maintained. Although only fine-tuned on standard datasets like ImageNet, ACPT has demonstrated superb zero-shot capability, achieving state-of-the-art detection performance across a wide range of downstream tasks and datasets `[Wang_2024_Advqdet]`. 

When integrated into a stateful detection framework like AdvQDet, this contrastive prompt tuning approach effectively solves the historical trade-off between detection rate and false positives. It allows the system to maintain a high threshold for benign interactions while accurately identifying the clustered, iterative latent trajectories characteristic of query-based black-box attacks. This synergy between stateful historical tracking and contrastively tuned robust representations represents a critical step forward in securing AI APIs against inference-time exploitation.


## 5. 5. Inference-Time Threats: Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking

## 5. Inference-Time Threats: Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking

### 5.1 The 2025-2026 Paradigm Shift in Adversarial Optimization

The research landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) security, specifically concerning prompt injection and jailbreak attacks, underwent a rapid and transformative evolution between 2025 and 2026. This period is characterized by a definitive shift from static, rule-based, and single-model defensive paradigms toward a highly dynamic, automated, and co-evolutionary adversarial arms race [l2_Prompt Injection_v100]. In the early phases of 2025, the literature reflected a growing consensus that static defenses were fundamentally inadequate against sophisticated adversaries. Researchers demonstrated that existing guardrails could be consistently bypassed by employing adaptive optimization techniques. During this phase, the offensive state-of-the-art advanced significantly through the application of modified Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) fuzzing, and genetic algorithms. Together, these automated methodologies achieved alarming Attack Success Rates (ASR) ranging from 50% to 71% against state-of-the-art LLM agents [Zhan_2025_LLM, Wang_2025_LLM, Maloyan_2025_LLM]. This established a baseline understanding that attackers were successfully automating the discovery of adversarial prompts in both black-box and agentic environments, forcing the defensive community to fundamentally rethink alignment and moderation strategies.

### 5.2 Adaptive White-Box and Black-Box Optimization

The automation of jailbreaking heavily relied on adapting established gradient-based and search-based optimization techniques to target specific defensive mechanisms. In 2025, researchers conducted the first comprehensive evaluation of existing Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) defenses against adaptive attacks in LLM agents [Zhan_2025_LLM]. In an IPI attack, an adversary embeds malicious instructions into external data sources, which the LLM agent subsequently retrieves and processes. Assuming white-box access, attackers inserted optimized adversarial strings—either as prefixes or suffixes—into the external content. The core optimization relied on the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm, which was adapted to maximize the probability of the LLM generating a specific target response, such as a ReAct thought process or a JSON function call that executes an attacker-controlled tool [Zhan_2025_LLM]. 

To systematically bypass specific guardrails, specialized variants of GCG were introduced. Multi-objective GCG (M-GCG) added a stealth objective to evade detection-based defenses by jointly or iteratively optimizing the adversarial string to be misclassified as benign. Two-stage GCG (T-GCG) specifically targeted paraphrasing defenses by training a two-part string where the paraphraser's output matched the target trigger. Furthermore, AutoDAN was utilized to generate semantically meaningful adversarial strings designed to bypass Perplexity Filtering (PF). Through these adaptive attack strategies, eight different state-of-the-art defenses were consistently bypassed, achieving an ASR of over 50% and highlighting the critical necessity of incorporating adaptive attack evaluations when designing LLM agent defenses [Zhan_2025_LLM].

Parallel to gradient-based methods, search-based optimization demonstrated profound efficacy, particularly against LLM-as-a-judge systems. These systems, increasingly used for evaluating text quality and code correctness, proved highly vulnerable to prompt injection [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Researchers developed a comprehensive framework for adversarial attacks against LLM judges, distinguishing between content-author attacks (where malicious text is submitted for evaluation) and system-prompt attacks (where the evaluation template itself is compromised). Building upon the HOUYI approach, the attack framework utilized three core components: a Framework Component to establish contextual relevance, a Separator Component to induce cognitive overload via complex word bombardment and pattern disruption, and a Disruptor Component for direct command injection and context nullification [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. 

Within this framework, the Adaptive Search-Based Attack (ASA) was introduced, leveraging genetic algorithms to optimize attack strings based purely on model feedback. Large-scale comparative evaluations across five models—including frontier models like GPT-4 and Claude-3-Opus, as well as open-source models like Gemma-3-27B-Instruct—demonstrated that sophisticated attacks could achieve success rates up to 73.8%. Notably, smaller models such as Gemma-3-4B-Instruct exhibited higher vulnerability, with a 65.9% average success rate, and attacks showed high transferability (50.5% to 62.6%) across different architectures. The study provided quantitative evidence that employing multi-model committees with diverse architectures could reduce attack success rates by 20 to 47 percentage points [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

### 5.3 Agentic Vulnerabilities and MCTS Fuzzing

As LLMs evolved into autonomous agents capable of interacting with complex environments and external tools, the attack surface expanded significantly. The black-box nature of real-world commercial agents, combined with their architectural complexity and the diversity of user tasks they manage, presented unique challenges for systematic security assessment [Wang_2025_LLM]. To address this, researchers proposed AGENTVIGIL, the first generic, automated black-box fuzzing framework explicitly designed to discover and exploit indirect prompt injection vulnerabilities in LLM agents [Wang_2025_LLM].

AGENTVIGIL operates by injecting malicious instructions into the external data sources that a target agent interacts with, aiming to fool the agent into executing the attacker's chosen task instead of the original user task. The fuzzing pipeline begins with a curated initial corpus of adversarial prompt templates that incorporate role-playing, delimiter-based, and obfuscation strategies, complete with placeholders for dynamic adaptation. These seeds enter an iterative fuzzing loop governed by a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) based seed selector. The selector utilizes the Upper Confidence Bound 1 (UCB1) algorithm to optimally balance the exploration of new prompt variants against the exploitation of historically high-scoring seeds [Wang_2025_LLM].

Because the target agent operates as a black box, the framework relies on sparse, binary feedback—specifically, the success or failure of the injection task. To maximize this weak signal, AGENTVIGIL employs an adaptive seed scoring strategy that calculates a final score based on a hybrid metric combining the ASR with attack coverage. A helper LLM (such as GPT-4o-mini) acts as a mutator, applying one of five basic mutation strategies: Shorten, Expand, Rephrase, Crossover, or GenerateSimilar. Empirical evaluations on public benchmarks, including AgentDojo and VWA-adv, demonstrated that AGENTVIGIL achieved high attack success rates of 70% to 71% against agents powered by frontier models like o3-mini, GPT-4o, and Gemini. Furthermore, the framework validated the practical applicability of these attacks in real-world environments by successfully misleading a web agent to navigate to arbitrary and malicious URLs [Wang_2025_LLM].

### 5.4 The Bifurcation of Offensive Research: Efficiency vs. Complexity

As 2025 progressed, the temporal trend in offensive LLM security bifurcated into two distinct but interconnected trajectories: the development of dynamic, ensemble-based attack systems (as seen in AGENTVIGIL and ASA) and the pursuit of extreme computational efficiency [l2_Prompt Injection_v100]. On the efficiency front, a critical turning point was the introduction of knowledge distillation to transfer jailbreaking capabilities from massive, high-capacity LLMs to highly efficient Small Language Models (SLMs) [Slms_2025_Efficient]. 

This asymmetric warfare tactic was formalized through the Adversarial Prompt Distillation (APD) framework. The APD methodology consists of a multi-stage approach to transfer capabilities from a teacher LLM (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B) to a lightweight student SLM (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa). First, the teacher model is pre-trained on a dataset constructed via the Cartesian product of harmful instructions and selected templates, optimized using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss to maximize the likelihood of generating harmful outputs. Next, the teacher's adversarial capabilities are distilled into the SLM by aligning their output distributions using KL divergence loss. To handle vocabulary mismatches, projection layers map logits to a shared dimension, while the last four layers of the student model are fine-tuned using masked language modeling to enhance diversity [Slms_2025_Efficient].

Finally, a Reinforcement Learning Optimization (RLAIF) phase employs a Policy Gradient method to maximize a composite reward function that balances stealthiness, harmfulness, and diversity, incorporating dynamic temperature control to prevent mode collapse. This distillation process allowed attackers to generate adversarial inputs 3.7 times faster than traditional LLM-based attacks while maintaining an exceptionally high ASR of over 96%, significantly reducing the memory demands and generation time required to execute state-of-the-art jailbreaks [Slms_2025_Efficient].

### 5.5 Co-Evolutionary Defenses and Real-Time Mitigation

Consequently, the defensive community was forced to pivot away from computationally heavy, static guardrails toward adaptive, real-time mitigation strategies. One prominent solution introduced in 2025 was AEGIS, an Automated co-Evolutionary framework for Guarding prompt Injections Schema [Liu_2025_Aegis]. AEGIS systematically evolves both attackers and defenders to provide adaptive robustness without requiring manual prompt engineering or model fine-tuning. The framework operates via a GAN-inspired, two-level optimization process. In the outer loop, an Attacker module and a Defender module alternate turns; the attacker generates adversarial prompts to maximize ASR, while the defender generates prompts to maximize True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) [Liu_2025_Aegis].

In the inner loop, AEGIS utilizes TGO+, an enhanced textual gradient optimization module tailored for black-box LLMs. TGO+ simulates gradient-like updates using natural language feedback. It features two key innovations: 'Multi-Route Gradient Optimization', which generates textual gradients based on multiple optimization metrics simultaneously, and a 'Gradient Buffer', which stores past textual gradients to encourage the exploration of novel pathways and prevent cyclical optimization. Evaluated on a real-world automated assignment grading application, AEGIS consistently outperformed existing baselines, improving the TPR by 0.20 compared to previous state-of-the-art methods, with only a marginal 0.02 decrease in TNR. Ablation studies confirmed that co-evolution, gradient replay, and multi-objective optimization are critical for sustaining long-term robustness [Liu_2025_Aegis].

Simultaneously, researchers developed lightweight moderation architectures to ensure LLM alignment without degrading model responses to benign prompts or introducing significant barriers to scalable implementation. The Real-Time Self-Tuning (RTST) framework exemplifies this approach [Zhang_2025_Real]. RTST is a multi-agent moderator framework that relies on a 'Behavior Set'—a human-readable JSON list of criteria describing prompts, each assigned a base score and a tunable weight. When a user submits a prompt, the Main Model and an 'Evaluator' agent are queried in parallel. The Evaluator matches the prompt to K Behaviors and calculates a total score. Depending on whether the score meets a specific threshold, either a 'False Negative Reviewer' or a 'False Positive Reviewer' agent evaluates the analysis. The Reviewer makes the final safety determination and can self-tune the system in real-time by suggesting weight adjustments or adding new adversarial Behaviors to the set, demonstrating significant improvements in ASR and F1 scores against modern jailbreaks compared to traditional fine-tuning or classifier models [Zhang_2025_Real].

### 5.6 System Prompt Strategies and the Think Mode Paradox

Beyond external moderation frameworks, the intrinsic interaction between system prompts and in-context learning emerged as a critical factor in LLM vulnerability. In 2026, researchers conducted the first systematic study of interaction effects between few-shot demonstrations and system prompt strategies for LLM safety [Wang_2026_LLM]. The study evaluated two primary prompt-based defenses: Role-Oriented Prompts (RoP), which frame the LLM as a safe AI assistant, and Task-Oriented Prompts (ToP), which explicitly define safe response generation as the task objective.

The investigation revealed a divergent interaction effect: few-shot demonstrations enhance RoP by up to 4.5% but degrade ToP by up to 21.2%. To explain these phenomena, researchers developed a mathematical framework. For RoP, Bayesian in-context learning models 'role reinforcement', where few-shot examples act as likelihood evidence that strengthens the posterior probability of safe latent concepts. Conversely, for ToP, attention analysis—incorporating softmax attention entropy, the 'lost in the middle' position bias, and the 'attention sink' phenomenon—models 'attention dilution'. Prepending few-shot examples pushes task instructions into middle context positions, thereby diluting the model's attention on the core safety directives [Wang_2026_LLM].

Furthermore, the study identified the 'think mode paradox', revealing that reasoning-enhanced models exhibit consistently higher vulnerability to both jailbreak attacks and negative few-shot interactions. Based on these findings, practical deployment recommendations advise the use of RoP combined with few-shot examples for optimal safety, while strictly avoiding the combination of ToP with few-shot demonstrations [Wang_2026_LLM]. This nuanced understanding of prompt interactions underscores the complexity of inference-time threats, demonstrating that even well-intentioned defensive configurations can inadvertently expand the attack surface if the underlying attention and learning mechanisms are not properly accounted for.


## 6. 6. Model Reliability and Factual Security: LLM Hallucinations

# 6. Model Reliability and Factual Consistency: LLM Hallucinations

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has unlocked unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and complex reasoning. However, the deployment of these models in high-stakes, real-world applications—such as healthcare diagnostics, legal analysis, and financial forecasting—remains fundamentally bottlenecked by the persistent challenge of "hallucinations." In the context of LLMs, hallucinations refer to instances where a model generates information that is syntactically plausible and delivered with high confidence, yet is factually incorrect, nonsensical, or entirely ungrounded in the provided source material. Ensuring model reliability and factual consistency has therefore emerged as a paramount objective within the artificial intelligence research community. The landscape of hallucination research has undergone a significant evolution, transitioning from basic, computationally expensive detection mechanisms to highly sophisticated, mechanistic, and persona-aware frameworks. This section explores the multifaceted domain of LLM hallucinations, tracing the trajectory from foundational detection methodologies and the advent of the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, to state-of-the-art evaluation metrics like ChainPoll. Furthermore, we examine the critical nuances of factual consistency across diverse topics and user personas, and delve into emerging mechanistic frameworks that seek to understand, isolate, and control the internal representations driving these hallucinatory phenomena.

Historically, the detection of hallucinations relied heavily on traditional natural language generation (NLG) metrics, such as ROUGE, BLEU, or BERTScore. While useful for assessing general text similarity, these metrics primarily measure n-gram overlap or surface-level semantic similarity, making them fundamentally ill-equipped to capture the subtle semantic deviations and logical inconsistencies that characterize LLM hallucinations. As language models grew more capable, the research community recognized that the most effective tool for evaluating complex text was often another language model. This realization catalyzed the widespread adoption of the "LLM-as-a-Judge" paradigm. In this approach, a powerful, instruction-tuned LLM is prompted to act as an impartial evaluator, assessing the factuality of a target model's response against a given context or a set of established facts. The LLM-as-a-Judge framework offers a significant leap in evaluative nuance, as it can parse complex reasoning chains and identify subtle contradictions that traditional metrics miss. However, this paradigm introduces its own set of challenges, primarily concerning the scalability of high-quality annotations, the computational cost of querying massive judge models, and the inherent biases or blind spots of the judge itself.

To address the limitations of the standard LLM-as-a-Judge approach, recent advancements have focused on automating and scaling the oversight process through sophisticated training regimens. A notable contribution in this space is the work by Gu et al., who proposed an iterative self-training framework designed to simultaneously scale up hallucination datasets and enhance the accuracy of the hallucination annotator [Gu_2024_Anah]. Recognizing that manual annotation of hallucinations is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, their multi-iteration framework leverages an analytical, sentence-by-sentence annotation strategy. Crucially, this approach is theoretically grounded in the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, allowing the system to iteratively refine its understanding of hallucinatory patterns [Gu_2024_Anah]. During this process, the model alternates between generating annotations for unlabelled data and updating its own parameters based on the most confident predictions. Despite the progressive scaling and the increasing accuracy of the hallucination annotator achieved through this method, the authors acknowledge that a non-negligible margin of error may still persist [Gu_2024_Anah]. This highlights the inherent difficulty of the task and underscores the ongoing need for robust, multi-layered oversight mechanisms in automated hallucination detection.

A significant breakthrough in operationalizing and optimizing the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm is the development of ChainPoll, a novel methodology that substantially improves both the accuracy and efficiency of hallucination detection [Friel_2023_LLM]. Traditional detection metrics often struggle to balance computational overhead with evaluative precision; for instance, methods like SelfCheck-BertScore require sampling multiple full-length responses from the target model, which is highly resource-intensive. ChainPoll elegantly circumvents this inefficiency by employing a meticulously engineered, detailed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompt directed at a judge model, such as GPT-3.5-Turbo [Friel_2023_LLM]. The core mechanism involves asking the judge whether a specific completion contains hallucinations, forcing the model to articulate its reasoning step-by-step before arriving at a conclusion. To mitigate the variance inherent in LLM sampling, ChainPoll repeats this querying process multiple times—typically five—using batch inference for speed, and aggregates the results through a polling mechanism [Friel_2023_LLM]. This combination of detailed CoT and ensemble polling allows ChainPoll to outperform all other existing methods by a massive margin across a diverse range of benchmark tasks, establishing a new state-of-the-art [Friel_2023_LLM]. Furthermore, to ensure these metrics translate effectively to practical applications, Friel and colleagues developed RealHall, a curated suite of four challenging datasets designed to close the gap between academic evaluation and real-world LLM usage, giving researchers confidence that ChainPoll's superior performance will generalize to production environments [Friel_2023_LLM].

Beyond binary hallucination detection, a critical and highly nuanced dimension of model reliability is "factual consistency." Factual consistency refers to the degree to which an LLM maintains a stable, non-contradictory set of objective truths across different interactions, contexts, or when prompted to adopt different user personas. Measuring factual consistency does not necessarily require verifying the absolute truth of the claims against an external database; rather, by comparing the facts presented to different personas, researchers can assess the model's reliability, its truth-seeking tendency, and its vulnerability to sycophancy [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. Recent empirical studies have revealed that an LLM's factual consistency is highly variable and deeply sensitive to the topic of discourse. Banyas et al. conducted an extensive evaluation demonstrating that contemporary and controversial issues often yield significantly less reliable responses [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. For example, their analysis revealed that discussions surrounding the "job market" exhibited the lowest mean factual consistency score (0.07865), which was substantially lower than other topics [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. Interestingly, performance varies considerably across different model providers. In the context of the "vaccines" topic, xAI's models performed exceptionally well, with Grok-3 and Grok-4 achieving the two highest factual consistency scores, outperforming their own average scores across all other topics [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. This variance underscores that factual consistency is not a static attribute but a dynamic property influenced by training data distributions, alignment strategies, and the specific persona the model is instructed to emulate.

While behavioral evaluations like ChainPoll and factual consistency benchmarking provide essential insights into *when* and *how* models hallucinate, they treat the LLM largely as a black box, failing to explain *why* these failures occur at a fundamental, algorithmic level. To bridge this explanatory gap, the research frontier is increasingly shifting towards mechanistic frameworks that investigate the internal representations, attention heads, and activation patterns of LLMs. A prominent example of this mechanistic turn is the exploration of "persona vectors" within a model's high-dimensional activation space. As highlighted in recent literature, researchers at Anthropic have pioneered a framework that identifies specific linear directions in the activation space that correspond directly to distinct behavioral traits, such as sycophancy, malicious behavior, and, crucially, hallucination [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. By isolating these persona vectors, researchers gain the unprecedented ability to monitor the internal state of the model in real-time. This allows for the prediction of hallucinatory outputs before the text is even generated. More importantly, this mechanistic understanding opens the door to "representation engineering" or "activation steering," where the model's internal activations are mathematically nudged away from hallucination-prone directions during inference [Banyas_2025_LLMs]. This approach represents a profound paradigm shift from post-hoc behavioral filtering to white-box interpretability, offering a promising pathway to fundamentally excise hallucinatory behaviors at their source and ensure robust model reliability.


## 7. 8. Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies

**Cross-Cutting Defenses and Mitigation Strategies**

As the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) expand, so too does the sophistication of the adversarial attacks designed to exploit them. The preceding sections have detailed a wide array of vulnerabilities, ranging from prompt injection and jailbreaking to data poisoning and the automated generation of malicious code. In response to this complex threat landscape, the security community is increasingly recognizing that isolated, single-point defenses are insufficient. Attackers continuously adapt to bypass static filters or narrow alignment protocols. Consequently, securing LLMs requires a paradigm shift toward comprehensive, cross-cutting defense mechanisms that operate at multiple stages of the model lifecycle—from training and alignment to inference and post-generation monitoring. This section explores the forefront of LLM security, focusing on holistic defense architectures, dynamic ensemble systems, the emerging concept of self-policing models, robust alignment techniques, and the necessity of co-evolutionary security strategies to keep pace with the adversarial arms race.

**Holistic Defenses**

The concept of holistic defense, or defense-in-depth, is a foundational principle in traditional cybersecurity that is now being urgently adapted for LLM ecosystems. A holistic defense strategy assumes that no single security measure is infallible; therefore, multiple layers of overlapping safeguards must be deployed to protect the model and its users. In the context of LLMs, this involves integrating input sanitization, robust model architecture, continuous monitoring, and output verification into a unified security pipeline.

Recent research underscores the critical necessity of implementing such robust security safeguards, particularly as custom and specialized LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes environments like healthcare automation [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. In these critical sectors, the failure of an LLM due to an adversarial attack could have severe real-world consequences. To mitigate these risks, defenders are exploring multi-faceted approaches. For instance, [Yin_2025_LLM] proposes CodeGPTSensor+, a system designed to address the real-world scenario where LLM-generated code may undergo minor, potentially malicious modifications. By employing adversarial training, CodeGPTSensor+ significantly improves the model's robustness against input perturbations, demonstrating the value of integrating adversarial sample generation directly into the training pipeline to harden the model against anticipated attack vectors.

Furthermore, holistic defenses must account for interactive, multi-turn threat models. [Wang_2024_Advqdet] highlights the challenge of query-based black-box attacks, where an adversary iteratively refines adversarial examples based on the model's responses. Defending against such persistent threats requires dynamic detection mechanisms that monitor query patterns over time, rather than evaluating each input in isolation. A truly holistic defense architecture seamlessly connects these detection mechanisms with the model's internal safety guardrails and external output filters, ensuring that if an adversarial prompt bypasses the initial input sanitization, the model's robust alignment or the post-generation verification will intercept the malicious payload before it reaches the user.

**Dynamic Ensemble Systems**

While static defenses can often be reverse-engineered or bypassed through iterative probing, dynamic systems introduce a level of unpredictability and adaptability that significantly complicates the attacker's task. In LLM security, dynamic ensemble systems are emerging as a powerful mechanism to enhance robustness. The core principle of an ensemble system is to aggregate the outputs or analyses of multiple diverse models or tools to reach a more reliable and secure consensus.

Interestingly, the efficacy of ensemble methods has been well-documented in offensive research. For example, [Jia_2025_Adversarial] demonstrates that ensemble-based adversarial examples exhibit vastly superior transferability compared to those generated against a single model. Their proposed FOA-Attack utilizes a dynamic ensemble weighting strategy that adaptively balances multiple image encoders based on their convergence rates, substantially boosting the success rate of the attack across different architectures. However, this same principle can be inverted to create formidable defenses. If attackers use dynamic ensembles to find universal vulnerabilities, defenders can deploy dynamic ensembles to cross-verify outputs and detect anomalies that a single model might miss.

In the realm of secure code generation, the integration of dynamic ensemble systems is particularly promising. [Liu_2025_Purpcode] notes that while their alignment approach achieves state-of-the-art defense capabilities, incorporating multi-turn interactions and dynamic analysis could further improve performance. Specifically, they suggest integrating dynamic analysis tools or ensemble static analyzers as oracles for code security. By dynamically routing generated code through an ensemble of diverse security analyzers—each specializing in different vulnerability classes (e.g., buffer overflows, injection flaws, logic errors)—the overall system can achieve a much higher detection rate for malicious or insecure outputs than any single analyzer could provide. Furthermore, by dynamically adjusting the weights of these analyzers based on the context of the prompt or the historical reliability of the tool, the defense system can adapt to new attack patterns in real-time.

**Self-Policing LLMs**

One of the most innovative frontiers in LLM security is the development of "self-policing" models—LLMs that are explicitly trained to monitor, evaluate, and correct their own reasoning processes and outputs for security violations. Rather than relying entirely on external filters or rigid, hardcoded rules, self-policing models internalize security principles, allowing them to generalize these concepts to novel, unseen attack vectors.

A prime example of this approach is PurpCode, introduced by [Liu_2025_Purpcode]. PurpCode represents a pioneering post-training recipe designed to train safe code reasoning models. The methodology involves a two-stage process that fundamentally alters how the model approaches security. First, through "Rule Learning," the model is explicitly taught to reference established cybersafety rules and guidelines. This enables the model to "think over" known safety protocols before generating code, effectively embedding a self-policing mechanism within its reasoning chain. Second, PurpCode employs an internal red-teaming pipeline to curate prompts that expose cyber-unsafe model behaviors. By continuously challenging the model with these internally generated red-teaming prompts during training, the model learns to identify and reject malicious requests, even when they are heavily obfuscated or framed as benign tasks.

Similarly, [Thang_2026_LLM] introduces GoodVibe, a fine-grained security optimization pipeline that takes a more mechanistic approach to self-policing. GoodVibe operates on the premise that security improvements should reflect a shift in how code LLMs internalize and apply security considerations during generation, rather than treating security as an external constraint. The pipeline identifies security-critical internal components—specific neurons whose representations strongly influence the model's security-related decisions—via gradient-based attribution. By selectively adapting only this critical subspace, GoodVibe strengthens secure coding behaviors intrinsically. This means the model naturally gravitates toward secure practices without the need for extensive prompt engineering or external post-processing, effectively policing its own internal state to ensure safe outputs.

**Robust Alignment**

Alignment—the process of ensuring an AI system's behavior matches human values and intentions—is central to LLM security. However, standard alignment techniques, such as simplistic Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), often fall short when subjected to adversarial pressure. Attackers frequently utilize jailbreaks and prompt injection to bypass these superficial alignment layers. Therefore, the focus has shifted toward robust alignment: training methodologies that instill deep, generalizable safety principles that withstand sophisticated exploitation attempts.

As noted by [Liu_2025_Purpcode], existing SFT approaches largely depend on expensive, high-quality response collections and often fail to generalize to complex, multi-step malicious requests. Furthermore, standard RLHF can sometimes degrade the model's core utility, leading to an overly cautious model that refuses benign requests (false positives). To achieve robust alignment, [Liu_2025_Purpcode] emphasizes the need for an alignment approach that enables generalizable cybersafety reasoning without hurting utility. Their method demonstrates that by teaching the model the underlying reasoning behind safety rules, rather than just the desired output, the model becomes significantly more resilient to template-based attacks (like those using H4RM3L or ArtPrompt) and search-based optimization attacks (like AutoDAN or GCG).

Robust alignment also requires addressing vulnerabilities across different modalities. In Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), alignment must encompass both text and visual inputs. [Jia_2025_Adversarial] highlights that existing methods often achieve targeted attacks by aligning global features (such as CLIP's [CLS] token) while overlooking rich local information encoded in patch tokens. This suboptimal alignment in the model's feature space creates vulnerabilities that attackers can exploit to generate highly transferable adversarial examples. Robust alignment in MLLMs must therefore ensure that both global semantic meaning and local feature representations are tightly coupled to safety constraints, preventing attackers from smuggling malicious payloads through unaligned visual channels.

**Co-evolutionary Security**

The landscape of LLM security is not static; it is defined by a relentless, co-evolutionary arms race between attackers and defenders. As defenders implement more sophisticated alignment and holistic security pipelines, attackers leverage the very same AI advancements to automate and scale their exploits. This dynamic necessitates a co-evolutionary approach to security, where defensive strategies are continuously updated in response to, and in anticipation of, next-generation AI-driven threats.

The offensive use of LLMs is accelerating this arms race. For instance, [Akil_2025_Llmalmorph] introduces LLMalMorph, a semi-automated framework that leverages the semantical and syntactical code comprehension capabilities of LLMs to modify malware source code and generate evasive variants. By automating the mutation of malware, attackers can rapidly bypass traditional signature-based detection systems. Similarly, [Ding_2025_Reward] presents RAWG, a reward-driven automated WebShell generator. RAWG addresses the limitations of static, prompt-engineered red-teaming approaches by using a reward-driven mechanism to generate highly diverse, obfuscated WebShells that cover multiple attack families. These automated generation tools highlight that attackers are no longer relying solely on manual exploit crafting; they are using AI to explore the vulnerability space at an unprecedented scale.

To survive this co-evolutionary arms race, defenders must adopt continuous, automated red-teaming and dynamic updating mechanisms. The macro-theme of this research intersection captures the adversarial arms race between sophisticated jailbreak attack frameworks and advanced alignment defenses, dictating that security cannot be a one-time post-training step. Instead, defenses must co-evolve. This involves using LLMs defensively to automatically generate novel attack vectors (internal red-teaming, as seen in PurpCode [Liu_2025_Purpcode]) and using those vectors to continuously retrain and harden the model. By simulating the attacker's capabilities within the defense pipeline, security researchers can ensure that LLMs remain resilient not just against the threats of today, but against the automated, AI-generated exploits of tomorrow.


## 8. 7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation

## 7. Testing Methodologies and Evaluation

As the threat landscape surrounding Large Language Models (LLMs) rapidly expands, the methodologies used to evaluate model vulnerabilities and defensive mechanisms have undergone significant evolution. Early evaluation paradigms relied heavily on static datasets and heuristic string-matching techniques. However, contemporary research has demonstrated that such approaches are insufficient for capturing the nuanced, context-dependent nature of modern adversarial attacks. Consequently, the community has shifted toward dynamic, agentic evaluation environments and sophisticated LLM-as-a-judge frameworks. This section synthesizes the evaluation frameworks, benchmarks, datasets, and metrics utilized across the four primary threat categories: prompt injection and jailbreaks, data and model poisoning, LLM hallucinations, and adversarial attacks. Furthermore, it critically analyzes the trade-offs between computational cost metrics and detection efficacy in modern red-teaming pipelines.

### 7.2 Prompt Injection and Jailbreak Evaluation

The evaluation of prompt injection and jailbreak attacks fundamentally relies on quantifying the model's compliance with malicious instructions. The primary metric for this assessment is the Attack Success Rate (ASR). Historically, benchmarks such as AdvBench evaluated ASR using human judgment or simple string-matching heuristics [Slms_2025_Efficient]. This early approach, formalized as Keyword-based Attack Success Rate (ASR_k), determines attack success by checking for the absence of predefined refusal keywords (e.g., "Sorry, I cannot assist" or "This is illegal") in the model's response [Slms_2025_Efficient]. While ASR_k provides a straightforward and computationally efficient method to quantify vulnerability, it is prone to inaccuracies, as models may avoid explicit refusal keywords while still failing to execute the harmful instruction.

To address these limitations, the community has increasingly adopted Harmfulness-based Attack Success Rate (ASR_l), which leverages advanced models like GPT-4o as automated judges to evaluate the actual harmfulness of the generated response [Slms_2025_Efficient]. This LLM-as-a-judge paradigm offers a more nuanced assessment of model behavior under attack. In standardized frameworks like HarmBench, evaluation is further formalized using metrics such as Safe Rate (SR), Refusal Rate (RR), and ASR, where ASR is mathematically defined as 1 minus the Safe Rate [Wang_2026_LLM]. These evaluations often employ specialized judge models, such as Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B, to classify response safety [Wang_2026_LLM].

The shift from static evaluation to dynamic red teaming has introduced sophisticated automated frameworks. Multi-round automatic red teaming (MART) enables iterative safety improvements, while Holistic Automated Red Teaming (HARM) utilizes top-down test case generation and multi-turn interactions [Wang_2026_LLM]. For black-box agentic systems, frameworks like AGENTVIGIL employ Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithms for adaptive seed scoring and selection, demonstrating high ASRs and strong transferability across unseen tasks [Wang_2025_LLM]. Similarly, the RTST framework utilizes a multi-agent system to improve ASR and F1 performance when identifying jailbreak prompts, highlighting the viability of adaptive, real-time optimization in defensive testing [Zhang_2025_Real]. Furthermore, multimodal systems require specialized evaluation, as visual adversarial examples, typographic attacks (e.g., FigStep), and compositional attacks (e.g., Jailbreak in Pieces) can exponentially accelerate jailbreak success rates [Wang_2026_LLM].

### 7.3 Evaluating Data and Model Poisoning

Evaluating generative backdoors and data poisoning attacks presents unique challenges, as the malicious behavior must remain dormant until triggered, without degrading the model's general utility. The establishment of standardized benchmarks is critical, as existing frameworks often prove insufficient for practical deployment in real-world enterprise applications, necessitating scalable, near-real-time evaluation infrastructure [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking].

To systematically evaluate these threats, the BackdoorLLM benchmark was introduced as a unified repository supporting four diverse attack modalities: Data Poisoning Attacks (DPA), Weight Poisoning Attacks (WPA), Chain-of-Thought Attacks (CoTA), and Hidden State Attacks (HSA) [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. The evaluation methodology in BackdoorLLM formulates the backdoor objective as a delicate trade-off between two metrics: clean loss and backdoor loss [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Clean loss measures the model's ability to maintain normal, benign behavior on standard inputs, while backdoor loss quantifies the model's propensity to generate the adversary's desired response when the specific trigger is present [Li_2024_Backdoorllm]. Testing methodologies for poisoning typically utilize diverse datasets, including Stanford Alpaca, AdvBench, ToxiGen, and GSM8K, to ensure that the evaluation spans multiple reasoning tasks and model architectures [Li_2024_Backdoorllm].

### 7.4 Assessing LLM Hallucinations and Factual Consistency

The evaluation of LLM hallucinations requires distinguishing between semantic fluency and factual accuracy. A critical challenge in this domain is the "factuality gap," where high semantic similarity scores mask severe hallucination rates [Kabongo_2025_Factual]. To rigorously assess factual consistency, researchers have developed prompting-based pipelines that extract atomic explanatory statements from source texts (e.g., Amazon Reviews) to construct factual ground-truth explanations [Kabongo_2025_Factual].

Evaluation metrics for hallucinations are broadly categorized into LLM-based and Natural Language Inference (NLI)-based approaches. LLM-based metrics, such as St2Exp-P, St2Exp-R, and St2Exp-F1, utilize models like Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to score the precision and recall of generated statements against the ground truth [Kabongo_2025_Factual]. Conversely, NLI-based metrics employ models like DeBERTa-large-mnli to compute entailment and coherence scores (e.g., StEnt-P) between statement pairs [Kabongo_2025_Factual]. 

Beyond reference-based metrics, zero-shot hallucination detection relies on measuring the internal consistency of generated content. Frameworks such as SelfCheckGPT stochastically generate multiple responses and verify if they support the original answer, while SAC3 analyzes consistency across different LLMs or rephrased queries [Liu_2025_Attention]. Other approaches, such as INSIDE, calculate answer inconsistency within the sentence embedding space, and InterrogateLLM detects hallucinations by asking reverse questions to verify if the original prompt can be reconstructed [Liu_2025_Attention]. For complex reasoning tasks, evaluation has moved toward step-level supervision; for instance, Process Reward Models utilize Monte Carlo Net Information Gain to provide fine-grained labels that distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning steps in Chain-of-Thought traces [Royer_2026_Process].

### 7.5 Adversarial Attacks and Detection Frameworks

The evaluation of adversarial attacks has evolved to address the brittleness of static, prompt-engineered LLM code generators. Empirical analyses reveal that static generators rapidly collapse onto narrow mode families, producing repetitive payloads that contain fewer than 12% novel tokens across repeated runs [Ding_2025_Reward]. This lack of diversity leaves crucial blind spots in safety audits. Consequently, evaluation frameworks are shifting toward reinforcement-learning and GFlowNet-based adversary fine-tuning, which systematically explore broader attack spaces to yield diverse, transferable, and heavily obfuscated jailbreak prompts [Ding_2025_Reward].

When evaluating detection mechanisms against these advanced attacks, researchers must test against a wide array of threat vectors. For example, robust detection frameworks are evaluated against multiple state-of-the-art query-based attacks and adaptive attacks to ensure comprehensive defensive coverage [Wang_2024_Advqdet]. In these evaluations, standard classification metrics such as F1 score, precision, and recall are utilized alongside rigorous statistical validation, such as bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over held-out evaluation sets [Corll_2026_Mirror].

### 7.6 The LLM-as-a-Judge Paradigm and its Vulnerabilities

While the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm has become the gold standard for evaluating text quality, safety, and factual consistency, the judge models themselves introduce new security vulnerabilities. LLM-as-a-judge systems are highly susceptible to prompt injection attacks designed to manipulate their assessments [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Adversarial attacks against these judges are generally categorized into content-author attacks and system-prompt attacks [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

Experimental evaluations across frontier models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus) and open-weights models (e.g., Gemma-3, Llama-3.2) reveal that sophisticated attacks can achieve success rates of up to 73.8% against popular LLM judges [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Techniques such as Contextual Misdirection have proven particularly effective, achieving a 67.7% success rate against Gemma models [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. Furthermore, smaller judge models (e.g., Gemma-3-4B-Instruct) exhibit higher vulnerability compared to their larger counterparts, and adversarial prompts demonstrate high transferability (50.5% to 62.6%) across different model architectures [Maloyan_2025_LLM]. To mitigate these vulnerabilities, evaluation frameworks are increasingly adopting multi-model committees with diverse architectures and prioritizing comparative assessment over absolute scoring methods [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

### 7.7 Dynamic Benchmarking and Computational Trade-offs

The transition from static benchmarks to dynamic, agentic evaluation environments necessitates a careful balancing of detection efficacy and computational overhead. Public prompt injection datasets are frequently noisy, often conflating general content safety with specific prompt injection vectors, and frequently suffer from poor provenance [Corll_2026_Mirror]. This noise complicates the reliable evaluation of defensive models. However, research demonstrates that small, static, carefully curated linear models (e.g., linear SVMs using character n-grams) can achieve exceptional performance (e.g., 0.9207 F1 score) when the data geometry is strictly controlled and cryptographic provenance is maintained [Corll_2026_Mirror].

In modern red-teaming pipelines, the computational cost of evaluation is a critical constraint. High-compute intensity metrics and sequential evaluation procedures often fail to meet the efficiency requirements for large-scale or real-time deployment [Friel_2023_LLM]. For instance, advanced judge models like G-Eval-4 can be up to 20 times more expensive than standard GPT-4 evaluations, rendering them impractical for continuous automated red teaming [Friel_2023_LLM]. Furthermore, baseline metrics must be carefully calibrated; in binary classification tasks for hallucination or safety detection, an AUROC score of 0.5 corresponds merely to random guessing, establishing a strict floor for baseline performance [Friel_2023_LLM].

To address these computational bottlenecks, defensive frameworks are increasingly evaluated not only on their defense success rate but also on their efficiency and utility preservation. Frameworks like SecurityLingua, which employ prompt compression to detect malicious instructions, are explicitly evaluated on their ability to lower latency and reduce token costs compared to available baselines, while simultaneously maintaining the utility of the original model on downstream tasks [Li_2025_LLM]. This dual focus ensures that security evaluations reflect the practical constraints of deploying LLMs in production environments, where computational overhead must be minimized without compromising the rigor of adversarial testing.

### 7.8 Conclusion

The evaluation of LLM security has matured from rudimentary keyword-matching heuristics to sophisticated, dynamic frameworks capable of assessing complex, multi-turn adversarial interactions. While the adoption of LLM-as-a-judge metrics and automated red-teaming agents has significantly improved the fidelity of security assessments, it has also introduced new vulnerabilities and computational challenges. Future testing methodologies must continue to standardize cross-category benchmarks, enforce strict data provenance, and optimize the computational efficiency of evaluation pipelines to ensure the scalable and robust deployment of secure LLM systems.


## 9. 9. Discussion and Open Challenges

### 9. Discussion and Open Challenges

The landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) security has undergone rapid evolution between 2020 and 2026 [Wang_2026_LLM]. The field has progressed from a foundational era focused on establishing basic safety alignment through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), into a period of rapid development characterized by a proliferation of novel benchmarks, sophisticated attacks, and reactive defenses [Wang_2026_LLM]. Despite these advancements, the current security paradigm remains fundamentally challenged by the dynamic nature of adversarial threats. This section synthesizes the critical gaps in the existing literature, examines the inherent tensions between model capabilities and safety, and outlines imperative directions for future research.

#### 9.1 The Utility-Security Trade-off

A pervasive and unresolved challenge in LLM security is the fundamental tension between model utility and robustness. Many existing defense mechanisms, such as adversarial training, prompt rephrasing, and strict guardrails, frequently degrade the model's performance on benign prompts or introduce substantial computational overhead and online latency [Li_2025_LLM, Zhang_2025_Real]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that standard safety alignment is often superficial; it primarily affects only the initial output tokens and can be easily compromised even through benign fine-tuning [Wang_2026_LLM]. Adversaries actively exploit this fragility. For instance, the BadEdit framework formulates backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem, directly altering a subset of LLM parameters to achieve a 100% attack success rate (ASR) using as few as 15 poisoning samples [Li_2024_Badedit]. Crucially, this method maintains the model's overarching performance on benign inputs and remains robust even after subsequent instruction-tuning, complicating detection efforts [Li_2024_Badedit]. 

To mitigate these utility trade-offs, researchers have proposed lightweight alternatives. Approaches such as SecurityLingua utilize security-oriented prompt compression to discern malicious intent without altering the original user prompt, thereby maintaining utility and minimizing token costs [Li_2025_LLM]. Similarly, real-time self-tuning (RTST) moderator frameworks offer adaptive jailbreak defense with a minimal training footprint compared to traditional fine-tuning [Zhang_2025_Real]. However, achieving a universal balance between robust security and uncompromised model utility remains an open problem.

#### 9.2 Multi-Agent Vulnerabilities and Adaptive Attacks

The increasing deployment of LLM agents in high-stakes domains—including finance, healthcare, laboratory research, and autonomous driving—has exponentially expanded the attack surface [Zhan_2025_LLM]. These systems are particularly susceptible to Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) attacks and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) poisoning, where malicious instructions are embedded within external tools or knowledge bases rather than direct user inputs [Zhan_2025_LLM]. Automated black-box fuzzing frameworks, such as AGENTVIGIL, have demonstrated alarming efficacy in this domain. By employing Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) for adaptive seed selection, AGENTVIGIL achieves approximately 70% ASR against state-of-the-art agents based on models like GPT-4o and o3-mini, successfully misleading agents to navigate to malicious URLs [Wang_2025_LLM]. 

A critical limitation of current agent defenses is their vulnerability to adaptive, co-evolutionary attacks. Empirical evaluations have shown that adaptive adversaries can successfully bypass multiple established defenses against IPI attacks, consistently achieving an ASR of over 50% across eight different defense mechanisms [Zhan_2025_LLM]. This underscores the inadequacy of static defense mechanisms and highlights the necessity for co-evolutionary adversarial frameworks that systematically evolve both attackers and defenders, utilizing techniques like multi-route textual gradients and gradient replay to maintain long-term robustness [Liu_2025_Aegis].

#### 9.3 Theoretical Guarantees and Scaling Challenges

The current LLM security landscape is predominantly empirical, lacking rigorous theoretical guarantees for certified robustness [Wang_2026_LLM]. Existing evaluation mechanisms are often inadequate for detecting sophisticated anomalies, leaving models vulnerable to evasion tactics and data exposure [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Furthermore, scaling robust training methods to trillion-parameter models presents formidable difficulties. Full-parameter fine-tuning is computationally prohibitive and prone to catastrophic forgetting, while parameter-efficient adaptations often operate at a coarse granularity with limited interpretability [Thang_2026_LLM]. Observations indicate that the weights of models compromised via fine-tuning attacks exhibit larger norms, suggesting a potential metric for detecting structural vulnerabilities, yet a comprehensive theoretical framework remains elusive [Yang_2024_Adversarial]. Additionally, the reliance on LLM-as-a-judge systems for automated safety evaluation introduces recursive vulnerabilities, as these evaluator models themselves remain highly susceptible to sophisticated adversarial attacks [Maloyan_2025_LLM].

#### 9.4 Future Directions

Addressing these open challenges requires a paradigm shift in how LLM security is conceptualized and implemented. Several promising research directions emerge from the current literature:

**Mechanistically Interpretable Security:** Future defenses must move beyond black-box approaches toward mechanistically interpretable interventions. Recent work has demonstrated that security-relevant reasoning is often localized to a small subset of "security neurons" [Thang_2026_LLM]. Frameworks like GoodVibe leverage gradient-based attribution to identify and selectively fine-tune this security-critical subspace. By utilizing activation-driven neuron clustering, such approaches offer a scalable method to secure code generation, achieving significant improvements over base models while reducing training computation by more than 3.6× compared to parameter-efficient baselines [Thang_2026_LLM].

**Cross-Modal Defense Generalization:** The integration of multimodal capabilities introduces formidable challenges, as visual adversarial examples, typographic attacks, and compositional adversarial inputs can rapidly jailbreak aligned LLMs [Wang_2026_LLM, Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Notably, single-image attacks have been shown to compromise multimodal agents exponentially fast [Wang_2026_LLM]. Future research must prioritize cross-modal defense generalization, developing robust input sanitization and custom defense prompts that operate seamlessly across text, image, and audio modalities [Chowdhury_2024_Breaking].

**Efficient Red Teaming and Distillation:** To keep pace with evolving threats, the security community must develop more efficient red teaming methodologies. Distilling the jailbreaking prowess of massive LLMs into smaller language models (SLMs) via adversarial prompt distillation—integrating masked language modeling and reinforcement learning—offers a pathway to conduct efficient, robust vulnerability assessments with significantly reduced resource overhead [Slms_2025_Efficient].

**Standardized Evaluation and Regulatory Compliance:** Finally, the deployment of LLMs in critical sectors necessitates the establishment of standardized regulatory compliance testing and real-time monitoring systems [Yang_2024_Adversarial, Chowdhury_2024_Breaking]. Enhancing the robustness of automated evaluation will require combining multiple defense approaches, particularly the use of diverse multi-model committees and comparative assessments, to mitigate the vulnerabilities inherent in single-model judges [Maloyan_2025_LLM].


## 10. 10. Conclusion

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical infrastructure, commercial applications, and daily life has precipitated a paradigm shift in both artificial intelligence capabilities and the corresponding adversarial threat landscape. This survey has systematically examined the multifaceted security vulnerabilities inherent in modern LLMs, spanning the entirety of the model lifecycle. Our analysis reveals that the attack surface is vast and continuously expanding. During the training phase, models are highly susceptible to data and model poisoning, exacerbated by the reliance on massive, uncurated datasets. At inference time, the landscape is dominated by sophisticated adversarial attacks, prompt injection, and jailbreaking techniques that subvert safety alignments. Furthermore, intrinsic reliability issues—most notably hallucinations and factual inconsistencies—continue to undermine the trustworthiness of model outputs, posing severe risks independent of malicious intent.

A critical pattern emerging from recent literature is the definitive transition from static, heuristic-based exploits to dynamic, optimization-driven adversarial attacks. The period between 2024 and 2026 marked an accelerated adversarial arms race, characterized by the automation of jailbreak generation and the expansion of vulnerabilities from unimodal text to complex, multimodal architectures [Wang_2026_LLM]. Attackers increasingly leverage the models' own reasoning capabilities and cross-modal interactions to bypass safety guardrails. This evolution demonstrates that traditional safety alignment techniques, such as standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), are fundamentally insufficient against adaptive adversaries. Furthermore, evaluation methodologies have been forced to evolve; the community has recognized that static datasets and heuristic string-matching are inadequate for assessing the robustness of non-deterministic, generative systems, necessitating a shift toward automated, LLM-assisted red-teaming and dynamic benchmarking.

The practical implications of these vulnerabilities for LLM deployment are profound. As these models are increasingly embedded in sensitive and high-stakes sectors—ranging from healthcare diagnostics and legal analysis to chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) research—the consequences of a security breach or factual hallucination escalate from benign errors to critical safety failures. Consequently, the deployment of LLMs can no longer rely on perimeter-based security or post-hoc patching. Organizations must adopt a "secure-by-design" philosophy, acknowledging that models will inevitably face adversarial and out-of-distribution inputs in the wild. This necessitates a paradigm shift in risk assessment, where models are continuously monitored, contextually bounded, and evaluated within the specific operational parameters of their deployment environments. 

Despite significant advancements in cross-cutting defenses, several critical open challenges remain, charting the course for future research. First, the fundamental tension between model helpfulness and harmlessness remains unresolved; overly stringent safety alignments often degrade the model's utility and reasoning capabilities, while permissive alignments invite exploitation. Future research must explore novel alignment techniques that decouple these objectives, potentially through modular safety architectures, constitutional AI frameworks, or representation engineering. Second, the mitigation of hallucinations requires deeper architectural interventions rather than superficial prompt engineering. This necessitates advanced research into retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) optimization, epistemic uncertainty quantification, and intrinsic self-correction mechanisms [Liu_2025_Attention]. Finally, the field currently lacks robust theoretical guarantees for LLM security. Developing formal verification methods and provable robustness guarantees for large-scale, non-deterministic, and multimodal models represents a formidable but essential frontier.

To navigate this complex landscape, we offer several actionable recommendations for both practitioners and researchers. For practitioners, deploying LLMs must involve a comprehensive defense-in-depth strategy. This includes implementing robust input sanitization, utilizing secondary LLMs as semantic firewalls to filter malicious prompts and hallucinated outputs, and establishing continuous, automated red-teaming pipelines that evolve alongside the model. Practitioners should also strictly enforce principle-of-least-privilege access when integrating LLMs with external APIs and databases to mitigate the blast radius of successful prompt injections. 

For the research community, priority should be given to developing standardized, multimodal vulnerability benchmarks that accurately reflect real-world, multi-turn threat vectors. Researchers should also focus on the mechanistic interpretability of LLM representations to better understand the underlying basis of vulnerabilities, thereby enabling the design of inherently resilient architectures rather than relying on reactive patches. Furthermore, fostering open-source frameworks for evaluating defensive mechanisms will accelerate the development of robust countermeasures.

Ultimately, securing Large Language Models is not a static destination but a continuous, adversarial process. As foundation models grow in scale, autonomy, and multimodal capability, the collaborative efforts of the cybersecurity, machine learning, and policy communities will be paramount. Only through rigorous empirical evaluation, transparent reporting of vulnerabilities, and the proactive development of adaptive, cross-cutting defenses can we harness the transformative potential of LLMs while safeguarding against their inherent and evolving risks.
